# Rank the Goals First: A Railway Memorial and the Order of Argument

*The censors asked to halt the Beijing-Tongzhou railway. Zhang Zhidong's memorial ranked the benefits of building railways, set them out as numbered items, and assigned the northern and southern sections of a north-south line to governors-general and a governor. After "approved by rescript," the biography goes on to record his transfer to Huguang and his petition to open an ironworks to supply the railway; this biography does not record how the line itself was built.*

## To Halt, or to Build

Zhang Zhidong spent much of his career as a senior provincial official; the biography says he held provincial posts for several decades. Before this memorial, he had already set up an academy, an arsenal, and a mining bureau in Guangdong. His railway memorial was written in the middle of a dispute: at court there were several positions on whether to build railways and where. His biography in the Draft History of the Qing records it this way (here and throughout, quotations from the Chinese are our own translations): "When the Naval Office petitioned to build the Beijing-Tongzhou railway, the censors pressed the harms of railways and asked that the project be halted." The Naval Office, which, as its name indicates, was the office in charge of naval affairs, stood for building the Beijing-Tongzhou line. The censors, the court's officials charged with remonstrance and impeachment, pressed the harms and asked for a stop. The biography then records two further proposals. Weng Tonghe and others asked to try building in the frontier regions, for ease of moving troops. Xu Huifeng asked to build a Dezhou-Jining line instead, for the benefit of grain transport. Only after these positions does the biography record the memorial Zhang Zhidong submitted on the matter. Which harms the censors meant, and what reasons the Naval Office gave, the biography does not record. [QSG4371-E3][QSG4371-E18][QSG4371-E21][QSG4371-E1]

Zhang Zhidong answered these proposals with a memorial. The memorial proposes a separate trunk line, running from Lugou Bridge outside the capital through Henan to Hankou in Hubei, divided by the river into northern and southern sections; it is not the Beijing-Tongzhou line. The memorial sets out an explicit ranking of goals: "In building a railway, the greatest benefit lies in moving local goods and enriching the people's livelihood; raising troops and forwarding supplies come second." The relative weight of the goals is laid plainly on the table. In the author's view, that ranking is the easiest thing to skim past in this memorial, and the place where the real craft sits. [QSG4371-E5][QSG4371-E18][QSG4371-E7][QSG4371-E8][QSG4371-E3][QSG4371-E22]

## What He Had Been Up To

Before this, Zhang had set up an academy, an arsenal, and a mining bureau in Guangdong. The biography gives those ventures a cause: "Ashamed of suing for peace, Zhidong quietly set about making himself strong," and then he founded the Guangdong Naval and Military Academy, created an arsenal, and opened a mining bureau. "Ashamed of suing for peace" is the motive the compilers of the Draft History assigned, not a conjecture of this article. [QSG4371-E1]

When it came to the railway, his ranking of goals was explicit: moving local goods and enriching livelihoods first, raising troops and forwarding supplies second. In one and the same memorial, shipping produce and improving the ordinary livelihood sit up front; military use comes after. [QSG4371-E5]

One reading is that the ranking is deliberate. The censors were arguing "the harms of railways," and Weng Tonghe and others had already asked to try building in the frontier regions for ease of moving troops; had he opened on military matters, he would have been contesting the same ground as that proposal, and the fight might have grown hotter (the author's conjecture; the biography does not say so). Placing livelihoods first steers the debate onto ground where points can be settled one by one - do local goods flow, do livelihoods thicken. The author infers this is tactics in the ordering; the biography records no reason of his own for the sequence. [QSG4371-E5][QSG4371-E3][QSG4371-E18]

## Turning the Arguments into a List

The memorial works through a numbered list of benefits, and the biography preserves the numbering: benefit one through benefit seven, each a sentence or two. The last reads: "Should war come by sea, grain transport will not be obstructed - benefit seven." The memorial then proposes: "With these seven benefits, let it be completed section by section, year by year." These are qualitative reasons the memorial itself lists; the recorded text gives no quantities. [QSG4371-E9][QSG4371-E10][QSG4371-E11][QSG4371-E19]

The first two are plain: "Situated in the interior, there is no fear of drawing in the enemy - benefit one; the open plains are broad, and graves and houses can easily be avoided - benefit two." Benefit one says the line lies in the interior and will not draw in an enemy; benefit two says the country is open, so graves and dwellings can easily be routed around. One reading is that they address worries about security and about moved graves and demolished homes, but the biography does not record which harms the censors meant. In the author's view, worth remembering is precisely that half-sentence: under the pen of a man the verdict calls "grand in scale," the most concrete landing point is whether the graves and houses along the route can be spared. [QSG4371-E9][QSG4371-E17]

Benefits three and four turn to livelihoods and revenue: "As works flourish and stations multiply, laborers and traders can leave the old for the new - benefit three; with one route commanding the crossroads of eight or nine provinces, people and goods converge, and the sources of military pay are made ample - benefit four." Hired hands and traveling merchants can find new lines of work; one line holds the crossroads of eight or nine provinces, people and goods crowd in, and revenue has somewhere to come from. In the author's view, benefit three echoes "enriching the people's livelihood"; benefit four lands on the sources of military pay. [QSG4371-E10][QSG4371-E5]

Benefit five concerns gathering troops, benefit six coal and iron mining: "Should trouble arise near the capital, the picked troops of Huai and Chu can be gathered within a single morning - benefit five; Taiyuan is rich in coal and iron, and where transport is easy, mining will certainly increase - benefit six." If trouble arises in the region around the capital, the picked troops of Huai and Chu can assemble within a morning; Taiyuan is rich in coal and iron, and easier transport means more mining. [QSG4371-E11]

Then comes the picture of connections. Of the northern stretch: "When the northern line is complete, the wheel-ruts of the Three Jin will meet at Jingxing, and the teams of Guan and Long will cross at Luokou" - Shanxi's cart-tracks connect at Jingxing, and the horse-teams of Guan and Long meet at Luokou. Of the southern stretch: "South of the river it will draw in Huai and Wu to the east and reach Xiang and Shu in the south; news from ten thousand li away can be carried on schedule." He does not stop at one line; he draws the line in his memorial as two stretches, north and south of the river, each hooking into a larger network. [QSG4371-E7][QSG4371-E8]

Then the division of labor: "Let the northern section be the charge of the Governor-General of Zhili, and the southern section the charge of the Governor-General of Huguang, assisted by the Governor of Henan." Who carries which stretch is written out plainly. In the author's view, this leaves no room for a section that no one is specifically responsible for. [QSG4371-E13]

The outcome: "The memorial received an approving rescript, and there followed the order transferring him to Huguang." The throne approved the request, and an order sending him to Huguang followed. Be clear: "approved by rescript" means the memorial was accepted, not that work began. Zhang's own biography records no schedule, outlay, or relocations for the line he proposed. It goes on to record his petition for an ironworks to supply the railway. Later it records that in the thirty-fourth year he was put in charge of the Canton-Hankou railway; that is a different line from the trunk his memorial proposed, from Lugou Bridge through Henan to Hankou. The biography of Wang Wenshao in the same chapter records the building of the Beijing-Hankou railway; whether that is this line, this article does not decide, and it will not supply the missing details. [QSG4371-E14][QSG4371-E20][QSG4371-E22][QSG4371-E23]

At Huguang the biography records another string of undertakings. Daye yielded iron and Pingxiang in Jiangxi yielded coal, so he petitioned to open an ironworks below Mount Dabie at Hanyang to supply the railway, and at the same time specialist works for rifles, cannon, steel, and powder. Because Jing and Xiang grew mulberry, cotton, hemp, and ramie and had hides to spare, he set up bureaus for weaving cloth, spinning yarn, reeling silk, and making hemp and leather. He backed them with dike works and knit them together with currency measures: "From this Hubei's revenues came to be called abundant, and building works rose by the day." Beyond the railway, ironworks, bureaus, dikes, and currency were strung into one chain. [QSG4371-E14][QSG4371-E15][QSG4371-E16]

The biography passes a verdict on him: "Wherever he took office, he had works begun. He aimed at the grand, and did not ask how much it cost." It also records that after decades as a provincial official, "at his death, his family had not gained a single mu of land." One reading: that verdict and the numbered list share the same style - bold enough to open big undertakings, willing to write each out item by item. As for spending, the verdict says he did not ask the amount; the biography gives no figures. In the author's view, read with the qualifier about his family's land, "did not ask how much it cost" should not be taken as spending for private gain. [QSG4371-E17][QSG4371-E21]

## The Order Is the Argument

One reading is that the memorial includes at least four components (besides the picture of connections): a ranking of goals (moving goods and enriching livelihoods greatest, raising troops and forwarding supplies next); a numbered, item-by-item list of benefits, seven in all; a proposal to complete the line section by section, year by year; and responsibility assigned to offices (north to the Governor-General of Zhili, south to the Governor-General of Huguang, the Governor of Henan as deputy). In the author's view, writing it this way may steer the argument away from "is it harmful" toward "do these itemized benefits hold, and can these three offices carry them." [QSG4371-E5][QSG4371-E13][QSG4371-E19][QSG4371-E7][QSG4371-E8]

One reading: putting "enriching livelihoods" before "raising troops and forwarding supplies" sets the least contested, hardest-to-topple goal at the front. The censors spoke of "the harms of railways," and the two points about not drawing in the enemy and easily avoiding graves and houses may answer the word "harms," though the biography does not record which harms were meant. The author infers that this drafting is tactical; the biography records no thought process, so this is the author's interpretation. [QSG4371-E9][QSG4371-E5][QSG4371-E3]

Alternatives the author offers (not found in the memorial as the biography records it, therefore counterfactual): the memorial already proposes completing the line section by section, year by year, but the recorded text has no design for building a short stretch first and letting freight volumes and the degree of disruption decide the rest. The biography does record that Weng Tonghe and others asked to try building in the frontier regions, but that was for ease of moving troops, not a matter of building one stretch first and letting freight volumes and disruption decide the remaining sections. Another option would be to attach a checkable yardstick to each "benefit," so opponents could test them one by one, and that too does not appear in the memorial as recorded. Both designs are comparison items the author raises, not part of the memorial as the biography records it. [QSG4371-E19][QSG4371-E18]

## How to Use This Today

> When drafting a proposal or a review document, you can borrow this memorial's order: rather than opening with a list of pros and cons, state up front which goal weighs most and which comes next; then number the supporting reasons one by one, like those seven numbered benefits, leaving each open to the reader's challenge: "does this hold?"

> Once the plan is chosen, the second step is stages and owners: who takes the northern stretch, who the southern, who serves as deputy - written down, no fuzzy territory. At the same time agree when the first stage can be checked on its own, so a stretch gets running and tested early rather than waiting for the whole line to be joined before the first inspection.

## What This Source Cannot Tell Us

First, this article relies mainly on Zhang Zhidong's biography in the Draft History of the Qing, plus one lead from the biography of Wang Wenshao in the same chapter, and has not checked it against any record outside the Draft History. Which harms the censors meant, and what reasons the Naval Office gave, the biography records not at all; so this article can go no further than "pressed the harms of railways and asked that the project be halted." [QSG4371-E3][QSG4371-E20]

Second, the memorial text is documentary text as the historians recorded it, not necessarily words he spoke verbatim; all text quoted in this article is reported as the history records it. That set of "benefits" is a list of qualitative reasons he compiled himself; the recorded text gives no quantities, and it cannot be read as cost-benefit analysis. [QSG4371-E5][QSG4371-E19]

Third, Zhang's own biography records no schedule, outlay, or relocations for the proposed line. It goes on to record his petition for an ironworks to supply the railway, and also records that in the thirty-fourth year he was put in charge of the Canton-Hankou railway, which is not the same line as the one his memorial proposed. The biography of Wang Wenshao in the same chapter records the building of the Beijing-Hankou railway, but whether it is the same line cannot be determined, and this article does not fill in the gaps. "Approved by rescript" is approval, not the start of work, still less a finished line. This article also converts no Common Era years the original does not have, and will not say he knew the outcome in advance: when he wrote those numbered benefits, what lay before him was a checklist, not a report card. ## Think about it

A team of ten or so must decide whether to rewrite an old system they have run for years: one side wants to tear it down and start over, one wants incremental patching, one says leave it alone this year - and none of the three can produce data that overpowers the others.

**With no second independent audit available and nobody able to guarantee the outcome, how would you publish the ranking of priorities, and rewrite each side's reasons as a checklist that can be tested point by point? If you had to break the work into stages and assign them to named people, which stage would you cut first, and why?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The disagreement is mainly about the weight of goals, not about what is true; third-party audit data will not arrive in the short term; at least one person on the team is willing to be accountable for the acceptance of each stage.
- **What you could do**: First fix and publish the goal ranking: put "what matters most to users" first and "what is technically prettiest" second, in writing and up front. Next write each of the three positions as a numbered checklist: one line per item, stating the basis, what counts as holding, and what would refute it. Then choose by the checklist, split the work into two or three stages, and name the owner and a checkable result for each stage. Finally agree that when the first stage is done you return to the checklist and see on the spot which items hold.
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's alternative: decide nothing wholesale; run a two-week pilot on one small stretch and use real data to settle the whole plan - or keep the old system and replace only the part that matters most. Neither appears in this case record; both are comparison items the author raises.
