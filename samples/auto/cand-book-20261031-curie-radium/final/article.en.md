# No Patents, and the Bill for a Gram of Radium

*The Curies published their method for preparing radium without reserve, and the radium industry grew freely on it. They themselves long went without money and without a proper workplace. When Meloney, who launched the subscription and later wrote the introduction to Pierre Curie, the book Marie Curie published in 1923, visited before the 1921 gift, Marie Curie's laboratory still lacked sufficient equipment, and a subscription raised by American women finally secured her a gram of radium. It is a ledger with weight on both sides.*

## A Golden Key

May 20, 1921, the White House. The women of America gave a gram of radium to its discoverer, but the radium itself was not in the room. What President Harding placed in Marie Curie's hands was a small golden key, the one that opened the casket devised for transporting the radium. The tubes of radium were still at the Bureau, whose officials had kindly offered to make the measurements and to take care of the packing and delivery to the ship. [CUR-E19][CUR-E27][CUR-E28]

A gram of radium then sold for one hundred thousand dollars. The process for preparing it had been published years earlier, without reserve, by Marie Curie and her husband Pierre Curie, and the radium industry was now thriving on that very process and selling its product at a high price. She had helped give the method away, yet the radium in her laboratory was used at that time only for extracting emanations for hospital use. Meloney set out to raise a subscription in America to enable her to go on with her work; Meloney later wrote the introduction to Pierre Curie, the book Marie Curie published in 1923. That book records both sides of the account: Meloney's introduction notes the price and the subscription, and in the book's autobiographical notes Marie Curie reckons up the fortune she gave up. [CUR-E17][CUR-E7][CUR-E8][CUR-E13][CUR-E18][CUR-E11]

## In a Leaky Wooden Shed

The story begins in Paris in the late 1890s. The Curies were trying to extract radium and polonium from ore, and they lacked a proper place to work in, personnel and money. Pitchblende was an expensive mineral, and they could not afford to buy a sufficient quantity. At that time the principal source of this mineral was at St. Joachimsthal in Bohemia, where the Austrian government worked a mine for the extraction of uranium. Its residues had so far not been used at all, and they believed the radium, and part of the polonium, would be found in them. Through the influence of the Vienna Academy of Sciences they secured several tons of the residues at an advantageous price. In the beginning the costs came out of their own pockets; only later were they given a few subventions and some help from outside sources. [CUR-E1]

The chemical treatments had to be carried on in an abandoned storeroom: a wooden shed with a bituminous floor and a glass roof that did not keep the rain out, and without any interior arrangements. All it contained were some worn pine tables, a cast-iron stove that worked badly, and the blackboard Pierre Curie loved to use. There were no hoods to carry away the poisonous gases thrown off by the treatments, so the work had to be moved outside into the court; when the weather was unfavorable they went on inside, leaving the windows open. [CUR-E2]

For two years they worked there practically unaided. One qualification: the laboratory helper, Petit, kept lending a hand in whatever time he could spare; G. Bemont, one of the laboratory chiefs at the School, gave temporary help in 1898; and around 1900 the young chemist André Debierne joined them. Once the work was divided, Pierre studied the properties of radium while Marie Curie pushed ahead with the chemical experiments aimed at preparing pure radium salts. She worked with as much as twenty kilogrammes of material at a time, until the hangar was full of great vessels of precipitates and liquids: moving the containers about, transferring the liquids, and stirring the boiling material in the cast-iron basin with an iron bar, for hours at a stretch. [CUR-E3][CUR-E23]

## The Method Given to Everyone

At one point (the book gives no year), Geneva came calling for Pierre, offering material advantages and the opportunity of a life comparable in its tranquillity with that in the country. In Marie Curie's account the decision was a grave one, and Pierre was tempted. What finally settled it was the radium research itself, since he feared the interruption that such a change must involve. At this moment the chair of physics in the Sorbonne's P.C.N. course was vacant; this was the physics, chemistry and natural history course obligatory for students of medicine. He applied, and was appointed through the influence of Henri Poincaré, who was anxious to free him from the necessity of quitting France. Marie Curie was given charge of the physics lectures at the Normal School for Girls at Sèvres. They stayed in Paris and their income increased, but working conditions grew harder: Pierre was doing double teaching, and the P.C.N., with its very large number of students, fatigued him greatly. [CUR-E4][CUR-E21]

The new post came without a laboratory. In the annex of the Sorbonne at 12 rue Cuvier, which served as teaching quarters for the P.C.N., all he had at his disposition was a little office and a single work room. He meant to receive students and start them in research, so he took steps to find larger working quarters. Anyone who has taken similar steps knows the wall of financial and administrative obstacles he was throwing himself against: the official letters, the visits, the requests, and what even the least success entailed. It all thoroughly wearied and discouraged him. Besides these difficulties, the research could not advance without industrial means of treating the raw material. As early as 1899 Pierre Curie organized a first industrial experiment, using for it a chance installation placed at his disposition by the Central Society of Chemical Products, with which he had dealt over the construction of his balances. The technical details were arranged very successfully by André Debierne, and the operations brought good results, though a special crew had to be trained for chemical work that demanded special precautions. [CUR-E5][CUR-E6]

There is something else she writes about in two places in the book. In Marie Curie's telling, and with her agreement, Pierre Curie refused to draw any material profit from the discovery. They also took no copyright (the English translation of the biography reads "We took no copyright"), published without reserve all the results of their research as well as the exact processes of the preparation of radium, and gave whatever information was asked of them. In the autobiographical notes she says it again: the two of them always refused to draw any material profit from the discovery, the process was published from the beginning, they took out no patent (the translation reads "We took out no patent"), and they reserved no advantage in any industrial exploitation. The book gives no date for the decision. [CUR-E7][CUR-E11]

The money was still short. Inspired by their attempt, a French industrialist, Armet de Lisle, took what seemed at that epoch the daring step of founding a veritable radium factory to furnish the product to physicians, whose interest in the biological effects of radium and its possible therapeutic applications had been aroused by the publication of various investigations. The project proved a success because he could employ men already trained by the Curies in the delicate processes of the manufacture. Radium was now regularly placed on sale, at a high price it is true, given the special conditions under which it had to be made and the immediate rise in the cost of the minerals necessary to its production. The book goes on to say that, in an entirely disinterested manner, the industrialist placed a little working place in his factory at their disposition, together with a part of the means necessary to use it. Other funds were added either by the Curies themselves or through subventions, the most important of which, accorded by the Academy of Sciences in 1902, amounted to 20,000 francs. [CUR-E8][CUR-E9]

In 1902 Marie Curie succeeded in preparing a decigramme of chloride of pure radium that gave only the spectrum of the new element, radium, and made a first determination of its atomic weight, a value much higher than that of barium. The chemical individuality of radium was completely established. In 1907 she made a second and more precise determination, as 225.35; the number accepted when she wrote was 226. She also succeeded, jointly with André Debierne, in obtaining radium in the state of metal. The total quantity she prepared and gave to the laboratory, in agreement with Pierre Curie's desire, amounted to more than a gramme of radium element. [CUR-E22][CUR-E12]

Years later, in the autobiographical notes, she laid out the rest of the ledger. Radium is found in minerals in very small quantities, so its price is very high, and the profits of its manufacture have been great, since the substance is used to cure a number of diseases. What they sacrificed in renouncing the exploitation of their discovery, then, was a fortune, one that could after them have gone to their children. And what was even more to be considered, she wrote, was the objection of many of their friends, who argued "not without reason" that if they had guaranteed their rights they could have had the financial means of founding a satisfactory Institute of Radium, without the difficulties that had been such a handicap to both of them and were still a handicap to her. She conceded the point and held her ground all the same; in the English translation: "Yet, I still believe that we have done right." [CUR-E13]

Meloney, who later wrote the book's introduction, had launched the subscription in New York. When Meloney met her, Marie Curie said that America had about fifty grammes of radium: four of these in Baltimore, six in Denver, seven in New York, and on she went, naming the location of every grain. And in France? "My laboratory," she said, "has hardly more than a gramme." Meloney, astonished, exclaimed that she had only a gramme. "I? Oh, I have none," Marie Curie corrected. "It belongs to my laboratory." That week Meloney learned that the market price of a gramme of radium was one hundred thousand dollars. She also learned that Marie Curie's laboratory, although practically a new building, was without sufficient equipment, and that the radium held there was used at that time only for extracting emanations for hospital use in cancer treatment. When she arrived in New York a few weeks afterwards, Meloney had hoped to find ten women to subscribe ten thousand dollars each and buy a gramme without the publicity of a general campaign. She found one or two. There were not ten women to buy that gramme of radium, but there were a hundred thousand women and a group of men determined that the money must be raised, and in less than a year the fund had been raised. In Marie Curie's own account, a committee including several prominent women and distinguished scientific men received some important gifts and made an appeal for a public subscription. A great number of women's organizations, especially colleges and clubs, responded, and in many cases gifts came from persons who had experienced the benefit of radiumtherapy. In this way the "Marie Curie Radium Fund" of more than one hundred thousand dollars was collected. [CUR-E15][CUR-E17][CUR-E18][CUR-E24][CUR-E25]

As Meloney tells it, on the eve of the ceremony there was one more snag: the papers were to put this gramme of radium in Marie Curie's own name. Marie Curie read them and said it must not be left that way. The gramme represented a great deal of money, but more than that it represented the women of the country: it was not for her, it was for science. She was not well and might die any day. Her daughter Eve was not of legal age, and if she died the radium would go into her estate and be divided between her daughters. That was not its purpose; it had to be consecrated for all time to the use of science. The paper had to be fixed that night, because the next day she received the radium, and, she said, as Meloney reports it, "I might die tomorrow. Too much is at stake." So, late as it was on that hot May evening, a lawyer was secured and prepared the paper from a draft Marie Curie herself had written. She signed it before starting for Washington: in the event of her death the gramme of radium went to the Institut du Radium in Paris, for the exclusive use of the Laboratoire Curie. The next day at the White House, what she received from President Harding was the small golden key. [CUR-E26][CUR-E27][CUR-E28][CUR-E19]

## Both Sides of the Ledger

The author's reading is that the cost of these choices was not a single loss but a chain. The method went public; the industry developed freely; physicians and scientists got their products quickly. At the same time the minerals got dearer and the price of radium stayed high. Her laboratory was without sufficient equipment, and the radium held there was used at that time only for extracting emanations for hospital use; the gramme and more that Marie Curie prepared with her own hands had gone entirely to the laboratory. One way to put it: they paid the start-up costs of a whole industry out of their own research conditions. [CUR-E8][CUR-E12][CUR-E13][CUR-E17]

She gave her own account of it in the autobiographical notes, and it is her position, not a fact that can be checked: humanity needs practical men who make the best of their work for the sake of their own interests without forgetting the general interest, but it also needs dreamers, for whom the unselfish following of a purpose is so imperative that it becomes impossible for them to devote much attention to their own material benefit. No doubt it could be said that these idealists do not deserve riches, since they do not have the desire for them. A society well organized, she thought, ought to assure to these workers the means for efficient labor, in a life from which material care is excluded, so that the life may be freely devoted to the service of scientific research. [CUR-E14]

The author's alternative, and the book records no such discussion: license by use, free for research and teaching and charged for commercial exploitation, or else charge for the product and leave the method in the public domain. Some income might have been kept that way, at the price of negotiation, oversight, and a long entanglement with industry. What the friends urged was retaining the rights and building the institute out of the proceeds. In the author's view, the difference is not who is nobler; it is who carries the risk. [CUR-E13][CUR-E14]

## The Same Ledger Today

> A familiar scene today: a hospital laboratory technician writes up the sample-processing method she has refined over many years as an open document, and several peer hospitals start using it. Her own department is still running the same old equipment, and the request for new purchases sits at the end of the queue. What she has to judge is not whether she is noble enough, but who pays for the next round of improvements once the method is public.

> Another scene, at the level of institutions: a small team opens up a dataset it has curated for years, free of charge; large companies turn it into services that sell; the team lives on project contracts and donations, and the people who did the data work earn far less than they would by changing jobs. Organizations like that are not short of applause; they are short of stable funding and conditions that keep people around. Applause does not pay the rent.

## What This Material Cannot Tell Us

The limits need to be stated. Every historical fact in this piece comes from one book: Pierre Curie, Macmillan, 1923, translated by C. and V. Kellogg. The Pierre Curie biography and the autobiographical notes are Marie Curie's own account, and the Meloney passages are her report of what she saw and heard; none of it is an independent record. The decision not to patent is undated in the book, and no minutes or conversations of the deliberations survive there; this piece does not supply them. [CUR-E7][CUR-E11][CUR-E16]

The English translation phrases the decision two different ways, and this piece follows the wording as printed: "We took no copyright" in one place, "We took out no patent" in the other. The numbers are taken as printed too: one hundred thousand dollars, fifty grammes, more than a gramme, one hundred thousand women. Her remarks about practical men and dreamers are her views, not statements of fact; the paragraphs marked as the author's inference and as the author's alternative are this piece's interpretation, with nothing in the material to match. ## Think about it

You and a colleague spend two years developing an extraction method that could be patented or could be published. Your employer leaves the decision to you: seek protection, which might pay for a new laboratory or might only produce a pile of paperwork; or publish, so that peers and customers can use it at once, the field may get moving earlier, and your own funding stays exactly as it is.

**Before you start, how should the account be worked out? And if you decide to publish, who should guarantee, by what mechanism, the conditions for the next round of research by the people who published?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume the method does have commercial value, that your employer genuinely lets you decide, and that in the short term it will not bring back the money for laboratory equipment.
- **What you could do**: First, split the returns into three parts and estimate each: direct licensing income; the collaborations and citations that come from peers accelerating once the method is public; and the gain or loss in your own research conditions. Second, ask the worst case: if not one dollar comes back after publication, how long can the group hold out? Third, if you still choose to publish, settle the support first, matching funds from your employer, access to equipment, and the person-hours needed to turn the method into a deliverable document. Fourth, put the decision in writing, so there is no dispute afterwards about what was agreed.
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's alternative, and no such discussion appears in the material: tiered licensing, free for research and teaching and paid for commercial use; or publish the method while keeping the supply of the key material in your own hands. It preserves some income, at the cost of someone doing negotiation and oversight for the long haul.

</details>
