# A Marriage Offer as a Test: Why Wu Qi Left Wei

*Wu Qi held Wei's western frontier at Xihe, and even Tian Wen, chief minister at the time, admitted that his record was the greater. A later chief minister, Gongshu, resented him and wanted to drive him out, so he laid a two-step trap: persuade the ruler to offer Wu Qi a princess in marriage, then bring Wu Qi home to watch Gongshu's own wife, a princess, treat her husband with contempt. Wu Qi declined the marriage, and the ruler grew suspicious and stopped trusting him.*

## A Scene in the Chief Minister's House

The wife of Gongshu, chief minister of the state of Wei, was a princess of Wei. One day Gongshu brought the general Wu Qi home with him, and in front of their guest the princess flew into a temper and treated her husband with contempt. Marquis Wu, the ruler of Wei, wanted to give Wu Qi a princess in marriage; having seen that scene in Gongshu's house, Wu Qi declined. From then on the marquis suspected him and no longer trusted him. Afraid of being punished, Wu Qi left Wei for the state of Chu. [SJ0651-E17][SJ0651-E19][SJ0651-E20]

Neither the temper nor the marriage offer was an accident: both were arranged by Gongshu, following a plan from one of his own servants. How could a single marriage offer drive out a general who had held Wei's western frontier, and whose record even Tian Wen, the chief minister before Gongshu, admitted was greater than his own? [SJ0651-E17][SJ0651-E18][SJ0651-E19]

## The First Time He Was Suspected, It Was Also About a Marriage

Wu Qi came from the state of Wey and loved warfare. As a young man he served the state of Lu, and the first time he fell under suspicion, it was over a marriage. When Qi attacked Lu, Lu wanted him to command its army, but his wife was from Qi and Lu did not trust him. Eager to make his name, Wu Qi killed his wife to show he had no tie to Qi. Only then did Lu make him a general, and he led its army against Qi and routed it. [SJ0651-E1][SJ0651-E2]

After the victory, someone in Lu denounced him to its ruler, charging that Wu Qi was suspicious and ruthless. The accuser dug up his past: as a young man from a rich family he had sought office abroad and failed, squandering the family fortune; when his neighbours laughed at him, he killed more than thirty of those who mocked him and left Wey. Parting from his mother, he bit his arm and swore never to return to Wey until he was a chief minister. When his mother later died he did not go back, and his teacher Zengzi broke with him. The accuser also did the sums for Lu's ruler: Lu was a small state, and a name for victories would make the other states eye it greedily; Lu and Wey were brother states, so to employ Wu Qi was to abandon Wey. The ruler of Lu grew suspicious and dismissed him. Hearing that Marquis Wen, the ruler of Wei, was a worthy ruler, Wu Qi went to serve him. [SJ0651-E3][SJ0651-E26]

## From Governor of Xihe to the Refused Marriage

Marquis Wen first asked Li Ke what sort of man Wu Qi was. Li Ke's verdict was mixed: Wu Qi was greedy and fond of women, but in the use of troops not even Sima Rangju could surpass him. As a commander, Wu Qi dressed and ate like the lowest of his soldiers, slept without a mat, marched instead of riding, and carried his own rations. When one soldier had an abscess, Wu Qi sucked out the pus himself. The soldier's mother wept when she heard. Years before, she said, General Wu had done the same for the boy's father, who then fought without ever turning back and died among the enemy; now he had done it for her son, and she did not know where her son would die. Valuing Wu Qi's skill with troops, his honesty and fairness, and the loyalty he won from his men, Marquis Wen made him governor of Xihe, to hold off Qin and Han. [SJ0651-E4][SJ0651-E5][SJ0651-E6][SJ0651-E7]

After Marquis Wen died, Wu Qi served his son, Marquis Wu. Wei then appointed a chief minister and chose Tian Wen. Wu Qi was displeased and challenged Tian Wen to compare their merits. He compared them on three counts. In command of the armies, making soldiers glad to die and enemy states afraid to plot against Wei, how do you compare with me? Tian Wen said: Not as well as you. Holding Xihe, so that Qin's troops dare not march east and Han and Zhao come as guests, how do you compare with me? Again Tian Wen said he could not match him. Governing the officials, winning the people's affection and filling the treasury, how do you compare with me? Once more Tian Wen said he could not match him. When the three were done, Wu Qi asked: in all three you rank below me, yet your post ranks above mine. Why? [SJ0651-E11][SJ0651-E12][SJ0651-E13][SJ0651-E14][SJ0651-E15][SJ0651-E27][SJ0651-E28]

Tian Wen answered with a single question. The ruler is young and the state uneasy, he said; the great ministers have not yet rallied to him and the common people do not yet trust the government. At such a time, is the state to be entrusted to you, or to me? Tian Wen did not deny Wu Qi's victories. Wu Qi had been comparing personal merit; Tian Wen was asking who should carry the state at that moment. Wu Qi was silent for a long time, then said: To you. That, said Tian Wen, is why I rank above you. The history adds that Wu Qi then knew he was not Tian Wen's equal. [SJ0651-E15][SJ0651-E16][SJ0651-E17]

After Tian Wen died, Gongshu became chief minister. Gongshu was married to a princess of Wei, and he resented Wu Qi and wanted to drive him out. His servant said: Wu Qi is easy to drive away. When Gongshu asked how, the servant first described the man: Wu Qi had integrity, kept his hands clean, and prized his good name. Then he laid out a plan in two steps. [SJ0651-E17][SJ0651-E18]

The servant's plan: in step one, Gongshu would tell Marquis Wu that Wu Qi was a man of worth, but that the marquis's state was small and bordered on mighty Qin, and he privately feared Wu Qi had no mind to stay. In the servant's script, the marquis would ask what to do. Gongshu would then propose testing Wu Qi with the offer of a princess: if he meant to stay he would surely accept, and if not he would surely decline, so the offer would serve as a divination of his intentions. In step two, Gongshu would then invite Wu Qi home and have the princess he had married flare up and slight him in front of Wu Qi. Seeing a princess treat her husband with such contempt, the servant predicted, Wu Qi would surely turn the marriage down. [SJ0651-E18][SJ0651-E19]

It went exactly as the servant had foretold. Seeing the princess scorn the chief minister of Wei, Wu Qi declined Marquis Wu's offer. The marquis grew suspicious and no longer trusted him. Afraid of being punished, Wu Qi left Wei and went to Chu. [SJ0651-E20]

In Chu, King Dao, who had long heard of his ability, made him chief minister. He made the laws clear and strict, cut offices that were not needed, dismissed royal kinsmen of distant blood, and put the state's strength into its army; Chu grew strong, and the other states feared its power. For this reason, the history says, Chu's nobles all wanted him dead. When King Dao died, members of the royal clan rose up and attacked Wu Qi. He ran to the king's body and threw himself on it; his attackers shot and stabbed him, and struck the king's body too. After King Dao was buried and the crown prince came to the throne, he had everyone who had shot at Wu Qi and also struck the king's body put to death, and more than seventy families were wiped out for it. [SJ0651-E21][SJ0651-E22][SJ0651-E23][SJ0651-E24]

## Why the Trap Worked

The author's inference: the trap turned a question no one could settle, whether Wu Qi meant to stay, into an act anyone could see: would he accept the marriage or not? And its designer had worked out his answer in advance. A man who prized his good name, having just seen how a princess treated her husband the chief minister, would very likely refuse such a match. Put together, the two steps made a refusal that came from his own character look, to the marquis, like proof that he had no mind to stay. In Lu he had killed his wife to prove he had no divided loyalty; in Wei, one refused marriage was taken as a sign he meant to leave. [SJ0651-E2][SJ0651-E18][SJ0651-E20]

At the end of the biography, Sima Qian remarked that Wu Qi had once told Marquis Wu that strong terrain counts for less than virtue, yet in Chu he himself governed harshly and with little kindness, and that cost him his life. That is Sima Qian's own verdict. [SJ0651-E25]

## Think about it

In a small company, a core member with an unmistakable record is handed a staged test: management pretends the firm is about to merge with another, to see whether he immediately picks a side or watches and waits. He sees through the arrangement and resigns on the spot — and management reads the resignation as proof that he never meant to stay.

**When you see trust being engineered into an examination, when should you explain, when should you wait, and when should you walk? Should the call rest on how far suspicion has already travelled, or on whether you hold something in hand that can speak for you?**

- **Assumptions**: Suspicion is already open, not mere rumor.

- **What you could do**: First decide whether this is a test or a verdict: if it is a test, your reaction is the answer sheet; if it is a verdict, explanation is only extra paperwork.
Find something that can land within two or three weeks, and let the result speak rather than declarations.
Watch which way suspicion is moving: if tests come one after another and no chance to explain is given, the problem is not information but position.
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's alternative: neither play the part you have been handed nor walk out on the spot, but call the arrangement by its name and force the question of whether he means to stay into an open negotiation. The cost is being read as provocative; the gain is turning a test staged in the dark into a bargain made in the light.
