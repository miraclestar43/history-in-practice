# Refusing the Bride: A Staged Test and Wu Qi's Departure from Wei

*He had held Wei's western frontier at Xihe, and his record was not in dispute. Then an arranged marriage cost him his ruler's trust, and he left for Chu. What drove him out was not a battle but a piece of stagecraft.*

## A Domestic Detail Made Into a Verdict

Wu Qi governed the district of Xihe for Wei, holding the frontier against Qin and Han. Yet what finally pushed him out of Wei was not a lost battle but a marriage. He refused to take a Wei princess as his wife; Marquis Wu stopped trusting him; and before long Wu Qi had gone to the state of Chu. [SJ0651-E7][SJ0651-E20]

In the author's reading, almost nothing in this turn of events can be scored as a victory or a defeat. The man who set the trap used an ordinary household matter as bait, converting an unanswerable question — did he mean to stay? — into an act everyone could see. One explanation: precisely because the test is so small, the man being tested cannot easily turn his refusal into a large matter to be explained.

## The Last Time He Was Suspected, It Was Also a Marriage

Wu Qi was a man of the state of Wey (衛 — distinct from Wei 魏, the state he would later serve), devoted to the art of war. He had studied under Zengzi and served the ruler of Lu. When Qi attacked Lu, Lu wanted him as commander; the trouble was that his wife was a woman of Qi, and Lu suspected him. To show that he had no bond with Qi, he killed his wife. Lu then made him commander, he marched against Qi, and he smashed its army. [SJ0651-E1][SJ0651-E2]

After the victory, someone in Lu described him to the ruler of Lu like this: a suspicious, ruthless man. In his youth his family was worth a fortune; he went off seeking office, failed, and ran through the family fortune, until his neighbors laughed at him. He then killed more than thirty of those who had mocked him and went out eastward through the outer gate of Wey's city wall. Parting from his mother, he bit his arm and swore he would not return to Wey until he became a high minister or chancellor. She died, and he never went back, whereupon Zengzi broke with him. The speech then turned to the arithmetic: Lu is a small state, and once it carries the name of a victory, the other lords will begin to plot against Lu. Lu and Wey are brother states; to employ Wu Qi is to abandon Wey. The text relied on here ends with this speech and records nothing of how the ruler of Lu responded. [SJ0651-E3]

Notice what the speech never says: it does not deny that Wu Qi can fight. It attacks the man and the ledger of using him — a flaw in private character, no return home at his mother's death, a small state that cannot afford a reputation for victory. The author infers that the thrust of the speech is to swap one question for another: not can he win, but can we afford him. The source records no reaction from Wu Qi to this suspicion, and this article will not write one for him.

## From Sucking an Abscess to Refusing a Bride

Later, Marquis Wen of Wei asked Li Ke what sort of man Wu Qi was. Li Ke replied — rendered here in translation — that Wu Qi was greedy and fond of women, but that in commanding troops even Sima Rangju could not surpass him. As a general, Wu Qi wore the same clothes and ate the same food as the lowest of his soldiers. He slept without a mat, rode neither horse nor cart on the march, wrapped and carried his own share of grain, and shared the men's toil and hardship. [SJ0651-E4][SJ0651-E5]

One soldier developed a carbuncle, and Wu Qi sucked the carbuncle himself. The soldier's mother heard of this and wept. Someone asked her why she wept, when the general himself had tended her son's carbuncle. Her answer, given here in translation and paraphrase: it was not as they thought. Years ago Lord Wu had done the same for the boy's father, who then went into battle without turning on his heels and died at the enemy's hands. Now Lord Wu had done it again for her son, and she did not know where this child would meet his death. That was why she wept. In the author's reading, it is the most jarring stroke in the whole story: the way he won his men's hearts, and what that cost, are written in the same tears. [SJ0651-E6]

Marquis Wen judged him skilled in war, honest and fair, and able to win the hearts of his men, and made him governor of Xihe to hold off Qin and Han. Once Marquis Wu was drifting down the West River; in midstream he turned to Wu Qi and exclaimed, in paraphrase, at how magnificent and secure the mountains and rivers were — a treasure of the state of Wei. Of Wu Qi's reply only its conclusion is given here, in translation: from this it can be seen that safety lies in virtue, not in natural defenses; if my lord does not cultivate virtue, everyone in this boat will become an enemy state. Marquis Wu approved, calling it well said. [SJ0651-E7][SJ0651-E8][SJ0651-E10]

In time Wei appointed a chancellor, and the office went to Tian Wen. Wu Qi was displeased and challenged him to compare their merits. In translation and paraphrase, the exchange runs as follows. Wu Qi asked how Tian Wen compared with him in commanding the three armies, making the soldiers glad to die, so that no enemy state dared to plot; Tian Wen said he was not his equal. Wu Qi asked how Tian Wen compared with him in holding Xihe, so that the armies of Qin dared not turn eastward and Han and Zhao came as obedient followers; again Tian Wen said he was not his equal. (The original weighs three matters; two are given here.) Wu Qi pressed the point: in all three of these Tian Wen fell below him, yet his rank stood above Wu Qi's — why? [SJ0651-E11][SJ0651-E12][SJ0651-E13][SJ0651-E14]

Tian Wen answered — rendered here in translation — that the ruler was young and the state uncertain, the great ministers had not attached themselves to him, and the people did not trust him; at such a moment, should the state be entrusted to Wu Qi, or to Tian Wen? Wu Qi fell silent for a long time, then said it should be entrusted to Tian Wen. Tian Wen replied that this was precisely why he sat above Wu Qi. The source adds that Wu Qi knew himself the lesser of the two. [SJ0651-E15][SJ0651-E16][SJ0651-E17]

After Tian Wen died, Gongshu became chancellor; he had married a Wei princess, and he sought to harm Wu Qi. His servant said something very light — in translation, that Wu Qi was easy to remove. The scheme ran in two steps. First, a word to the marquis: Wu Qi is a man of talent, but Wei is a small state and borders mighty Qin; I fear he has no thought of staying. Test him with the offer of a princess — in the servant's words, rendered here in translation: if Wu Qi means to stay, he will certainly accept; if he has no such intention, he will certainly decline; use this to gauge him. Second, the arrangement: invite Wu Qi home with the chancellor, and have the princess lose her temper and slight the chancellor in front of him. [SJ0651-E17][SJ0651-E18][SJ0651-E19]

Wu Qi saw the princess slighting the chancellor of Wei, and sure enough he declined the match. Marquis Wu drew suspicion from this and no longer believed him. Wu Qi, afraid of the consequences, left Wei and went on to Chu. [SJ0651-E20]

King Dao of Chu had long known his reputation and made him chancellor on arrival. Wu Qi laid down clear laws and careful orders, cut away the offices that were not needed, removed distant members of the royal clan, bent everything toward a strong army, and swept away the talk of the persuaders who argued for the vertical and horizontal alliances. Chu then pacified the Hundred Yue in the south, absorbed Chen and Cai in the north, repelled the Three Jin, and struck west against Qin. The other lords began to fear Chu's strength. Every nobleman of royal blood wanted to do him harm. When King Dao died, men of the royal house and the great ministers rose in revolt and attacked Wu Qi; he ran to the dead king's body and flung himself over it. Those hunting him shot and stabbed at Wu Qi — and hit the corpse of the king as well. Once the king was buried and the crown prince on the throne, the chief minister was ordered to execute everyone who had shot at Wu Qi and struck the royal body; more than seventy clans were destroyed for their part in it. [SJ0651-E21][SJ0651-E22][SJ0651-E23][SJ0651-E24]

## Why the Test Worked

Step back and look at the design. It trades an unanswerable question — does he mean to stay? — for a visible act: does he accept the marriage? The servant's reading of Wu Qi is on the record — in translation, that he was a man of integrity and restraint who cherished his good name. That is the history's description of him, not a sketch invented here. One explanation for the scheme's success: Wu Qi, seeing the princess treat the chancellor so cheaply, declined the match. Agreeing to a marriage while the household he was visiting was being humiliated would not fit the name-cherishing character the servant attributed to him. He acted just as the servant had predicted, and stepped into a frame drawn around that reading of his character. [SJ0651-E18]

The key sentence is the one in which, in translation, Marquis Wu grew suspicious and no longer believed him. The source records no defense by Wu Qi; it says only that he feared being punished and so left. The author infers that he may have judged explanation already useless, or that he worried the explanation itself would be taken as one more piece of evidence that he had no intention of staying. The alternative the author would put on the table: tell Marquis Wu on the spot what had happened at that meeting, or hold off on any decision and wait for another chance to prove himself. The source says nothing about his weighing such options. [SJ0651-E20]

Sima Qian closes the biography with this verdict, rendered here in translation: Wu Qi urged Marquis Wu that strength of position was no substitute for virtue, yet when he acted in Chu, cruelty and a want of kindness cost him his life — how sad. The line sets the words he spoke on the boat in midstream of the West River against his end in Chu. It is the historian's judgment, and this article presents it only as a record — not as an answer Wu Qi could have seen coming on the day he left Wei. [SJ0651-E25][SJ0651-E8][SJ0651-E10]

## A Small Test Arranged for You

> In modern teams, people with clear credit run into small arrangements of the same kind: a staged declaration of loyalty, a meeting they had no need to attend, a question thrown out casually. What is being tested is not performance but position. Some blow up on the spot and pour out their grievance, and by the next day everyone knows he will not be around long. The test itself proves nothing; the reaction proves everything.

> Others see through the test. They neither take the bait nor quarrel; they go back and finish the one piece of work in hand that can speak for them, and discuss leaving or staying once the results are in. The difference is often not the thickness of the record of achievement but the stage suspicion has reached: while it is still only doubt, facts can talk; once trust is gone, the same facts will only be reinterpreted.

## The Boundaries of This Story

This piece rests on a single source: the "Account of Sunzi and Wu Qi" in Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian. The exchange between Wu Qi and Tian Wen, the weeping of the soldier's mother, and the servant's scheme are all narratives within that history and need not be verbatim transcripts. Except where explicitly marked as the author's inference, the article does not invent Wu Qi's inner life. The original gives no dates in the common era, and none have been calculated here.

The account of Wu Qi's reforms in Chu and of his death is brief. What can be confirmed is the clarifying of laws and the careful framing of orders, the cutting of unneeded offices, the removal of distant members of the royal clan, and the campaigns — south against the Hundred Yue, north absorbing Chen and Cai, repelling the Three Jin, west against Qin. Beyond these the article adds nothing. Wherever the text above or below marks a view as an authorial inference or an author's alternative, it is interpretation and counterfactual, offered for discussion, not history. ## Think about it

In a small company, a core member with an unmistakable record is handed a staged test: management pretends the firm is about to merge with another, to see whether he immediately picks a side or watches and waits. He sees through the arrangement and resigns on the spot — and management reads the resignation as proof that he never meant to stay.

**When you see trust being engineered into an examination, when should you explain, when should you wait, and when should you walk? Should the call rest on how far suspicion has already travelled, or on whether you hold something in hand that can speak for you?**

- **Assumptions**: Suspicion is already open, not mere rumor.
The test has been run, and may well be run again.
A direct channel of conversation is still open.
One piece of work in your hands will land soon and can speak for you.
- **What you could do**: First decide whether this is a test or a verdict: if it is a test, your reaction is the answer sheet; if it is a verdict, explanation is only extra paperwork.
Find something that can land within two or three weeks, and let the result speak rather than declarations.
Watch which way suspicion is moving: if tests come one after another and no chance to explain is given, the problem is not information but position.
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's alternative: neither play the part you have been handed nor walk out on the spot, but call the arrangement by its name and force the question of whether he means to stay into an open negotiation. The cost is being read as provocative; the gain is turning a test staged in the dark into a bargain made in the light.
