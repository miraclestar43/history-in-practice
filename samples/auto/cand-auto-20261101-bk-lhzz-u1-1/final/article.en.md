# A Surrender Pact Outside Suzhou

*A field commander promised rewards face to face, a foreign officer stood guarantor, and the city gates opened. When the other side came to collect, whose promise was it to keep?*

unscheduled · Modern China · cand-auto-20261101-bk-lhzz-u1-1 rev 1

## When the Food Ran Out

In the tenth month of the second year of the Tongzhi reign, government forces had broken through the outer walls of Suzhou. Two of the city's commanders, Li Xiucheng and Tan Shaoguang, fell back into the inner city and held on. The besiegers closed in from three sides, by land and by water. Inside, the food ran out and the garrison's resolve began to waver. Gao Yunguan and other subordinate commanders among the defenders made overtures to Cheng Xueqi, a general on the government side outside the walls, offering to surrender. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E8][BK-LHZZ-U11-E9]

A surrender is not settled with a single message. Gao Yunguan needed to know what he would have to hand over, what he would receive in return, and why he should believe the other side would keep its word. All of it had to be agreed in person. How that pact was negotiated, and what made it believable, became the crux of everything that followed. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E9][BK-LHZZ-U11-E10]

## An Army Under Foreign Command

Cheng Xueqi was one of Li Hongzhang's field commanders. Also before the walls of Suzhou was an unusual force known as the Ever-Victorious Army. An American named Ward had first recruited and drilled it in Shanghai: a few dozen Europeans and Americans, joined by several hundred Chinese volunteers. It beat larger forces again and again, and both the government troops and their enemies came to call it the Ever-Victorious Army. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E1]

When Li Hongzhang's Huai Army first arrived in Shanghai, Westerners laughed behind their hands at the soldiers' rough clothes and caps. Li Hongzhang asked the men around him, in effect, whether an army's quality was to be judged by its uniforms. The rhetorical question was his reply to the mockery. Let them see the banners and drums of our generals, he went on, and they will reach their own verdict. In time the Europeans and Americans saw how brave the Huai officers were and how strict their discipline, and their attitude changed. The Ever-Victorious Army, too, began to accept Li Hongzhang's command. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E2]

After Ward's death, his successor, Burgevine, demanded an enormous sum for military funds from Yang Fang, the circuit intendant, or daotai, of Shanghai. When the money did not come, Burgevine beat Yang Fang and carried off forty thousand taels of silver. Li Hongzhang was furious. He took the matter up with the British consul, had Burgevine removed, and put a British officer, Gordon, in command of the Ever-Victorious Army in his place. That was the second month of the same year. Gordon, in other words, took over the force only after Li Hongzhang had negotiated with the British. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E3]

In the third month, government forces retook Taicang and Kunshan, with Cheng Xueqi doing the most to win them. Liang Qichao, in his biography of Li Hongzhang, records that from then on Gordon held Cheng in ever greater respect. In the sixth month, Cheng led more than 10,000 men on land and water and laid plans with Liu Mingchuan to recover Suzhou. Fighting hard near the city day after day, he took more than ten fortified positions in turn, among them those at Baodai Bridge and Tiger Hill. The Huai Army's reputation soared. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E4][BK-LHZZ-U11-E5][BK-LHZZ-U11-E6]

Li Xiucheng sent his subordinate commanders to gather more than eighty thousand men from Wuxi, Liyang, Yixing and elsewhere, with over a thousand boats, out to the mouth of the Grand Canal, while he himself took several thousand picked troops to hold Jinkui and relieve Suzhou. The two forces supported each other and clashed repeatedly with the government side, each winning some and losing some. On the nineteenth day of the tenth month, with Li Hongzhang directing the attack in person and Cheng Xueqi and Gordon leading the vanguard, the government forces fought their way through the outer walls. Li Xiucheng and Tan Shaoguang withdrew into the inner city and refused to yield. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E7][BK-LHZZ-U11-E8]

## A Pact on the Lake, a Banquet on the Ship

After Gao Yunguan's offer reached him, Cheng Xueqi went with Gordon by small boat to Yangcheng Lake, north of the city, to settle terms face to face with Gao and his fellow commanders. The terms put to them were these: kill Li Xiucheng and Tan Shaoguang and deliver their heads, and in return receive rewards of the second rank. Gordon acted as guarantor. Liang's next line, in translation, is that because Gordon stood as their guarantor, Gao Yunguan and the others had no doubts. As Liang tells it, what made the defectors trust the pact was Gordon's guarantee. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E10]

The terms were not accepted in full. Liang writes that in the end Gao Yunguan and the others could not bear to harm Li Xiucheng; they agreed only to behead Tan Shaoguang, and so they parted. This was a concession within the pact. The men outside the walls had asked for two heads, and the defectors would deliver only one. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E10]

Li Xiucheng sensed something of the plot, but by then there was nothing he could do, and he slipped out of the city by night. On the twenty-fourth, Tan Shaoguang summoned Gao Yunguan to his tent on some business. Gao went, taking with him Wang Youwei, a fierce fighter, and the moment he came before Tan he stabbed him to death. Gao's men then fell on Tan's personal guard of more than a thousand, and the gates were opened in surrender. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E11][BK-LHZZ-U11-E12]

On the twenty-fifth, Gao Yunguan and the others presented Tan Shaoguang's head and invited Cheng Xueqi into the city to inspect it. Liang lists the surrendering leaders by the titles they held among the defenders: Gao Yunguan, King Na; Wu Guiwen, King Bi; Wang Anjun, King Kang; Zhou Wenjia, King Ning; and four Heavenly Generals, Fan Qifa, Zhang Dazhou, Wang Huanwu and Wang Youwei. Four kings and four Heavenly Generals: eight men in all. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E13]

The gates were open, but the matter was not closed. More than a hundred thousand of these eight men's troops were still inside the city, a force Liang describes as menacing, pressing and full of threat. Cheng Xueqi had earlier promised the eight posts such as brigade general and deputy general, and now they asked that the agreement be honoured. The defectors had done their part: they had opened the gates and handed over a head. It was now the turn of the side that had made the promises. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E14]

Liang records Cheng Xueqi's judgment of the eight: in translation, he judged them wolf cubs at heart and feared they could not be controlled later, untameably ambitious men who might slip out of his grip. That is the reason the book gives. Cheng then plotted in secret with Li Hongzhang, and the eight were invited to a lavish banquet aboard a ship. When the signal gun sounded, the hidden soldiers rose and killed all eight together. More than a thousand of their followers who tried to resist were killed as well, and the rest of their troops surrendered. With Suzhou pacified, Li Hongzhang was rewarded with the honorary title Junior Guardian of the Heir Apparent. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E14]

Now return to Gordon. When the eight surrendered, he had been the guarantor of the pact made on Yangcheng Lake. Liang writes, in effect, that when Gordon heard Li Hongzhang had broken his word, he was enraged. Gordon wanted to kill Li Hongzhang to make him pay, and went looking for him with a pistol himself. Li Hongzhang kept out of his way and dared not return to camp. After a few days Gordon's anger slowly cooled, and he let the matter drop. Liang then comments that Li Hongzhang had cause for shame here. He lists three offences: killing men who had surrendered violated what is right; Li Hongzhang broke the agreement and went back on his word; and he deceived Gordon, wronging a friend. In Liang's view, Gordon's fury, to the point of wanting to stab him, was only to be expected. Liang allows that Li Hongzhang may have had the earlier cases of Miao Peilin and Li Shizhong in mind and acted partly out of necessity, but he holds that the episode shows Li Hongzhang's lifelong fondness for petty cleverness and small tricks. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E15][BK-LHZZ-U11-E20]

After this, the Ever-Victorious Army still fought alongside the Huai Army: Liu Mingchuan, Guo Songlin and other commanders led their troops with it against Changzhou and the surrounding area, taking Yixing and Jingxi and capturing the enemy commander Huang Jingzhong. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E19]

## Promise, Guarantee, Payment: Three Different Men

Take the episode apart and the pact had three roles. Cheng Xueqi made the promise: he and Gordon settled terms with the defectors on the lake, and it was Cheng who promised them posts such as brigade general and deputy general. Gordon guaranteed it, and the book says plainly that the defectors had no doubts because of him. The decision not to pay came from the secret plan between Cheng and Li Hongzhang. Yet the idea grew out of Cheng's judgment, while the man Gordon went hunting for was Li Hongzhang, and the book's own phrase is that Hongzhang had broken his word; in his commentary, Liang also counts breaking the agreement and deceiving Gordon among Li Hongzhang's offences. One reading is that, to Gordon, a field commander's promise spoke for his superior; once the superior agreed to kill men who had surrendered, the broken word was his. Promises may be made at the front, but whether they are kept is answered higher up. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E10][BK-LHZZ-U11-E14][BK-LHZZ-U11-E15][BK-LHZZ-U11-E20]

The second layer is the guarantor's position. The book says Gordon heard of it; this article infers from that word that he learned of it after the fact. The defectors had trusted the pact because of him; when they were killed, his word as guarantor fell with them. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E10][BK-LHZZ-U11-E15]

The third layer is Cheng Xueqi's reasoning. More than a hundred thousand troops, a menacing presence, men he judged wolf cubs at heart: what the book sets out is a calculation about controlling risk. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E14]

The costs fall into two accounts. The immediate one is plain: Suzhou was taken and Li Hongzhang received a new title. Of the longer account, the material this article draws on records that Li Hongzhang stayed out of sight for a few days, Gordon's anger subsided, and the Ever-Victorious Army went on fighting beside the Huai Army; Liang, for his part, judges in his commentary that the act was cause for shame. Their later cooperation does not prove the decision was right; it shows only that the rift did not break them apart on the spot. If honouring the promise really carried too great a risk, this article suggests two other courses: before promising, agree with the commander-in-chief on the limits of what may be offered; before going back on the promise, consult the guarantor. Both are this article's own hypotheses. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E14][BK-LHZZ-U11-E15][BK-LHZZ-U11-E19][BK-LHZZ-U11-E20]

## How Much This Account Can Tell Us

This article rests on a single book, Liang Qichao's biography of Li Hongzhang. The book centres on Li Hongzhang; neither the defectors' version nor Gordon's own account is among the evidence. The promise is also described in two ways: at the lake it was rewards of the second rank, while after the city fell the defectors asked for posts such as brigade general and deputy general. This article cannot determine how the two relate. The figures of more than a hundred thousand troops and more than a thousand followers killed are taken from this book and have not been checked against other records. [BK-LHZZ-U11-E10][BK-LHZZ-U11-E14]

## Think about it

Zhou runs his company's business in a city far from head office. To win over a distributor, he promised its owners in person: once they signed, they would get exclusive rights in the region plus a year of rebates. The distributor still hesitated, so Zhou asked Chen, a logistics partner the company had worked with for years, to vouch for the deal, and only then did the distributor sign. With the channel open, head office concluded that the distributor was too strong and would be hard to keep in line later, and decided not to grant exclusivity. The notes from that meeting still carry Chen's name.

**If you ran head office, how would you weigh the control gained by going back on the deal against the credibility of Zhou and Chen? And who should bear the consequences of this promise?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume Zhou's promise went beyond his written authority, but head office had tacitly let him negotiate; Chen received nothing for vouching.
- **What you could do**: 1. First settle whose promise it was: since head office let Zhou negotiate, it is hard to tell outsiders that this was only Zhou's personal word. 2. Then ask whether the risk can only be handled by reneging, or whether contract terms, performance thresholds or rights granted in stages could keep the distributor in check. 3. If an adjustment really is needed, go to Chen first, explain why and hear his view; then have head office itself explain to the distributor and offer alternative compensation, so that Zhou and Chen are not left to face the distributor alone.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another view: with a promise made in person and a guarantor behind it, head office should honour the deal first and use renewal terms to keep the distributor in line. The price is that head office carries the risk it is worried about; the gain is that its front-line staff and partners will still be willing to speak for the company in future.

</details>

## Sources and uncertainty

The whole article relies on a single account, Liang Qichao's biography of Li Hongzhang, and lacks any narrative from the defectors or from Gordon himself; how the two descriptions of the promise relate is unclear, and the figures cited are taken from this book and have not been checked elsewhere.
