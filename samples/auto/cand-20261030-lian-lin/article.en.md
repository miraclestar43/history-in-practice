# The Quarrel Over Rank: Lian Po's Grievance and the Bundle of Thorns

*Back from the meeting at Mianchi, Lin Xiangru was made shangqing for his service and ranked above Lian Po, a general whose merit lay in cities stormed and battles won. Lian Po would not swallow it and swore he would humiliate him wherever they met. Lin Xiangru kept giving ground, until even his own retainers threatened to walk out. The standoff ended with Lian Po baring his back and carrying a bundle of thorns to beg forgiveness; and Lian Po's grievance had had grounds from the start.*

## Who Should Give Way

Lin Xiangru came back from the meeting at Mianchi and was made shangqing, the highest ministerial rank, placed above Lian Po. Lian Po was Zhao's finest general: he had beaten Qi, taken Yangjin and won a name for courage among the states. His reaction was blunt. Lin Xiangru had earned his place with nothing but his tongue; why should he sit above me? He announced that the next time they met he would humiliate the man. What makes the episode hard is that the grievance had grounds. Lian Po really had great merit from storming cities and fighting in the field, while Lin Xiangru's credit was entered under words. Now both held the top rank at court: one meant to humiliate, the other hid. [L-E5][L-E1]

## Where the Two Men Came From

Lian Po's credentials were earned on the battlefield. In the reign of King Huiwen of Zhao he commanded against Qi, routed it, seized Yangjin, and was raised to shangqing, his courage known to every state. Lin Xiangru's origins could hardly have been more different. He was a man of Zhao, and his standing was that of a shimen - a retainer, a dependent gentleman living in the household of Miao Xian, the head of the palace eunuchs. When Lian Po later refused to accept him, one of the reasons he gave was exactly this: the man had always been of low station. [L-E1][L-E2][L-E5]

In King Huiwen's time Zhao acquired the Heshi jade from Chu, and King Zhaoxiang of Qin wrote offering fifteen cities for it. The king of Zhao consulted his great general Lian Po and the other ministers, and both answers looked bad: hand it over and the cities might never come, leaving Zhao cheated; refuse, and Qin's armies might. Nothing was settled, and there was no one fit to carry the reply to Qin. Miao Xian put forward Lin Xiangru, and told a story against himself. He had once been guilty of an offence and meant to flee to Yan. Lin Xiangru stopped him: Zhao was strong and Yan weak, and the king of Yan had courted Miao Xian only because he stood in the king of Zhao's favour; flee there, and Yan would tie him up and send him back. Better to "strip and lie on the block", as the phrase went, and ask pardon. Miao Xian did, and the king pardoned him. [L-E2][L-E10]

## How It Played Out

Before setting out, Lin Xiangru laid the logic straight. If Qin offers cities for the jade and Zhao refuses, the wrong is Zhao's; if Zhao hands over the jade and Qin refuses the cities, the wrong is Qin's. Weighing the two courses together, he said, it is better to agree and leave Qin carrying the blame. He was willing to go with the jade. [L-E11][L-E2]

At the Qin court the king received Lin Xiangru in the Zhang Terrace, was delighted with the jade, and passed it around to his ladies and attendants, who all cried long life to him. Lin Xiangru saw he had no intention of paying. He said the gem had a small flaw he would point out, took it back, and stood holding it with his eyes on a pillar, ready to smash it. The alarmed king apologised, sent for the official in charge of maps and pointed out fifteen cities for Zhao. Lin Xiangru judged this mere show. The Heshi jade was a treasure the whole world talked of, he said; the king of Zhao had fasted five days before sending it, and the king of Qin should fast five days and hold the nine-fold ceremony at court before he would dare present it. The king agreed and lodged him at the Guangcheng hostel. Sure that the king would break his word even after fasting, Lin Xiangru had a follower put on coarse clothes, hide the jade in his robe and take it home to Zhao by back roads. At the next audience he laid it out: in more than twenty reigns since Duke Mu, Qin had never kept a firm agreement; the jade was already back in Zhao; "I know that deceiving Your Majesty is a crime deserving death, and I ask to go to the cauldron." The king and his ministers looked at one another. Some attendants moved to drag him out, but the king reasoned that killing him would not bring back the jade and would end good relations with Zhao. In the end he received Lin Xiangru at court with full ceremony and sent him home. [L-E3][L-E12]

Before the meeting at Mianchi the king of Zhao, afraid of Qin, did not want to go. Lian Po and Lin Xiangru took counsel and told him that staying away would show Zhao weak and afraid. So the king went, and Lin Xiangru with him. Lian Po escorted him to the border and, taking leave, made an arrangement: allow for the road and the formalities of the meeting, and the round trip should take no more than thirty days; if the king was not back in thirty days, let the crown prince be set up as king, to cut off Qin's hopes. The king agreed. At Mianchi the king of Qin, to the end of the banquet, could not gain the upper hand over Zhao; Zhao, for its part, had massed its forces to meet Qin, and Qin dared not move. [L-E13][L-E4]

Back home, the king judged Lin Xiangru's service great and made him shangqing, ranked above Lian Po. Lian Po put it plainly: I am the general of Zhao, with great merit from storming cities and fighting in the field, while Lin Xiangru has labored only with his tongue and sits above me; and he has always been a man of low station. I am ashamed, and I cannot bear to stand beneath him. Then he declared openly: when I set eyes on Lin Xiangru, I will humiliate him. [L-E5]

Lin Xiangru, hearing of it, refused to meet him. Whenever there was a court audience he pleaded illness rather than jostle with Lian Po over precedence. When he did go out and caught sight of Lian Po in the distance, he turned his carriage aside and hid. [L-E6]

His retainers could not stomach it, and came to him together to protest: we left our kith and kin to serve you only because we admired your lofty honor. Now you sit in the same rank as Lian Po, he pours out insults, and you cringe and hide, frightened out of all proportion. Even ordinary men would be ashamed of this - let alone generals and ministers. We are not good enough for you; we ask leave to go. [L-E7]

Lin Xiangru firmly refused to let them leave, and put a question back: how does General Lian compare with the king of Qin? Not his equal, they said. With all the king of Qin's might, said Lin Xiangru, I denounced him to his face in his own court and shamed his whole circle of ministers. Incompetent as I am, would I be afraid of General Lian alone? But consider: the reason mighty Qin has not dared to send its armies against Zhao is only that the two of us are still here. Now two tigers fight in one pit; the way things stand, they cannot both live. I do this because I put the state's urgent need first and my private enmity second. [L-E8]

When Lian Po heard this, he stripped off his shirt, took up a bundle of thorns on his back, and came by way of a mutual acquaintance to Lin Xiangru's gate to apologize: a crude and lowly man, he said, who did not know how far the general's generosity went. In the end the two were reconciled and became friends who would die for each other. They did not idle afterwards. That same year Lian Po struck east against Qi and broke one of its armies. Two years on he campaigned against Qi again and took Ji. Three years after that he stormed Wei's Fangling and Anyang. Four years later Lin Xiangru himself led an army against Qi and stopped at Pingyi. The quarrel over rank never held up either man's work. [L-E9][L-E14]

## How the Way Out Was Built

Lian Po's grievance deserves a moment first. His merit was won storming cities; Lin Xiangru's, at the Qin court and at Mianchi. The two kinds of credit have different shapes and are not easily measured with one ruler, and his jibe that Lin Xiangru had always been a man of low station shows that what rankled was not only the ordering but the birth behind it. One reading is that what Lian Po cared about was not whether merit existed but whether it could be seen: a battlefield result can be counted off, whereas success with words depends on someone else retelling it. [L-E5]

Lin Xiangru's yielding was not simple endurance either. The reason he gave his retainers was not that Lian Po was beneath notice, but a judgment about the whole board: the reason mighty Qin has not dared to send its armies against Zhao is only that the two of us are still here. He shifted the dispute from whose merit was greater to how many usable men Zhao had left. And the yielding itself used offstage moves - pleading illness, turning his carriage aside - and the record notes no public reply to Lian Po. On one reading, that kept the other man from being forced to take a stand on the spot. One reading is that this was precisely the ramp built for a retreat. The insult had been spoken in the open; the undoing happened in private, so Lian Po could turn back without first withdrawing his words before an audience. [L-E8][L-E6]

Within the same biography the gesture of admitting fault appears twice. Lin Xiangru's advice to Miao Xian was to strip and lie on the block to ask pardon; when Lian Po came to the gate, he came stripped to the waist with thorns on his back. Both are postures of baring the body and handing it over. One cannot say that Lian Po knew of the earlier case or was influenced by it; the parallel is the author's observation, not something the text states. Advising the king of Zhao before the mission, Lin Xiangru used a different grammar: first settle where the wrong will lie, with Zhao or with Qin, arrange for it to fall on the other side, and only then decide how far to give. The Grand Historian's verdict: he stepped back and yielded to Lian Po, and his name came to weigh as much as Mount Tai. [L-E10][L-E9][L-E11][L-E17]

## Today

> Take an invented scene. On a company's engineering team, the veteran who has kept the core system alive for seven or eight years watches a newcomer promoted above him on the strength of a few incident recoveries and a handful of status reviews. At the weekly meeting the old engineer mutters that his rival is nothing but a talker. The newcomer hears about it and never argues with him, conceding even on scheduling. The younger members of the team feel he is being far too soft. The only difference from that ancient court is the furniture: no war chariots, only meeting minutes.

> Another invented scene. An employee has botched a project for an important client, and the first impulse is to take up a rival firm's standing offer and walk away. An older colleague stops them: that firm wants you because of the weight you carry here; leave, and the weight goes with you, and they may not keep you for long. Better to go back, own the mistake face to face, and put a plan for repairing it on the table. It is the same reasoning Lin Xiangru once gave Miao Xian, minus the axe and the block.

## Limits and Uncertainties

The uncertainty to flag: the sole historical source for this story is Sima Qian's biography of Lian Po and Lin Xiangru. The speeches there are a historian's record and need not be verbatim transcripts, and the details of the thorn apology - the bared body, the bundle of thorns, the arrival by way of guests - cannot be checked against any other material in this piece's evidence. This piece does not write Lian Po's inner thoughts for him; it records only the actions and the spoken words the text preserves. Dates stay in the source's own terms, such as in the time of King Huiwen of Zhao, rather than being converted into years before the common era. [L-E9][L-E2]

One apology did not remake Lian Po's temper. When King Xiaocheng of Zhao died, his successor King Daoxiang sent Le Cheng to replace Lian Po; Lian Po attacked Le Cheng, who fled, and then went over to Daliang in Wei. Earlier, when he had lost power, his retainers had all left, and when he was made a general again they came back; one of them told him that the world deals "the way the marketplace does": people come when you have power and leave when you do not. None of this is a moral verdict on anyone. It only shows that the apology at Lin Xiangru's gate was real, and that it did not rewrite the man from that day on. [L-E16][L-E15]

## Think about it

Two partners who have long worked together fall out after one of them says something unkind in public. The one who misspoke will not speak first, because of face; the one who was offended is waiting for an explanation. The team splits into two camps and the actual work grinds to a halt.

- **Assumptions**: The two sides still have to work together afterwards; neither can do without the other
The hurtful words were said in public, so demanding a public apology would amount to forcing a public recantation on the spot
The one who misspoke does have visible credit to his name; his grievance is not groundless
- **What you could do**: Start by finding the shared dependency: what is the hard thing the two must carry together next? Move the conversation from ranking merit to shared risk
Have a third party both sides trust arrange a private meeting, with no one required to bow in public
The offended side states first which specific remark or incident he cares about, rather than demanding a full confession
The one who misspoke apologizes for that remark while making his own basis at the time - his merit, his years - explicit, so the grievance stays on the table
Close on one small shared task, and put the relationship back to work
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another approach (raised by the author): stage no apology at all, but tie the two men to a single goal and let a shared result quietly cover the old grudge. The cost is that the remark is never dealt with, and may surface again in some other setting.

## Sources and uncertainty

This piece rests on the single chapter of Sima Qian's biography of Lian Po and Lin Xiangru. The speeches and the details of the thorn-apology cannot be cross-checked against other sources. Where the original text says nothing about a figure's psychology or motive, the piece marks its claims as one reading or as the author's inference. The juxtaposition of Miao Xian's old affair and Lian Po's apology is the author's observation only and implies no influence between them.
