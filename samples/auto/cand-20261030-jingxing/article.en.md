# At Jingxing Pass: The Plan Nobody Heeded

*A battle won against the odds has been retold for two thousand years, and most retellings remember only the winner's tricks. This one is about something else: a proposal that was rejected, and how the winner treated the man who made it after the fighting stopped.*

unscheduled · Ancient China · cand-20261030-jingxing rev 1

## Before the battle, a proposal is turned down

Han Xin and Zhang Er were marching east with tens of thousands of troops, bound to attack the state of Zhao through Jingxing. King Xie of Zhao and the Lord of Cheng'an, Chen Yu, massed their forces at the Jingxing pass under a strength they claimed at two hundred thousand. The disparity was obvious. The trouble lay elsewhere: the Han army was fighting far from home on the back of a string of victories, its grain straggling along behind. Before the two sides came to blows, someone in the Zhao camp put forward a very specific plan, and the plan was turned down. This piece is about that rejected proposal, and about how the winner treated the man who drew it up once the fighting was over. [J-E1][J-E2][J-E7]

## An edge no one can stop, and grain trailing behind

Start with what Han Xin's army was up against. In the "Account of the Marquis of Huaiyin," one chapter of Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, Li Zuoche — the Lord of Guangwu — traces the Han army's road to Zhao: Han Xin crossed the Western River, captured the King of Wei, took Xia Yue alive, and fought a bloody battle at Yuyu; with Zhang Er now beside him he means to bring down Zhao. This is an army riding its victories far from home, and "its edge cannot be withstood." But Li Zuoche keeps a second ledger in the same breath: grain hauled a thousand li leaves soldiers with hunger on their faces; you cut firewood before you can cook, and no army stays fed. On the Jingxing road two carts cannot run abreast and cavalry cannot form a column; after several hundred li, the supplies must be somewhere behind. Strength and frailty were two faces of one army. [J-E2]

His proposal followed from that: "Grant me thirty thousand picked troops and I will cut their baggage train from the side roads." Hold the main body behind deep ditches and high walls and refuse battle. Ahead the Han would have no one to fight; behind them no road home; cut off and unable to forage, and in less than ten days "the heads of the two generals will be delivered to your command tent." He closed with a warning: otherwise, he said, you will surely be taken by those two. [J-E2]

The Zhao commander-in-chief gets one line of characterization from the historian: "a Confucian, who always maintained that a righteous army does not use deceit and cunning schemes." His grounds for refusing are recorded too, in two layers. One is arithmetic: the manual says ten-to-one besieges, two-to-one fights; Han Xin claims tens of thousands but has no more than a few thousand, and a thousand-li raid has worn them to the bone. The other is reputation: to hide behind walls at such odds would mean "the lords will think me cowardly, and come lightly to attack me." He did not take the Guangwu Jun's advice. [J-E3]

## From two thousand red banners to a thousand in gold

Han Xin sent men to spy out the camp, learned the plan had not been adopted, and only when they reported back, delighted at the news, did he dare lead his army down toward Jingxing, halting thirty li short of the pass. At midnight he chose two thousand light cavalry, each man carrying a red banner, and sent them by side paths to hide in the hills and watch the Zhao camp, with orders: "When Zhao sees us withdraw, they will empty their camp to pursue us; go in fast, pull down the Zhao banners and stand up Han's red banners in their place." Everything this detachment did later hung on one premise — that the Zhao army really would come out. [J-E4]

He sent ten thousand men out first to form up with their backs to the river. The Zhao soldiers saw them from the ramparts and "roared with laughter." By the manual his generals later quoted, a proper line kept hills at the right rear and water at the left front. Ten thousand men with no retreat behind them looked like men who had never fought. [J-E5]

At dawn Han Xin raised the general's banners, beat the drums, and came out through the pass. The Zhao camp opened and attacked, and the fighting went on a long time. Then Han Xin and Zhang Er made a show of abandoning their drums and standards and fell back toward the force by the water, which opened its ranks to take them in and turned to fight again. The Zhao army duly poured out of its walls, scrambling for the Han drums and standards, chasing Han Xin and Zhang Er. The Han soldiers at the riverbank, with nowhere left to go, fought to the death and could not be broken. [J-E6]

The ambush had been waiting for exactly that. With the Zhao camp emptied, the two thousand riders swept in, pulled down the Zhao banners and raised two thousand red ones of Han. Beaten in front and unable to catch Han Xin, the Zhao troops turned for their camp and found "the walls were all hung with Han's red banners, and they were greatly alarmed," sure the Han had already taken the king of Zhao and his generals. The army dissolved; commanders cut men down to hold the line and could not. The Han struck from both sides, routed the Zhao army, killed the Lord of Cheng'an on the banks of the Zhi, and took King Xie alive. [J-E6]

Then comes the scene this piece is really about. Han Xin ordered that the Guangwu Jun, Li Zuoche, must not be killed, and set a thousand in gold on bringing him in alive. Someone delivered him bound at headquarters; Han Xin "loosed his bonds, seated him facing east, sat facing him from the west, and served him as a teacher." An army had just won, and one of the commander-in-chief's first tasks was keeping the other side's adviser alive — with a reward of a thousand in gold for it. [J-E7]

At the celebration the generals asked a question. The manual says to keep hills at the right rear and water at the left front; you ordered us to form up with our backs to the water, and said we would eat after Zhao was broken. We were not convinced — yet we won. What art is this? Han Xin's answer: it is in the manual; you simply failed to see it there. Does it not say "throw them onto death ground and they will live; place them where destruction is certain and they will survive"? The men I have are not old followers I have cared for over the years; this is what they call "driving men of the marketplace to fight." The situation demanded putting them on death ground, so that every man fought for his own life; give them ground to live in and they would all run, and then what use would they be? The generals conceded the point. [J-E8]

Han Xin then asked Li Zuoche: I mean to strike Yan in the north and Qi in the east — how do I make that work? Li Zuoche declined: a defeated general cannot speak of courage, a minister of a ruined state cannot plan survival; what am I, a captured and ruined man, to weigh great matters with? Han Xin offered the story of Baili Xi, who saw Yu fall while he served Yu and saw Qin rise to hegemony while he served Qin: "not that he was foolish at Yu and wise at Qin, but that his advice was used or not used, heeded or not heeded." And had the Lord of Cheng'an heeded your plan, a man like me would long ago have been your prisoner. It is because he did not use you that I sit here asking. [J-E9]

Li Zuoche began: "the wise man, in a thousand considerations, must have one failure; the fool, in a thousand considerations, must have one success" — hence the saying, the sage still picks through a madman's words. A man whose state had just fallen, taken alive, opened by reminding everyone that anyone can be wrong. [J-E10]

## Why the plan was refused, and how the win gets explained

Split Chen Yu's two grounds apart and they are not the same kind of thing. "Claims tens of thousands, really no more than a few thousand," "a thousand-li raid has worn them to the bone" — those are readings of the enemy, contestable in principle. "Refuse battle and the lords will think me cowardly" is not a reading of the Han army at all; it is a reading of his own image. And "a righteous army does not use deceit and cunning schemes" is not a judgment at all — it is a rule. One explanation: the rule had already struck half the options from the table, so Li Zuoche's plan may have been out of the running before the discussion opened. The author's inference is that this is why the text stamps Chen Yu "a Confucian": the historian is recording a stance, not merely a mistake. [J-E3]

Second detail worth holding onto: Han Xin did not march in on confidence. He advanced only after "sending men to look in on them and learning the plan had not been used." The rejected proposal was, in other words, one of the premises of his battle. The two thousand riders' success depended on the Zhao army emptying its camp to chase; the ten thousand men with the river at their backs were a separate arrangement. The author's inference is that this condition held only because Li Zuoche's plan had been shelved. One explanation: intelligence carried as much weight in this victory as formation. Han Xin first confirmed that his opponent would not listen to sound advice, then placed himself on the field. [J-E4][J-E8]

There is also an easy-to-miss asymmetry. After the battle the generals asked why the backs-to-the-water formation — a question about what made the winner's move clever. The text does not record anyone asking why that plan had not been adopted before the fighting. The author's inference: after-action review naturally spotlights the winner's technique, because technique can be learned, while a veto looks like it takes no skill and yields no result. Yet in this battle the decisive acts happened before the first blow fell — a refusal, and the discovery of that refusal. [J-E8][J-E4]

Han Xin put the conclusion more bluntly than anyone: it turned on "whether advice is used or not used, heeded or not heeded," and had the Lord of Cheng'an listened to you, I would be the captive. To be clear, that is Han Xin's own account after the event, not a fact anyone can check; battles are not run twice. The alternative the author raises: had Chen Yu adopted the plan, held his walls and cut the supplies, what Han Xin would have done instead is unrecorded and can only be guessed at. What can be established is that his deployment depended on the Zhao army coming out to chase; how directly that depended on the plan's rejection is the author's inference. [J-E9]

Having won the bid, the client hires as consultant precisely the person on the losing side whose plan was voted down — not to humiliate him, but to treat his thinking as coursework. Untying the bonds and serving Li Zuoche as a teacher is exactly that: the person most worth asking is often not your own adviser, but the one on the other side who went unheard.

## How far this piece can be certain

Every fact here comes from one text, Sima Qian's "Account of the Marquis of Huaiyin"; there is no second independent source to cross-check against. Li Zuoche's supply arithmetic, Chen Yu's reasons, the generals' questions, Li Zuoche's modesty — all of it is a single author's record. The speeches are the historian's account and need not be verbatim transcripts; where this piece uses the original wording, it takes only phrasing actually present in the text.

Two things must stay open. First, "had the Lord of Cheng'an heeded your plan, a man like me would long ago have been taken" is Han Xin's own claim after the event, untestable; this piece records it as his explanation of himself, not as fact. Second, where Chen Yu's mind is concerned, this piece follows exactly the reasons the text gives and adds nothing; what would have happened had he adopted the plan is an analytical counterfactual the author offers, not a record, and it will never have an answer. [J-E9]

## Think about it

At a project review, someone proposes pausing expansion to reinforce the rear — the equivalent of "cut the baggage train, hold the walls, refuse battle." The person in charge turns it down on two grounds: first, "doing that will make rivals think we are afraid"; second, "we play straight; we don't do clever tricks."

**When the main grounds for rejecting a proposal are "adopting it would make us look weak" or "that is not how we do things," how do you tell which words are judgments about the situation and which are declarations of identity? And if you want such a proposal to get at least one serious test, what would you change before the meeting?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Both sides hold roughly the same public information
The person rejecting the proposal has final decision authority
No third party will settle in the short term who is right
- **What you could do**: Split the grounds for rejection into two columns — does it work, and does it look right — and mark what each ground rests on
Ask the proposer for the shortest verification path: within a few days, what signal would show the proposal really does not hold up?
Put the rejection on record, noting the reasons and expectations at the time, and agree on a date to look back at it
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another approach, proposed by the author: set the merits of the proposal aside first and buy information instead — as Han Xin "sent men to look in on them," confirm whether the other side will actually take that route before deciding your own advance or retreat.

</details>
