# Rising or Setting: The Final Address Before Signing, September 17, 1787

*A constitution with known flaws lay on the table: sign it or not, and how? On the last day of the Constitutional Convention: a deliberately ambiguous signing formula, several readings of what a signature meant, three refusals, and a sun painted on the back of the presiding officer's chair.*

## Last Day: Draft and Signatures

September 17, 1787, the last day of the Constitutional Convention. The engrossed Constitution had just been read aloud when Benjamin Franklin rose with a speech in his hand; Madison's notes say he had written it out for his own convenience, and James Wilson read it to the hall. The meeting was about to dissolve. Many in the room knew the document on the table had flaws. One question was left: sign it or not, and how. [F-E1]

He opened with plain talk. There were several parts of this Constitution, he said, which "I do not at present approve", but he was not sure he would never approve them. He had lived long enough to change his mind again and again, even on important questions he had once thought settled, once better information or fuller consideration came along. So he confessed: "the older I grow, the more apt I am to doubt my own judgment." A man who stood up in public to admit he might be wrong was about to put his name to a document. [F-E2]

## Why Sign Anyway

His reason for consenting was down-to-earth: "I think a general government necessary for us." He would take the charter with all its faults, if they were such. By now the argument was not about whether the text was good, but about whether anything better lay within reach. [F-E3]

Nor was he sold on the escape route of calling another convention to write a better charter. Assemble a group of men for the advantage of their joint wisdom, he said, and you inevitably assemble with them all their prejudices, passions, errors of opinion, local interests and selfish views. "From such an assembly can a perfect production be expected?" [F-E4]

Plenty of others had reservations of their own. So the day's real dispute came into view: what did the act of signing actually mean? [F-E15][F-E16]

## The Argument Over Signing

The hardest passage of the speech is about silence. Franklin said the opinions he had held of the Constitution's errors he sacrificed to the public good, and had never whispered a syllable of them abroad: "Within these walls they were born, and here they shall die." What was said in the chamber stayed in the chamber. [F-E5]

Then came the request. To any member who still objected he asked one small thing: join him in a readiness to "doubt a little of his own infallibility", and put his name to the instrument, "to make manifest our unanimity". Note the ask — not that everyone praise the document, but that every name appear on the same page. [F-E6]

He then moved that members sign, and offered a convenient form: "Done in Convention by the unanimous consent of the states present, the 17th of September, &c. In witness whereof, we have hereunto subscribed our names." Madison adds a backstage note: this ambiguous form had been drawn up by Gouverneur Morris in order to gain the dissenting members, and put into Franklin's hands so that it might have the better chance of success. [F-E7]

In the same closing stretch the convention was still adjusting a number. Gorham asked, if it was not too late, for the purpose of lessening objections to the Constitution, to reconsider the clause capping representation at one member for every forty thousand people — a clause that had produced so much discussion — and to strike out forty thousand in favour of thirty thousand. That would fix no absolute rule, he remarked, only give Congress greater latitude. When the president rose to put the question — the signature block names him George Washington, deputy from Virginia — he said his office had restrained him from speaking on questions before the House, yet he could not forbear this wish. Many members had thought the small proportion of representatives thin security for the rights and interests of the people; he acknowledged it had always appeared to himself among the exceptionable parts of the plan. The change passed without opposition, and the engrossed Constitution was agreed to for signing, every state answering aye. [F-E12][F-E13][F-E14][F-E24]

Randolph rose and, alluding to Franklin's observations, apologized for refusing to sign, notwithstanding the vast majority and venerable names that would give the plan their sanction. Refusing, he said, did not mean he would oppose the Constitution outside the convention's doors: "He meant only to keep himself free to be governed by his duty, as it should be prescribed by his future judgment." [F-E8][F-E19]

Then several members explained why they would sign. Morris said he had objections, but this was the best that was to be attained, and he would take it with all its faults; the majority had decided and he would abide by that determination. The moment the plan went forth, every other consideration would be laid aside and the great question would be whether there was to be a national government at all — with anarchy as the alternative. The signing in the proposed form, he pointed out, related only to the fact that the states present were unanimous. Hamilton urged that every member sign: a few characters of consequence, by opposing or even refusing, might do infinite mischief, kindling the latent sparks that lurked under an enthusiasm for the convention which might soon subside — and that between anarchy and convulsions and the good the plan might bring there was nothing to hesitate over. Blount said he had declared he would not sign so as to pledge himself to support the plan, but the form relieved him: without committing himself, he could attest the fact that the plan was the unanimous act of the states in convention. [F-E15][F-E16][F-E17]

Franklin explained that when drawing up that paper he had not known that any particular member would refuse to sign, and hoped to be so understood. He felt a high sense of obligation to Randolph for bringing the plan forward in the first instance and for his help along the way, and hoped he would yet lay aside his objections and prevent the great mischief that the refusal of his name might produce. Randolph was not moved. To sign in the proposed form was, for him, the same as signing the Constitution; the change of form made no difference. Refusing might be the most awful step of his life, but conscience dictated it, and he could not hesitate, much less change. He added that holding out the plan to the people with a final alternative of accepting or rejecting it in toto would really produce the anarchy and civil convulsions that were apprehended. [F-E18][F-E19]

Gerry then described the painful feelings of his situation and his fear that the present crisis might end in civil war — in Massachusetts especially, where one party was devoted to democracy, which he thought the worst of all political evils, and the other as violent at the opposite extreme. The form meant nothing to him either. But if it were not otherwise apparent, the refusals to sign should never be known from him. As for Franklin's remarks, he said, he could only view them as levelled at himself and the other gentlemen who meant not to sign. [F-E20]

Others found the form too coy. Pinckney thought ambiguity would win few converts; better to be candid and let the form speak the substance. He meant to sign with a view to supporting the Constitution with all his influence, and would pledge himself accordingly. Franklin's reply was light in tone but decisive: "It is too soon to pledge ourselves, before Congress and our constituents shall have approved the plan." Ingersoll offered another reading: the signature was neither a bare attestation of the fact nor a pledge to support the plan at all events, but "a recommendation of what, all things considered, was the most eligible". [F-E21]

On Franklin's motion for the signing form, ten states voted ay: New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina and Georgia. South Carolina was divided. [F-E22]

In the end every member signed except Randolph, Mason and Gerry, who declined to give the Constitution the sanction of their names; the convention then dissolved itself by an adjournment sine die, without naming a day to return. While the last members were signing, Franklin looked toward the president's chair, at the back of which a sun happened to be painted, and remarked to the few members near him that painters had found it difficult in their art to tell a rising sun from a setting one. "I have," said he, "often and often, in the course of the session, and the vicissitudes of my hopes and fears as to its issue, looked at that behind the president, without being able to tell whether it was rising or setting; but now, at length, I have the happiness to know that it is a rising, and not a setting sun." [F-E9][F-E10]

## One Signature, Several Readings

Almost the whole day's argument circles a single act: writing a name at the foot of a page. On one reading, Morris's form split signing into two questions — what do you attest, and what do you commit to? Morris and Blount acknowledged only the fact that the states present were unanimous; Ingersoll read the signature as a recommendation; Pinckney held that it ought to be a commitment; Randolph thought the two inseparable, a signature however softly worded being still a signature. The author's inference: precisely because the act meant different things to different men could a majority stand beneath one row of names. The price was that the meaning of that row would need explaining ever after. [F-E7][F-E15][F-E17][F-E19][F-E21]

A second reading turns on Franklin's plea to "doubt a little of his own infallibility". The author's extension, not Franklin's words: what he offered may have been less a reason to call the plan right than a posture for signing under uncertainty. When another convention might well do no better, first admit you may be wrong, then put your name down. The author's alternative would be to require a written reservation beside each signature, but the author also grants that this would weaken the unanimity that some in the room wanted most that day. [F-E4][F-E6]

## Signing Moments Today

> Fictional modern scene one. A review committee has just turned down a plan that one colleague pushed hard for. Afterwards a member tells someone in the corridor that they had disagreed too and were simply outvoted. Franklin's line, "Within these walls they were born, and here they shall die," points to another way: argue the objection fully in the room and get it into the minutes, then stop treating a settled disagreement as gossip once you are outside. That is not hiding the outcome; it is declining to fight the same battle again in the hallway.

> Or take a joint statement to be published, where you agree with most of its positions and object to the wording of one passage. One person tells you not to fuss; another warns that signing means endorsing all of it. What helps here is neither courage nor tact, but a distinction: is the signature attesting a fact, recommending a choice, or committing you?

## What We Can Know

The meeting is known chiefly from James Madison's notes, printed in Elliot's 1845 edition (Wikisource), a participant's record. The 1793 Works of Franklin carries a printed text of the speech under the title "Final Speech of Dr. Franklin in the late Federal Convention", which can be set beside it, but this piece has not collated the two word by word. All English quotations in the body of the piece are taken from Madison's notes; whether the wording of Franklin's speech differs between the two versions is not asserted. [F-E11]

The record's own provenance deserves a look. In these notes King suggests that the journals be destroyed or left in the president's custody, since made public they would be put to bad use by those wishing to prevent the Constitution's adoption; Wilson prefers the second course — false suggestions may be spread, and it should not be made impossible to contradict them — and the papers are left with the president, subject to the order of Congress, if ever formed under the Constitution. As for Franklin and the sun, the notes record that he observed to a few members near him: an aside to those beside him, not a formal address to the whole hall. In any case, this piece stops at the adjournment of 17 September 1787 and touches neither ratification nor amendment. [F-E23][F-E10]

## Think about it

At the meeting that finalizes a department merger plan, you are one of those voting: the plan contains a provision you clearly oppose, but it must be settled today and announced tomorrow, and the chair asks you to sign the resolution.

**In that setting, what would your signature mean — attesting that the plan has passed by a majority, recommending that this is the best available choice, or committing you to endorse every clause? Under which reading would you sign, and under which would you rather not?**

- **Assumptions**: The plan must be finalized today; the cost of delay falls on everyone
Your objection is on the record of the meeting but was not adopted
Others will read your signature as full-throated endorsement
- **What you could do**: First establish what a signature conventionally means on this occasion, separating attestation from commitment
Write your reservation into the minutes so that it is filed alongside the signature
When you sign, state in one sentence which meaning you intend, so it cannot be over-read afterwards
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's proposal: request a short reservation beside the signature, such as attesting only that the plan passed due process and not endorsing every clause. It shares something with the ambiguous form of 1787 but is not the same device; and the author adds a caution that it weakens the effect of unanimity, which has to be weighed.
