# Only Poetry Was Left: How an Argument Turned into Hu Shi's Book of Experiments

*Between 1915 and 1917, a Chinese student in America who called himself a conservative — who thought language and writing the world's most unchanging things — was, by his own account, pushed by a few doggerel verses and his friends' opposition into writing poetry in the spoken language alone; earlier, a leaflet had first moved him to want to do something about reforming the written language. He worded his claim modestly; Chen Duxiu, editor of New Youth, raised the banner for him, into "revolution."*

## Only One Left

A group of Chinese friends studying in America argued for more than a year over how Chinese literature should change. In July 1916 a storm on a lake that nearly capsized their boat, and a friend's poem about it written in archaic words, set off a fresh round of quarrelling, and the dispute finally narrowed to one question: could poetry be written in baihua, the everyday spoken language? On one side, arguing that the vernacular could indeed be used for poetry, stood Hu Shi, a Chinese student who had studied at Cornell. He called himself a conservative: language and writing, he liked to say, are the most conservative things in the world, more conservative than religion. He also said he had been pushed down this road: bi shang Liangshan, driven to Liangshan, an idiom for someone forced by circumstances into extraordinary acts against his own intentions. [HS-E1][HS-E2][HS-E5][HS-E16][HS-E20][HS-E21]

His chief opponents were two friends: Mei Guangdi, bound for Harvard and his most determined opponent, and Ren Hongjun, also at Cornell, who had mocked him in verse. The quarrel was over whether the vernacular could serve literature. By the summer of 1916 his friends granted that it would do for fiction and speeches, but not for poetry. Poetry was the last fortress. Hu's tactic was first to set out his friends' objections in order. First, no poet had ever written verse in colloquial words; he himself could find only one poem, perhaps only a few lines of one, written in the vernacular. Second, and more persuasive, no Chinese writer had ever seriously written poetry in the vernacular. In his own telling, all the serious discussion grew out of a small accident set off by a farewell poem; what made him resolve to stop writing old-style verse and write only in the living language was another small accident, the incident on the lake. [HS-E8][HS-E10][HS-E15][HS-E17][HS-E19][HS-E20][HS-E38]

## It Began with a Leaflet

There was an earlier prelude. A clerk keen on reform used to slip leaflets into the envelopes that carried the students' monthly scholarship checks; one slogan, in translation, was to abolish the Chinese characters and switch to an alphabet. Around 1915 (Hu said he thought it was then), Hu wrote back a rude little note telling the man, in translation, that he had better shut his beak. [HS-E3][HS-E4]

Once the note was sent he regretted it: the man was kind and sincere about reform, and the rudeness was uncalled for. He reproached himself in front of friends and said that if men like him were not qualified to discuss the question, the qualified should do the work. So he agreed with a fellow student that each would write a paper on the problem of the Chinese script for the annual meeting of a Chinese students' association. Hu's topic was how to make the classical written language easier to teach. [HS-E5][HS-E6]

Hu's paper called the classical written language — in our translation — a half-dead language. Some words live, some have died: gou, "dog," is still in use; quan, the old word for the same animal, is not. "Ride a horse" is everyday speech; the classical equivalent is a dead phrase. So the teaching of a living language and of a half-dead one must be very different business. Looking back, he said his doctrine — vernacular alive, classical half-dead — had begun in this period. [HS-E7][HS-E26]

## A Year and More of Argument

It began with a farewell poem. When Hu was preparing to leave Cornell, Mei Guangdi was about to go on to Harvard. Hu sent him off with a doggerel poem, long by Chinese standards, stuffed with eleven foreign names. Ren Hongjun, also at Cornell, strung the names into a mock verse of his own, which in our translation runs: literature's revolution today, a poem to send off Hu. Hu would not let it pass. His send-off had been meant in earnest. It urged his friends to watch where Chinese literature went next, and it ranked a revolution in Chinese literature with conquering nature, which he called, in our translation, shrinking the earth and taming the heavens. So on the train to New York he wrote an answer, and in it first set down the idea of a revolution in the poetry world. His couplet, in our translation, asks where that revolution should begin, and answers that poetry must be written like prose. [HS-E8][HS-E10][HS-E11]

Thus the slogan — write poetry like prose — started a fight. Mei and Ren both weighed in. Poetry and prose, Mei held, could never be mixed: poetic diction and prose diction had parted ways since each began, in China and the West alike. The debate never stopped, and the further it ran the more it dragged in. As Hu put it, the more his friends argued the more conservative they grew; the more he argued, the more radical. [HS-E11]

The turn came in February and March of 1916. Hu reached a general idea: the history of Chinese literature is a history of changing literary tools, one replacing another, and at the same time a history of living literature gradually displacing dead literature. When a tool loses its vitality and hardens it must be changed, and taking up a living tool in that succession is what he called literary revolution. In March he wrote a long letter to Mei, then at Harvard. After the medieval period, he wrote, the old language tool could no longer carry people's thoughts. The change had begun in the Tang, when prose style was revolutionized and a vernacular recorded-sayings style grew up. Monks took it up first, then Song philosophers used it for their discussions, and then ordinary people used it for songs, plays, stories and novels. Vernacular literature of that kind, Yuan drama most obviously, was, he said, the climax of a literary revolution the masses completed without knowing it; the revival of Chinese literature owed much to the rise of vernacular drama and fiction. To Hu's surprise, this conclusion drawn from historical analysis was gladly accepted by his conservative friend. One note: they then took the great traditional novels for Yuan works. Hu corrected himself in later years, saying that stories like Water Margin circulated for centuries before being written down in final form under the Ming. [HS-E12][HS-E13][HS-E33]

Then the diary. On July 6, 1916, he set down nine propositions. By his account, that summer he had passed through Ithaca on the way out and back, and had discussed many Chinese questions, literature above all, with old friends spending the summer there: Ren Hongjun, Tang Yue and Yang Quan. The nine were items from those discussions that he recorded, and they also summed up his own views quite precisely. The first two, in translation: the classical written language of today is a half-dead form of writing; the vernacular of today is a living language. These nine, he said, expressed precisely the central ideas behind his launching of China's literary revolution — and all these serious discussions and historical analyses came out of one small accident, a farewell poem to a friend going to Harvard. [HS-E14][HS-E15][HS-E39]

Two days later came a close call on Cayuga Lake. On July 8 Ren Hongjun, Mei Guangdi and several other friends were rowing on the lake when a black cloud gathered overhead, a storm broke and the rain fell. Pulling for shore in a hurry they nearly capsized, and all came away soaked and dishevelled. For that band of Chinese students it was a real adventure, and Ren wrote it up in Fanhu jishi (Boating on the Lake), an old four-character-style poem he mailed to Hu for criticism. It used archaic words such as the particle yan, of the kind found in the Book of Songs. Hu had earlier written an essay on that very word, and he disliked dead diction mixed with modern language. He wrote back that the poem was bad because its wording was thoroughly incongruous, and he spoke of dead language of three thousand years ago (both paraphrased from the Chinese). Ren was indignant. More interesting was the letter that followed: Mei, riding to the rescue in high dudgeon. Mei was under the influence of his conservative teacher, a literary critic, and he opposed all of Hu's radicalism to the end. Language becomes poetic, he held, only after first-rate poets refine it, and the language of literature, poetry above all, must be beautified by first-rate poets and artists before it counts as poetic diction. On that ground he lashed Hu's ideas. [HS-E16][HS-E17]

Hu answered with a comic doggerel poem of about a hundred lines, the longest he ever wrote. It opened with Old Mei in a towering rage, piled up examples to mock the theory that words have no life or death, and turned Mei's own argument against him: if words must be forged by poets over long practice, that is exactly what he wanted. Contemporary poets and writers should take these vulgar, colloquial words, temper them, raise their standing and bring them into poetry. The burlesque threw both friends into a fury, and both wrote back to mock his attempt at vernacular verse. Yet Hu found a common line in their letters: the liveliness of the vernacular could serve storytelling, tales and novels, but not poetry. Mei excluded more: the vernacular was usable in fiction and folk songs, but in high prose, and certainly in poetry, it must never be used. Ren was bluntest; his letter said, in translation, that the vernacular has its own uses, such as novels and speeches, but cannot be used for poetry. As Hu summed it up, his friends had given way everywhere else; only poetry was still in dispute. [HS-E18][HS-E19]

And so the dispute narrowed to a point: the vernacular for every department of literature except poetry. Hu ranked the objections. First, no poet had written in vulgar words and everyday speech — in Chinese poetry he could find one poem, perhaps a few lines of one; the scarcity of vernacular poems gave his friends their confident case. Second, and the more persuasive for his friends, no Chinese writer had seriously tried: a few vernacular lines in a fit of high spirits, no more, and beneath that a half-conscious contempt. To convince them there was one course only — set an example, seriously try to write vernacular poetry. Nothing else would do. [HS-E20]

In late July he wrote to these friends that the vernacular could be poetry: little had been written before, but more could be written now, in every colour and shape, and the urgent task was to experiment in earnest. On July 26 he wrote Ren — paraphrased here from the Chinese — that words are the instrument of literature, that the classical language could never be the sharp instrument China's future literature needed, and that Shi Nai'an and Cao Xueqin had proved in practice that the instrument for fiction is the vernacular; what remained was for someone to test in practice whether it could be the instrument of verse. He likened his practice in vernacular poetry and prose to opening up a new literary colony; it was a pity he had to go alone, with few companions. The letter also allowed for failure: if the new land proved to be sand and barren ground, he might end his days in the kingdom of classical poetry after all; if by luck he succeeded, after clearing the brambles he would open the gates and welcome his friends in. In late July and early August he decided to stop writing old-style verse — and before he had written more than a handful of vernacular poems, he had named the next collection Experiments. [HS-E21][HS-E22]

In November 1916 he shaped more than a year of argument into an article with eight very moderate suggestions. Even the title was modest: he dropped "literary revolution," a phrase that had come up often in his own poems and letters and in his friends' letters to him, sometimes in jest and sometimes in earnest. This was the first time the conclusions would be sent home to China for publication. By his own later account, it was in view of the undoubted resistance of the older conservatives that he kept the wording gentle: reform, not revolution; a humble opinion, not dogma. He made three copies. One went to the Chinese Students' Quarterly he edited in America, then printed by the Commercial Press. Another went to New Youth, which Chen Duxiu, from 1916 dean of arts at National Peking University, had been editing for several years. [HS-E23][HS-E24]

It appeared in New Youth in January 1917 and stirred a great response. Two Peking University professors took it seriously. One was Chen Duxiu himself, an unbending revolutionary. He answered with "On Literary Revolution," publicly backing a literary revolution and saying frankly that the banner had been raised by his friend Hu Shi; he himself was raising the banner high in his friend's support. The other, a professor of classical phonology and a master of ancient prose, wrote no article but sent Chen and Hu long letters of small criticisms and great praise, also printed in New Youth; that so eminent a classicist should sympathize buoyed their cause. Zhao Yuanren, Hu's fellow student at Cornell and his partner in the earlier pair of papers, often said, in our translation, that Hu's vernacular poems and essays were not vernacular enough, not the genuine spoken style ordinary people used. Hu admitted it. He compared himself to a woman who had bound her feet for forty, or even twenty, years: however much she loosened the bindings, she would never recover natural feet. [HS-E25][HS-E34][HS-E26][HS-E32][HS-E5][HS-E6]

## Why an Experiment

Hu's own explanation of how it all happened is that he opposed single-cause theories of history. He used to joke that accidents often matter more than any monistic cause, such as economics, lust or God: those single causes explain everything that has ever happened, and precisely because they explain everything, they explain nothing. Of this episode he said that these events finally pushed him toward the radical course of abandoning the dead classical language for the living vernacular, and that a chain of small accidents had gradually forced him into his position. He explicitly called the farewell poem a small accident, and he also called the Cayuga Lake incident a small accident that set off a chain reaction. Counting the leaflet among the events he refers to is this article's reading of what he meant. Reading a chain of small accidents as the push behind his position is Hu's own view of history, not a finding of this article. [HS-E9][HS-E10][HS-E38]

The author's inference: his decision to lead by example was not merely a pose; it converted a disagreement into a testable question. The claim that the vernacular cannot be used for poetry is a universal one, and one decent vernacular poem is enough in principle to shake it; without a poem, letters could go on forever as a standoff of opinions. By naming the collection Experiments in advance, he took on the burden of proof in advance and left the outcome to a long, open trial that others could watch. One reading is that this matches the translator-editor Tang Degang's portrait of him as a pragmatist standing between necessity and chance, unwilling to let events drift and determined to search out the right direction through experiment. Still, Tang's remark — in paraphrase, that without Hu Shi there would probably have been no Vernacular Movement, or at least it would not have happened then — is commentary, not verifiable history. [HS-E20][HS-E21][HS-E22][HS-E27]

A second reading concerns the division of labour over words. He swapped revolution for reform and demoted his conclusion to a humble opinion, taking on himself the risk of publishing moderately; Chen Duxiu then raised it as literary revolution, and a more radical man carried the flag. By Hu's report, Chen said frankly that the banner was Hu's and that he himself was raising it high in support. This doubling — one man lowering the volume, one raising it — is a possibility the author reads in the sequence; it is this article's inference. [HS-E23][HS-E25]

The outcome can be set down too. By Hu's account, in only about four years the effort to replace the classical language with the vernacular in the schools had all but succeeded: in 1920 the Ministry of Education of the Beijing government ordered that all first- and second-year textbooks in the national primary schools be written entirely in the vernacular, with the old textbooks abolished in stages. He said the struggle had won at least a partial victory, the important part being the vernacular as the instrument of education. He also said the student movement of 1919 had been an interruption to the Renaissance movement, turning a cultural movement into a political one, though it did much to spread the vernacular. Yet he said he still felt dissatisfied: in forty years the vernacular had never become a complete instrument of education and literature. He blamed this chiefly on the conservatism of those in power and on textbooks that mixed the classical and the vernacular. This is his own judgment. [HS-E30][HS-E37]

## How to Use This Today

> Suppose you are on a product team of ten or twelve and want to turn the weekly report from a long document into a few bullet points. A senior colleague insists the formal document must be written out in full. Two months of argument, and nobody has moved. What you can borrow from Hu is the order of operations. First find the one place the other side will not give up — there is usually only one, perhaps the section that goes to customers — and shrink the dispute to that point. Then run a small, public experiment: produce two versions of the same content in the two formats, let the people who actually read it choose, and agree beforehand on what counts as evidence. The trial has to be visible to both sides, or the argument slides back into two monologues.

> The second thing is wording. You call your plan an optimization while meaning to replace the whole process; someone else renames it a process revolution and copies the whole company. Do you accept the label? Hu's own article used moderate words; it was Chen Duxiu who later raised it as a revolution. Taking the ringing phrase buys attention; the cost is losing control of the definition, and the middle-of-the-road people drawn in by gentle wording may be scared off by a radical name. This is the trade-off the author offers; it is not the only way.

## The Limits of the Material

The material here comes from one book only: Hu Shi's oral memoir, translated and annotated by Tang Degang, The Oral Memoir of Hu Shi (Chinese Literature and History Press, 2020). As Tang explains, the English draft of this chapter was abridged from the Bi shang Liangshan (Driven to Liangshan) chapter of Hu's Forty Self-Narratives, revised in Hu's own hand, then translated back into Chinese — and it differs from the original in some degree. This is therefore a participant's account given many years later, not an independent record from the time. Mei Guangdi's and Ren Hongjun's reactions and their words all reach us through Hu's retelling, and this piece does not speculate about their inner thoughts. [HS-E35][HS-E16][HS-E19]

Why he used reform rather than revolution is his own retrospective explanation. Tang Degang's remarks are the annotator's commentary: he thought the slogan that the classical language is a half-dead form of writing had a devastating force in its day, but that its truth is worth debating again; and he judged vernacular prose a hundred per cent successful, while vernacular poetry was not necessarily so, and at least had not yet left the experimental stage. [HS-E23][HS-E36][HS-E29]

Tang also records that Hu's opponents felt he claimed too much credit; when Hu gave this account in a 1956 lecture at Harvard, Jerome B. Grieder, later his biographer, simply scoffed at it. [HS-E28]

## Think about it

A fictional scenario offered by the author: a young designer at a company wants to convince their parents that going independent as a designer would pay the bills. The parents' answer is that the field is unstable, and other people's success is other people's business.

**When the other side admits that something works everywhere except in your line of work, do you keep piling up other people's success stories, or do you first run a small experiment both sides can watch? And if you run one, what should count as the criterion of judgment?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The parents' real worry is unstable income, not design as a field. Within three to six months it is possible to win commissions that can be shown and recorded as income. Both sides are willing to judge on publicly checkable results instead of relitigating on every phone call.
- **What you could do**: First, shrink the argument to one point: whether paid commissions can be won consistently, not whether the industry is any good. Second, try it on the side and keep one sheet recording commissions, income and rejected drafts. Third, agree in advance on the criteria and the deadline; when the date arrives, look at the record together and decide whether to scale up or stop.
- **Another reasonable choice**: An alternative the author suggests: skip the experiment and collect more stories of successful peers instead — the cost is that the other side can always say that is someone else, and the argument never converges. Or quit and go all in at once, where the cost is treating a single shot at success as the only evidence, with nothing to fall back on if it fails.

</details>

## Sources and uncertainty

This piece rests on a single source: Hu Shi's oral memoir in later life, itself abridged, revised in his own hand and translated back. Mei Guangdi's and Ren Hongjun's words and deeds reach us only through Hu's retelling, and Hu's explanations of motive are retrospective. Tang Degang's evaluations and inferences are commentary; the events he records (such as Grieder's reaction to Hu's 1956 Harvard lecture) are his testimony, not checked here. Paragraphs marked interpretation are the author's inferences, and paragraphs marked modern are scenarios invented for readers today.
