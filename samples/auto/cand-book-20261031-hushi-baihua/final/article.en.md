# Only Poetry Was Left: How an Argument Turned into Hu Shi's Book of Experiments

*In the summer of 1916 a few Chinese students in America had argued down to one last disagreement: could poetry be written in the everyday spoken language? Hu Shi concluded that the only way was to try it himself.*

unscheduled · Modern China · cand-book-20261031-hushi-baihua rev 2

## A Letter from the Lake

In July 1916 Hu Shi, then a Chinese student in the United States, received a letter from Ithaca. Some of his Chinese friends, it said — among them Ren Hongjun and Mei Guangdi — had been out rowing on Cayuga Lake when a black cloud gathered, a storm blew up and the rain came down. Rowing hard for shore, they nearly capsized and came back soaked to the skin. Hu was not in the boat. Ren had turned the adventure into an old-style poem in four-character lines, Boating on the Lake, and enclosed it, asking Hu for his criticism. [HS-E16][HS-E38]

Hu wrote back that the poem was bad. It mixed archaic words of the kind found in the ancient Book of Songs with modern language, so that, in his words (translated here), its diction did not hang together; he even spoke of dead words from three thousand years ago. The remark stung two friends and reopened an argument more than a year old, one that kept narrowing. By that summer, as Hu saw it, his friends granted that the spoken language would do for novels and the like — but not for poetry. Could poetry be written in baihua, the plain spoken language? Hu's next step was not to rely on argument alone: he decided to try it himself. [HS-E16][HS-E19][HS-E20]

## The Two Sides

Hu, the man arguing that it could, called himself a conservative. He used to tell his friends that language and writing are the most conservative things in the world, more conservative than religion. His most determined opponent was Mei Guangdi, then studying at Harvard under a conservative literary critic; it was Mei's fierce opposition, Hu later said, that drove him to Liangshan, the Chinese saying for being forced into a rebel's course against one's will. The other was Ren Hongjun, a friend at Cornell who once teased Hu in comic verse, and who had written the poem about the lake. [HS-E1][HS-E2][HS-E8][HS-E10][HS-E16][HS-E17]

Hu's serious interest in the written language began with a leaflet. The envelopes that carried Chinese students' monthly scholarship checks often held leaflets slipped in by a reform-minded clerk, with slogans such as do not marry before twenty-five and abolish the Chinese characters for an alphabet. Most went straight into the wastebasket. Around 1915 Hu instead wrote a rude note telling the man, in effect, to shut his beak. He soon regretted it: the man was kind, and those qualified, Hu felt, should study the problem themselves. He wrote a paper for a Chinese students' conference arguing that the classical written language was half dead. Gou, dog, is still in use, he pointed out; quan, the classical word for it, is not. The everyday phrase for riding a horse lives on; the classical one has died. [HS-E3][HS-E4][HS-E5][HS-E7]

## From a Farewell Poem to Experiments

The quarrel itself had begun with a farewell poem. As Hu was preparing to leave Cornell, Mei was about to go to Harvard. Hu saw him off with a long comic poem stuffed with eleven foreign names, urging his friends to watch where Chinese literature would go next and declaring that working for a revolution in Chinese literature was no less a contribution than conquering nature. Ren strung the foreign names into a mock poem of his own about literary revolution and sending off Hu. Hu would not let it pass. On the train to New York he wrote a reply asking where a revolution in poetry should begin, and answering that poems must be written like prose. Mei objected strongly: the language of poetry and the language of prose, he held, had gone separate ways from the start. His ideas were still vague, Hu later said, but this was the first time he had put them in writing. The more his friends argued, he said, the more conservative they became, and the more radical he became. [HS-E10][HS-E11]

In February and March 1916 Hu arrived at a general idea: the history of Chinese literature was a history of changing tools, a living tool replacing a dead one. The change, he argued, had begun in the Tang dynasty. Buddhist monks first used the spoken language to record their teachers' sayings; Song philosophers adopted it; ordinary people used it for songs, plays and novels. Vernacular literature such as Yuan drama, he said, was the high point of a literary revolution the common people had carried out without knowing it. He laid this out in a long letter to Mei. To Hu's surprise, his conservative friend gladly accepted the historical conclusion. [HS-E12][HS-E13]

That summer Hu passed through Ithaca on his way to a conference and back, and talked with Ren and other old friends about China's problems, literature above all. On July 6, 1916, he wrote nine points from those talks in his diary. The first two, in translation: today's classical language is a half-dead language; today's spoken language is a living language. According to Hu's recollection, the letter from Ithaca arrived just as he was writing down those nine points; by his account, the incident on the lake took place on July 8. The two dates do not quite fit together; this article reports Hu's account as given. [HS-E39][HS-E14][HS-E15][HS-E16][HS-E38]

His criticism of the lake poem set off a storm of its own. Ren was indignant about the dead words. Mei, angrier still, rushed to his friend's defence with a heated letter. By Hu's account, Mei was then under the influence of his conservative teacher and opposed everything radical Hu proposed. Words become poetic language, he insisted, only after first-rate poets have refined and beautified them. [HS-E16][HS-E17]

Hu answered with a comic poem of about a hundred lines, the longest poem he ever wrote. It opened with Old Mei losing his temper, mocked his claim that words are neither living nor dead, and turned Mei's own argument against him: if words must be refined by poets, that was exactly what Hu wanted — poets of their own day should take everyday words, refine them and bring them into poetry. Both friends were furious and wrote back mocking his attempt at vernacular verse. But in their letters Hu found a shared concession: the spoken language could be used for storytelling, tales and novels, just not for poetry. Ren put it most bluntly; in translation, he wrote that the vernacular has its own uses, such as fiction and speeches, but it cannot be used for poetry. [HS-E18][HS-E19]

The argument had shrunk to one last point. In Hu's words, his friends had conceded everything to him except the fortress of poetry. He set out their reasons in order. First, no poet had ever written verse in everyday words; Hu himself could find only one poem, perhaps only a few lines of one. Second, and more convincing, no Chinese writer had ever seriously tried; poets had dropped in a few vernacular lines on a whim, while unconsciously looking down on the spoken language. His conclusion was simple. To persuade his friends he had to set the example and seriously try writing vernacular poetry himself. There was no other way. [HS-E20]

On July 26, 1916, he wrote to Ren — paraphrased here — that Shi Nai'an and Cao Xueqin had already shown in practice that the spoken language was the right tool for the novel; what remained was for someone to test in practice whether it could be the tool of verse. Even if they failed once, twice or countless times, he told these friends, they could keep experimenting until they knew whether the vernacular could be poetry. He compared his practice in vernacular verse and prose to opening up a new literary colony. He would have to ride out alone, with few companions, but he said his mind was made up. He also wrote that if the new land turned out to be barren, he might end his days in the old kingdom of classical poetry after all. In late July and early August he decided to stop writing classical verse. Before he had written more than a few vernacular poems, he had already named his next collection: Experiments. [HS-E21][HS-E22]

In November 1916 he turned more than a year of argument into an article with eight very moderate proposals. Literary revolution was a phrase that had often come up in his poems and letters and in his friends' letters, sometimes as a joke and sometimes in earnest. This time he kept it out of the title, which, as he put it, spoke of reform rather than revolution and offered only a tentative proposal, not a dogmatic conclusion. By his own later account, he worded it this way because he expected the older conservatives at home to object. He made three copies; one went to New Youth, the magazine edited by Chen Duxiu. [HS-E23][HS-E24]

The article appeared in New Youth in January 1917 and caused a great stir. Chen, already a convinced revolutionary, answered at once with an essay on literary revolution, publicly backing the cause and saying that the banner had been raised by his friend Hu, while he himself was raising it high in his support. Another Peking University professor, a scholar of classical phonology, also greatly admired the student's article. In the months before Hu returned to China, early in 1917, the two articles were a national talking point, and readers' letters kept coming; Hu also sent the vernacular poems he had been trying out to New Youth. As for those poems, his old classmate Zhao Yuanren often said they were not vernacular enough. Hu agreed, comparing himself to a woman whose feet had been bound for years: however much she loosened the bindings, she could never have natural feet again. [HS-E25][HS-E26][HS-E32]

## Why an Experiment

Hu himself said that what pushed him down this road was a chain of small accidents: a farewell poem, a scare on a lake. He distrusted theories that explain all of history by a single cause, and liked to joke that accidents often matter more than any one cause that claims to explain everything. [HS-E9][HS-E38]

The author's inference: what is most interesting here is that Hu turned an argument nobody could win into a question that could be tested. The claim that the spoken language cannot be poetry is absolute; good vernacular poems would shake it, while without poems, any number of letters would remain a standoff. By naming his collection Experiments in advance, he in effect set himself the task of supplying the proof. In his letters he also wrote down his retreat in case of failure, accepting that the result might prove him wrong — which made it a real experiment rather than the argument continued by other means. When he published, he lowered his voice, and Chen then raised the banner. [HS-E20][HS-E22][HS-E23][HS-E25]

As for the result, Hu described it late in life. After the May Fourth movement of 1919, students across China started more than four hundred small publications, all in the vernacular. At the same time he said the student movement was an interference with China's cultural renaissance, turning a cultural movement into a political one, though it did great service in spreading the vernacular. He had expected the struggle to take twenty-five to thirty years; in only about four, replacing the classical language with the vernacular in schools had almost completely succeeded, which surprised even him. In 1920 the Ministry of Education of the Beijing government ordered that textbooks for the first two years of primary school be written entirely in the spoken language. He called it a partial victory — and said he was still not satisfied: in forty years the vernacular had never become a complete tool of education and literature. [HS-E30][HS-E37]

## Think about it

A fictional scenario from the author: a young designer at a company wants to convince their parents that going independent could pay the bills. The parents reply: the work is unstable, and other people's success is other people's business.

**When the other side already grants that something works elsewhere but not for you, do you keep citing other people's successes, or run a small experiment you can both see? And if you experiment, what should count as the test?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The parents' real worry is unstable income, not design as a career; within a few months it is possible to win commissions that can be shown, with a record of income.
- **What you could do**: Narrow the argument to one point: can paid commissions keep coming in? Try it on the side, logging commissions, income and rejected work in one sheet. Agree on criteria and a deadline in advance, then review the record together and decide whether to scale up or stop.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another approach the author suggests: keep collecting stories of successful peers, at the cost that the other side can always say that is someone else, so the argument never closes; or quit and go all in, at the cost of treating a single outcome as the only evidence, with nothing to fall back on.

## Sources and uncertainty

This piece rests on one book: Hu Shi's oral memoir, translated and annotated by Tang Degang (Chinese Literature and History Press, 2020), chapter 8. As Tang explains, the English draft of this chapter was abridged from the Driven to Liangshan chapter of Hu's Forty Self-Narratives, revised in Hu's own hand and then translated back into Chinese, and it differs from the original to some degree. The chapter as a whole is therefore a participant's oral recollection many years later. It quotes writings from the time, such as Hu's diary entry of July 6, 1916 and his letter to Ren Hongjun of July 26. This article relies only on those texts as Hu quoted them, then translated into English and back again, and has not checked the originals. Mei Guangdi's and Ren Hongjun's words and actions reach us through Hu, and his explanation of the moderate wording is his own later account. Tang's notes are commentary: he thought the slogan that the classical language is half dead was devastating in its day but that its truth deserves a fresh look; he judged vernacular prose a complete success and vernacular poetry not yet out of the experimental stage; and he records that Hu's opponents felt he claimed too much credit, and that when Hu gave this account in a 1956 lecture at Harvard, Jerome B. Grieder, later his biographer, scoffed. These are Tang's accounts and were not checked separately here. On the outcome for vernacular poetry, Hu himself says in the same chapter that the new poetry of the past forty years had achieved a great deal, while acknowledging that the different branches of the new literature developed unevenly. Paragraphs marked as the author's inference are this article's reading; the scenario in the question is fictional.
