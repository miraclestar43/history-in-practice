# One Idea, Two Manuscripts: An Arrangement at the Linnean Society, 1858

*Wallace sends a paper from the Malay Archipelago; Lyell and Hooker judge that it sets out the same theory as a manuscript Darwin has left unpublished for years. They arrange for both men's material to be read together, and set down their reasons in a letter.*

unscheduled · World History · cand-20261030-darwin-wallace rev 1

## One Letter, Three Packets, and a Question Hard to Ask

London, 30 June 1858. Charles Lyell and Joseph Dalton Hooker, both Fellows of the Royal Society, wrote a joint letter to J. J. Bennett, secretary of the Linnean Society, enclosing papers which, they said, "all relate to the same subject". The difficulty was plain to state and awkward to resolve: the same theory had occurred to two men working independently of each other, and neither had published. Whose achievement was it, and who had the standing to say so? Someone had to put a version of events on paper first. [V-E1][V-E10][V-E17][V-E2]

## Two Men Who Never Compared Notes

Begin with the two men themselves. Darwin had a manuscript on species: drafted in 1839, copied out in 1844. Hooker read that copy in 1844, and its contents later reached Lyell. For years the two of them pressed Darwin to publish his views; for years he held back. Wallace, meanwhile, was travelling. He set out for the East Indies in 1854 and spent nearly eight years exploring the islands of the Malay Archipelago and parts of Papua, studying their animals and plants. Earlier, in 1848, he had gone to Brazil with Bates, through the primeval forests of the Amazon and the Rio Negro, and returned to England in 1852; the collections he brought home, especially rich in birds and plants, were largely destroyed by shipwreck. A sketch published in 1877 supplies the decisive sentence: while pursuing his researches in the Malay Archipelago, Wallace was unaware of Darwin's earlier labours in the same direction, yet arrived at almost the same general conclusions. [V-E3][V-E2][V-E9][V-E18][V-E8]

So the question was never who was right. It was what to do when two people have independently done the same thing: how credit gets divided, and who divides it. On one reading, it had to be settled on paper - whose name went on the work, who spoke first, through which institution, and in what order. Lyell and Hooker chose to arrange the matter themselves, and to write their reasoning into a letter made for publication. [V-E2]

## Where the Three Items Came From

Darwin's old manuscript was awkward in a particular way: it had never been meant for other eyes. In 1858 he added a footnote to it: "This MS. work was never intended for publication, and therefore was not written with care.—C. D. 1858.". [V-E12]

To show that his views had not changed over the years, Lyell and Hooker chose a second item: an abstract of a private letter Darwin had written in October 1857 to Professor Asa Gray, of Boston, in which he repeated his views, and which showed that they had remained unaltered from 1839 to 1857. [V-E4]

One detail does not match, and is best reported as it stands. Lyell and Hooker's letter dates this letter October 1857, while the abstract's own heading gives 5 September 1857, at Down. Both datings stand in the record; nothing further in the evidence decides between them, and this article does not settle it. [V-E4][V-E11]

The third item was Wallace's essay, "On the Tendency of Varieties to depart indefinitely from the Original Type," dated Ternate, February 1858. It was written for his friend and correspondent Darwin to read, with the express wish that, "if Mr. Darwin thought it sufficiently novel and interesting," it should be forwarded to Lyell. The openings read very differently. Darwin's extract opens from De Candolle and Malthus: "De Candolle, in an eloquent passage, has declared that all nature is at war, one organism with another, or with external nature.", and its next breath calls the struggle "the doctrine of Malthus applied in most cases with tenfold force." Wallace opens from an objection - that domestic varieties are unstable and tend to revert to the parent form, which is taken to show that species were created distinct and are kept that way. Different starting points, converging destinations. [V-E13][V-E14][V-E5][V-E15][V-E16]

What Darwin did on receiving the essay, Lyell and Hooker describe precisely. He wrote to Lyell proposing to obtain Wallace's consent and have the essay published as soon as possible. The two intermediaries approved - with one condition: that Darwin must not withhold his own memoir on the same subject, "as he was strongly inclined to do (in favour of Mr. Wallace)". The letter records his inclination: to stand aside and leave the field to the other man. [V-E6]

Lyell and Hooker put their reasoning to Darwin, and he gave them permission to make what use they thought proper of his memoir. Their grounds went beyond ranking two men: "we are not solely considering the relative claims to priority of himself and his friend, but the interests of science generally". Then a plain practical point. The scientific world was waiting for Darwin's complete work; better that some of the leading results of his years of labour should be laid before the public now, together with those of his able correspondent, and at once constitute "a goal from which others may start." [V-E7]

On 1 July 1858 the material was read to the Linnean Society. The letter lists the three items by date: extracts from the manuscript drafted in 1839 and copied in 1844, the abstract of the 1857 letter to Gray, and Wallace's essay of February 1858; the order in which they were read that day is not recorded. Nineteen years later the sketch of Wallace recorded that his essay, transmitted through Sir Charles Lyell to the Linnean Society, "was read before that body on July 1, 1858, coincidently with the reading of Mr, Darwin's paper" - the sketch gives the title as "On the Tendency of Species to form Varieties, and on the Perpetuation of Species and Varieties by means of Natural Selection". [V-E1][V-E3][V-E4][V-E5][V-E19]

The sketch also follows Wallace's later course. He returned to England in 1862 and spent several years classifying an enormous collection - upward of 100,000 insect specimens and more than 8,000 birds. In 1868 the Royal Society gave him its royal medal; in 1870 the Geographical Society of Paris its gold medal, and in that same year he published "Contributions to the Theory of Natural Selection". On human origins he still would not follow Darwin: natural selection alone, he held, was not competent to effect the transition from the anthropoid apes to man without the joint agency of some higher cause. The author's view: the man placed in the position of co-discoverer did not become a follower because of it. [V-E21][V-E22][V-E20]

## Handing Credit to a Procedure

One reading of the arrangement is this: Lyell and Hooker rewrote a dispute between two men over credit as a question of procedure. What they could put on the table was of three kinds - texts not previously published, dates that could be checked, and the account of two intermediaries. Darwin's old manuscript and the abstract of the Gray letter showed how early he had reached the idea; Wallace's essay, with its dateline and the manner of its sending, was consistent with Lyell and Hooker's statement that the two men had worked unknown to one another, and it was Wallace himself who had sent it. The author's inference: they stressed so heavily that the two men did not know of each other's work and that neither had published, because beyond who thought of it first there sits a harder question - whether thinking of something and never publishing is enough to hold your place. [V-E2][V-E3][V-E4][V-E5]

Their answer was: yes, but it has to be cashed in publicly. The author sets out at least three alternative courses, each with its own price: Darwin publishing alone first; only Wallace published, with Darwin left to state his own priority later; or everyone waiting for Darwin's complete book. What is listed here for contrast are options constructed by the author, not episodes recorded in the documents. The course actually taken was a fourth: three sets of material read together in one session, the dates printed in the body of the text, the reasoning addressed to a secretary and made public. The warrant Lyell and Hooker gave was that views founded on a wide deduction from facts and matured by years of reflection should at once become a goal from which others might start. The allocation was handed to a procedure; a party's willingness to give way was one step in it, not the deciding one. [V-E7]

## What This Record Cannot Tell Us

The records this article rests on fall into two classes, and both deserve a discount. One is the explanatory letter Lyell and Hooker wrote to the secretary of the Linnean Society on 30 June 1858; they were at once parties to the arrangement and witnesses to it. The other is a personal sketch in Popular Science Monthly from 1877, written nearly twenty years after the event. With no full private correspondence of Darwin's or Wallace's in hand, this article does not write about their feelings, their reactions at the time, or how the two of them settled matters between themselves. [V-E2][V-E8]

Likewise, the evidence does not say whether Wallace knew of the reading beforehand or consented to it, and this article makes no statement either way. The date of the letter to Gray stands in two versions and is left as found. How the reading itself went on the day, and how Darwin's complete work was later published, lie outside the scope here. What can be fixed is the arrangement itself: three sets of material, one addressee, one date, and the reasons written into the letter. [V-E4][V-E11][V-E1]

## Think about it

A fictional scenario proposed by the author: two problem-solvers who have never met independently complete the same proof. Each has only the timestamped drafts on his own computer, and no third party can show who thought of it first. A journal is willing to accept the work, but wants the order of the names settled first.

**When nobody can prove who thought of it first, who would you rather give the power to divide credit - timestamped records, an intermediary both sides trust, or the two people working it out between themselves? Why?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume the two really were unaware of each other; that both are willing to sign; and that the journal will not take them one at a time on alternating terms.
- **What you could do**: First, each submits a complete draft with its timestamped record. Second, a colleague with no stake in either side checks the material - only the timing and the independence, not the merits. Third, both sets of material are presented publicly in one session, with each timeline stated in the body of the text. Fourth, the byline is joint, and the explanation is printed in the text rather than left to a private understanding.
- **Another reasonable choice**: One alternative the author proposes: settle the order of the names by lot or by turns, trading procedural randomness for peace. The price is that it leaves no record of who did what.

</details>

## Sources and uncertainty

This article rests on two records: Lyell and Hooker's explanatory letter of 30 June 1858 to the secretary of the Linnean Society (both were parties to the arrangement), and a magazine personal sketch of 1877. No full private correspondence of Darwin's or Wallace's is in hand, so their feelings and reactions are not described; whether Wallace knew of or consented to the reading beforehand is not recorded in the material and no statement is made; the date of the letter to Gray stands in two versions (October 1857 / 5 September 1857) and is not adjudicated; how the reading went on the day and the later publication of Darwin's complete work fall outside the scope.
