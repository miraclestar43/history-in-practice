# Two Water Pipes on the Same Street

*In 1854 the mains of two south London water companies ran down the same streets, so that next-door neighbours might be drinking entirely different water. John Snow set out to use this experiment nobody had designed to test the doctrine he had been arguing for five years.*

## An experiment nobody designed, and somebody recorded

In July 1854 cholera came back to London. John Snow had been advocating a doctrine for five years, and this time he wrote down his resolve without ornament. He would "spare no exertion which might be necessary to ascertain the exact effect of the water supply on the progress of the epidemic", in places where "all the circumstances were so happily adapted for the inquiry". And he would do the work himself, because he wanted "the most satisfactory proof of the truth or fallacy of the doctrine which I had been advocating for five years." [W-E5]

What he had in view was a stretch of south London served by two water companies. "The pipes of each Company go down all the streets, and into nearly all the courts and alleys." Which company a house took had been settled by the owner or occupier back "when the Water Companies were in active competition", and, as Snow put it, "In many cases a single house has a supply different from that on either side." Nobody had arranged a comparison, yet one was already laid out in front of him. [W-E3][W-E4]

## Two companies, two intakes

Start with where the water came from. Between 1832 and 1849 London's supply changed a good deal. The Southwark Water Company merged with the South London Water Company to form the Southwark and Vauxhall Company; the works at London Bridge were abolished, and the new company drew from the Thames at Battersea Fields, about half a mile above Vauxhall Bridge. The Lambeth Company went on taking its water opposite Hungerford Market. [W-E10]

London was free of cholera from the latter part of 1849 to August 1853. In that interval the Lambeth Company moved its works from opposite Hungerford Market to Thames Ditton, "thus obtaining a supply of water quite free from the sewage of London." Snow was careful about the date. In a footnote he corrected Dr Baly, who had supposed that the Lambeth Company was already drawing from Thames Ditton in 1849; the move, Snow pointed out, did not happen until 1852. Where the two supplies were mixed, the Southwark and Vauxhall pipes still ran down every street alongside Lambeth's. [W-E2][W-E1]

To Snow the arrangement could hardly have been better. "Each Company supplies both rich and poor, both large houses and small", he wrote, and since there was "no difference whatever" in the houses, the people or their physical surroundings, no experiment could have been devised that would more thoroughly test the effect of water supply on cholera "than this, which circumstances placed ready made before the observer." It was enormous, too: "No fewer than three hundred thousand people of both Sexes, of every age and occupation, and of every rank and station, from gentlefolks down to the very poor, were divided into two groups without their choice, and, in most cases, without their knowledge" — one group drinking water that carried the sewage of London and whatever might have come from cholera patients, the other water free of it. [W-E4]

## House by house

He had not seen all this at once. Snow admitted that in the short days at the end of the previous year he "could not spare the time to make the inquiry", and that he had not then been fully aware of how intimately the two supplies were mixed, nor therefore how much the inquiry mattered. To turn the experiment to account, one thing alone was needed: "to learn the supply of water to each individual house where a fatal attack of cholera might occur." [W-E9]

In 1854 he started from a list. He asked the General Register Office for the addresses of people who had died of cholera in the districts where the two supplies were intermingled; some had been printed in the Weekly Returns, and he was allowed to copy others. About the middle of August he began with two sub-districts of Lambeth, Kennington first part and Kennington second part. There had been 44 deaths there down to 12 August. Of the houses where they occurred, he found 38 supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company, 4 by the Lambeth Company, and 2 with pump-wells on the premises and no company supply at all. [W-E11]

As soon as he had these particulars he passed them to Dr Farr, who, Snow wrote, "was much struck with the result". At Farr's suggestion the registrars of all the south districts of London were asked to return the water supply of the house in which the attack took place, in every death from cholera. The order was to take effect only after 26 August, so Snow resolved to carry his own inquiry down to that date, "so that the facts might be ascertained for the whole course of the epidemic." [W-E11]

He carried the inquiry over the other intermixed sub-districts of Lambeth, Southwark and Newington, "with a result very similar to that already given". Where a patient had been moved to a workhouse or anywhere else after the attack began, he asked about the water of the house where the person had been living when it started, not the place they had been moved to. [W-E12]

The hard part was classification. Very few households could name their water company on the spot: "Even when the water-rates are paid by the residents, they can seldom remember the name of the Water Company till they have looked for the receipt." For working families who paid weekly rents it was worse. The rates were paid by the landlord or his agent, who often lived at a distance, "and the residents know nothing about the matter." Snow wrote that it would have been almost impossible to finish the inquiry had he not found that a chemical test could tell the two waters apart with certainty. [W-E6]

"The test I employed was founded on the great difference in the quantity of chloride of sodium contained in the two kinds of water" — common salt. Add nitrate of silver and the two waters looked so different that they "could be at once distinguished without any further trouble." When a resident could not say for certain, Snow put some water in a small phial, wrote the address on the cover and examined it at home. He looked at the water itself, especially as it came in, before it reached the butt or cistern; and once he had learned the hours when each company's turncocks visited a street, the time the water arrived told him something too. These signs he used only to corroborate more decisive proof, never on their own. [W-E7]

The first seven weeks of the epidemic went into a single table. Houses supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company: 40,046, with 1,263 deaths from cholera. Lambeth Company: 26,107 houses, 98 deaths. The rest of London: 256,423 houses, 1,422 deaths. Per 10,000 houses that came to 315, 37 and 59. Snow's conclusion: "The mortality in the houses supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company was therefore between eight and nine times as great as in the houses supplied by the Lambeth Company". [W-E8]

The author's reading: the two sets of figures point the same way. Kennington's 38 houses against 4 came from the same intermixed streets, though these are only counts of houses with deaths: the evidence does not say how many houses each company supplied there, so they cannot be compared directly as death rates. The table's 315 against 37 covers the houses each company supplied, with the rest of London's 59 falling between them. Houses on Lambeth water died at a lower rate than the rest of the city, houses on Southwark and Vauxhall water at several times it. Snow's argument was that, since there was no difference whatever between the two groups in their houses, the people or their physical surroundings, a gap like this pointed to the water supply. But Table IX in the evidence is not limited to the intermixed streets, and the Kennington figures are not death rates, so that step still rests on Snow's assertion that the two groups did not differ. [W-E11][W-E8][W-E4]

## The parallel run is itself evidence

One reading is that the inquiry draws its force from running in parallel. Two sets of pipes lay along the same streets; the split had been fixed back when the companies were competing, and nobody altered anything to make an experiment, so it is hard to say the result was arranged by anyone's wishes. That is the point Snow himself pressed: the two groups were divided "without their choice" and mostly without their knowledge. [W-E3][W-E4]

Another approach, and this is the author's counterfactual rather than history, would have been to compare average death rates by district, or rich districts against poor ones. Wealth, housing and occupation would then have crowded in, and the effect of the water would have been hard to separate out. What Snow actually did was more laborious — death addresses, house by house, water carried home in phials — and in return he got a comparison of two waters within the same streets.

One more point is easy to miss, and it is the author's reading. Snow did not wait for the inquiry to be finished before speaking. Once he had the first figures from Kennington he passed them to Farr, and at Farr's suggestion registrars in the south districts were asked to return the water supply of the house for every cholera death. The early result went first into improving how the evidence was gathered. [W-E11]

## The edges of this evidence

Plainly: every figure and every account in this piece comes from Snow's own book and his own tables, and the evidence used here contains no independent check by a health authority or anyone else at the time. The addresses of the dead came from the General Register Office, and after 26 August the registrars were asked to return the water supply; but the statistics and the argument we read here were all put together by Snow. When the same man proposes the doctrine, conducts the inquiry and presents the statistics, that does not make the data wrong, but it does mean we are reading one side's record. [W-E8]

Second, "no difference whatever" between the groups is Snow's assertion, not the product of random assignment. Which company a house took was decided by the owner or occupier, not drawn by lot; whether that choice tracked rents, the age of the houses or their location is a question the evidence here cannot answer, and it is the author's question, not Snow's. His own inquiry shows that for working families on weekly rents the rates were paid by the landlord or his agent, and the residents did not even know which company supplied them. One reading is that the choice was often not in the hands of the people drinking the water. [W-E4][W-E3][W-E6]

Finally, this piece works from OCR text of a scanned copy, and many figures in the book came through garbled: the year of the Lambeth move reads "185S", and the numbers in the chemical test are corrupted too. Only clearly legible figures are used here, such as Table IX's house counts, deaths and deaths per 10,000 houses, and the Kennington figures; the year of the move follows Snow's footnote (1852); the chemical test is described only qualitatively. [W-E2][W-E1][W-E7][W-E8]

## Think about it

You own a process that happens to be running in two versions at once. Because of legacy arrangements from a system migration, some customers go through version A and some through version B. The two groups broadly resemble each other, but the split was not random. Next quarter you must settle on one standard way of working — and your data is only a few weeks old.

**When the evidence is still incomplete, what would make you change course? Would you wait until the gap is too large to argue away, or act once the difference is stark enough and the rival explanations can be held down? Which mistake frightens you more — being too early, or being too late?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The two customer groups broadly resemble each other, but the split was not random; unseen differences (customer type, account manager) may exist.
Only a few weeks of data; the sample is not large, but both groups left complete records.
Changing the process has a cost; so does not changing it.
No perfect experiment is coming; you can only judge on the comparison in front of you.
- **What you could do**: Write down first where the differences between the two groups could come from: besides the process itself, what else could explain the gap in results?
Pick the most dangerous rival explanation and find one piece of evidence that pins it down — a comparison within the same class of customer, the equivalent of Snow's comparison within the same intermixed streets.
Compare only data you are confident in; skip suspect breakdowns, and turn the gap into plain language (one runs at several times the rate of the other) before reaching for a complicated model.
Set a line for action: how large the gap must be and how well the rival explanations must be pinned down before you switch. Below the line, keep watching — and write down the date to look again.
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's other option: keep both versions running for now, treat the split as an ongoing comparison, and decide when the data has run longer. The cost is that one group of customers goes on using the weaker process in the meantime; the benefit is avoiding a wholesale change made on incomplete data. Which way you go depends on which error you mind more.
