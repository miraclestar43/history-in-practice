# A Net That Caught No One

*The emperor wanted a sorcerer chief who did not exist, and one provincial governor after another was scolded for failing to catch him. How was an aging governor, known as "the Old Bodhisattva," supposed to reply?*

## The Rumours Reach Jiangxi

In the middle of June 1768, rumours of soulstealing reached Jiangxi. They came through the Xin River valley on Jiangxi's eastern side, the easy route into the neighbouring province of Zhejiang. The governor of Jiangxi, Wu Shaoshi, was about seventy. The rumours had entered his jurisdiction, and he sent no report to the court. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E10][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E8]

That year the Qianlong emperor, Hongli, pressed provincial officials over their failure to catch the sorcerers' leaders. Philip A. Kuhn, the historian who reconstructed the panic, points out that the emperor could turn his anger on provincial officials who failed to catch those leaders. They could not avoid failing, because no such leader existed. Kuhn also insists that Hongli did not see the absurdity of the case at the time. The governors faced an emperor who was in earnest about the hunt, and a quarry that could not be caught. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E1][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E21]

## What Other Governors Heard

Consider first how governors elsewhere answered. Asiha, the governor of Henan, explained to Hongli that the sorcerers had escaped because they knew a secret art of making themselves invisible. Hongli answered in a rescript, the comment the emperor wrote in vermilion ink on an official's report. He mocked Asiha: with ideas like that in his head, no wonder his subordinates were slack about arrests, since they were taking him for a "useless good-for-nothing." Offering an excuse for catching no one only brought the emperor's ridicule. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E2]

Feng Qian, the governor of Zhejiang, took a different line. He reported that he had handed the investigation of the suspects to the province's judicial commissioner. Kuhn notes that in ordinary circumstances this would have been an entirely reasonable step. Hongli instead accused Feng Qian of shirking. He opened with a rhetorical question: "What sort of matter is this, that you hand it to the judicial commissioner according to routine?" He then asked whether Feng Qian ought not to be conducting detailed interrogations in person every day, and closed with a verdict: "The habits of the provinces are truly detestable!" In this case even going by the rules counted as an offence. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E3]

## What the Old Bodhisattva Sent In

Wu Shaoshi's position was not quite like theirs. The year before the crisis, someone told Hongli that people called the governor of Jiangxi "the Old Bodhisattva." Kuhn writes that the emperor worried this Old Bodhisattva was too passive and might let official business slide. Wu Shaoshi's health was also in question. Hongli asked Gao Jin about it. Gao Jin was governor-general of Liangjiang, Wu Shaoshi's immediate superior, and a relative of the emperor by marriage. He replied that he had heard of nothing showing that Wu Shaoshi was unfit for office, and he promised to keep watching and report later. Hongli believed Wu Shaoshi to be experienced, honest and dependable, and kept him in his post. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E7][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E9][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E16]

Hongli also held Wu Shaoshi's family in high regard. Wu Shaoshi headed a celebrated family of legal experts. Under the rule of avoidance, members of one family were normally barred from serving in the same government office at the same time. Even so, Wu Shaoshi and his sons Wu Huan and Wu Tan were twice appointed together to the Board of Punishments, the central ministry of justice. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E8]

Back to June. The rumours had crossed the border, and Wu Shaoshi sent no report. He later told Hongli that he had verbally ordered his subordinates to keep an eye on suspicious travellers. But no one was arrested, and no cases of queue-clipping turned up in the province. On the failure to report the rumours, the emperor rebuked him only mildly: "You were wrong." Kuhn calls it an unexpected show of restraint toward an elderly and highly respected governor. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E10][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E11]

Wu Shaoshi's second son, Wu Tan, was judicial commissioner of neighbouring Jiangsu. Like his father he was a respected legal scholar. His case was different, though: what he failed to report to the court were actual soulstealing cases in Jiangsu that spring. Then Sazai, the imperial textile commissioner, sent a secret report on the cases, and Wu Tan was disgraced. When Wu Tan admitted that he had failed to report in time, Hongli's rescript began with his past record: "When you were at the Board of Punishments, you performed splendidly. But as soon as you took a provincial post, you caught the vile habits of equivocation and corruption." It ended by calling him an "ungrateful creature." The father received "You were wrong" and the son a torrent of abuse. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E17][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E4][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E11]

In early October, Wu Shaoshi sent in a very different kind of report. He said he had spread a net across his province tighter than any other province's. He had picked capable agents who would "change their dress and take other surnames," so that they could travel in disguise and search each county in secret, with a report every ten days on whether anyone suspicious had appeared. He had also ordered each prefecture and department to send sharp, able men to search "convents, temples and monasteries, ancestral halls, schools, and deep mountains and dense thickets." The places named ran from religious houses and ancestral halls to the remotest forest. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E12]

In his memorials Wu Shaoshi also issued a warning. Practitioners of "heterodox ways and evil arts," he wrote, set up incense-burning charitable societies as a front, "openly gathering ignorant village men and women, secretly colluding with desperadoes and ruffians." On the pretext of warding off disaster and bandits, they spread wicked talk, stockpiled weapons and drew people into their following. He ordered that anyone, monk, Daoist priest or layman, who was an unfamiliar face, looked dejected, kept moving from place to place, or avoided being seen, be reported to the local officials at once. Hongli's rescript read: "I fear these are empty words; truly hard to believe." It was open doubt. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E13]

And indeed nothing happened in Jiangxi. Six weeks later, after Hongli had already called off the campaign, Wu Shaoshi apologized humbly and repeatedly and then reported that not a single case of queue-clipping had been found in the province. Kuhn notes that no archival document shows Jiangxi ever rounding up suspects as the neighbouring provinces did. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E14]

Wu Tan went another way. Around September 28, about three weeks after the emperor's tirade, he reported that he had caught no soulstealers, but his inquiries had uncovered eleven Buddhist scripture halls outside Suzhou, built by lay believers. He then arrested about seventy people, and their confessions showed that the sects had been active in the area since 1677. Kuhn says plainly that he cannot prove it, but he conjectures that sects of this size could not have stayed hidden from the authorities for long in a city as busy as Suzhou. In his reading, the judicial commissioner was under pressure to make arrests and passed it down to his subordinates, and the clerks and runners at the bottom handed over these harmless, easily bullied believers to have something to show for their efforts. Hongli reacted very severely. He ordered the sectarians harshly punished and had them questioned about any links to the soulstealing. The scripture-hall case then set off a round of punishments for past officials, with names ranging from county magistrates up to governors-general, though most received only token penalties. Kuhn calls the impeachment process a farce. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E18][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E20]

When the soulstealing affair ended, Kuhn writes, Hongli brought the campaign to a close by looking for scapegoats among his officials, and he meant to show, by punishing their failures, that his campaign had been right. The official singled out for heavy punishment was Su'erde. He had been serving in Suzhou when the first cases broke out there and had not reported them. Later, as governor of Shanxi, he "obstructed the suppression most of all." Hongli demoted him to provincial judicial commissioner and sent him off to Xinjiang. Wu Shaoshi, by contrast, came through the crisis unharmed. Far from rebuking him, Hongli appointed him president of the Board of Punishments the following year. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E19][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E22][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E16]

## How a Net Can Work

What actually happened in Jiangxi? Kuhn sets out two possibilities: either the net was never really put to use, or it was incapable of catching anyone. He observes that Jiangxi shows none of the torture and false testimony found in other provinces, and from this he concludes that Wu Shaoshi never intended to pursue a case he believed rested on false rumour. In Kuhn's view, the anxious warnings and the elaborate deployment were only a performance. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E15]

If Kuhn's inference is accepted, I would argue that several things came together to let outward compliance and inward inaction succeed. The first was form. Wu Shaoshi sent in exactly what the emperor wanted: a deployment, named places to search, regular reports, even warnings phrased in the campaign's own language. The second was timing. The report that nothing had been found arrived after the campaign had been halted, wrapped in apologies. The third was the man himself: his seventy years, his family's reputation in the law, the trust the emperor showed in keeping him on, and Gao Jin, his immediate superior and the emperor's relative by marriage. Kuhn's own explanation of why Wu Shaoshi escaped also turns on the man. Because of his legal expertise, and perhaps because Gao Jin protected him, it was not easy to punish him for declining to join in what Kuhn calls a collective game. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E15][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E16][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E8][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E9][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E14]

The risks are on record too. Hongli wrote "I fear these are empty words" in response to Wu Shaoshi's warning. The Old Bodhisattva nickname, and the worry that he was too passive, had reached the emperor a year before the crisis. Su'erde's fate shows that when the campaign ended and Hongli looked for scapegoats among his officials, those who had failed to report or had obstructed the campaign could be singled out for heavy punishment. My inference is that Wu Shaoshi got through largely on advantages no one else could borrow. Another official doing the same thing might have received a very different rescript. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E13][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E7][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E19][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E22]

Nor is this a story of a wise man outwitting a tyrant. Kuhn lists several forms that bureaucratic resistance may have taken. Some was deliberate. Some came from sluggish procedures that kept officials from reacting to an emergency in time. Some officials, doubting the whole thing, may have refused to take the cases seriously. Others may have feared that the suppression campaign would endanger their careers. And some upright officials may have refused to persecute innocent people on the strength of false accusations. Kuhn writes that these actions do not look coordinated, and that resisting despotic power does not require collusion. For Jiangxi, Kuhn infers that Wu Shaoshi never intended to pursue a case he regarded as a false rumour; in my reading this comes closest to the officials who doubted the whole thing. Kuhn also cautions that saying Hongli "used" political crime is no closer to the truth than saying political crime "used" him. My own reading is that the quiet in Jiangxi and the seventy arrests in Suzhou came out of the same structure. What differed was how each official handled the same pressure. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E5][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E6][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E21][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E20][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E15]

## The Limits of This Article

This article rests entirely on the Chinese translation of Kuhn's Soulstealers, by Chen Jian and Liu Chang, and uses no other source. The rescripts and memorials are quoted as they appear in that translation, so I cannot tell whether they match the archival originals word for word, and the English renderings here are translations of that Chinese text. The claim that Wu Shaoshi was merely putting on a show is Kuhn's inference. Kuhn first named the possibility that the net simply could not catch anyone, but, finding no torture or false testimony in Jiangxi, concluded that only the explanation of a show was credible. As for why Wu Shaoshi was hard to punish, Kuhn attributes it to his legal expertise and conjectures that Gao Jin's protection may also have played a part. Kuhn was unable to find the follow-up report Gao Jin promised. [BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E15][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E16][BK-JIAOHUN-U141-E9]

## Think about it

Headquarters, acting on a wave of rumour, launches a sweeping investigation and tells every region to deliver results by a deadline. One by one, neighbouring regions hand in lists of names. You are a senior regional head. You judge the rumour to be false, so you submit a detailed investigation plan and do not round up people to fill the quota.

**Your choice protects people who might be wrongly accused, but headquarters may see it as going through the motions. What makes you think you can carry that risk? Would you do the same if you had no seniority or professional reputation to fall back on?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume this regional head has a strong professional reputation at headquarters and a direct superior willing to speak up for them, but some people at headquarters are already complaining that they deliver plans and no results.
- **What you could do**: 1. First make sure the judgment rests on evidence and not just instinct: where did the rumour come from, and are there leads that can be checked? 2. Write the plan in enough detail to be checked: which places were searched, how often reports are made, and who is responsible. 3. When reporting that nothing was found, give the grounds for that finding rather than the conclusion alone. 4. Estimate the worst case: if the work is judged a mere formality, who pays the price, and could it fall on subordinates?
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another approach, proposed by this article's author: instead of simply submitting a plan, state in it your own judgment of whether the rumour is true and your reasons, and ask headquarters to define what counts as results. This is more transparent, but it also brings the disagreement into the open, and the risk may arrive sooner.

</details>

## Sources and uncertainty

This article relies only on the Chinese translation of Kuhn's Soulstealers. Rescripts are rendered from the text as printed there. In that text, the rescript translated here as "I fear these are empty words; truly hard to believe" ends with a stray character (口) that the Chinese article reproduces unchanged, and it is left out of the English translation. The view that Wu Shaoshi was only putting on a show is Kuhn's inference. Of the reasons Kuhn gives for why Wu Shaoshi was hard to punish, Gao Jin's protection is a conjecture.
