# The Expedition That Wouldn't Stop: A Ledger from the First Year of Guangxu

*Coastal defense needed money; so did the western expedition. Those who wanted to halt it called stopping a way to cut losses. Zuo Zongtang kept a different ledger — some costs, he argued, stop nothing by being stopped.*

## The Opening: Two Claims on the Same Money

In the first year of the Guangxu reign, Zuo Zongtang had just pacified Guanlong, the Gansu–Shaanxi corridor, and his army was about to march out beyond the pass when the argument over coastal defense broke out first. Since the Qianlong emperor took Xinjiang, critics said, "several million a year have drained away, a leaky vessel"; now things had reached the point of "exhausting the strength of the whole realm to feed the western army, leaving nothing for the unforeseen." Better to do as the British proposed: "let Pasha found his own state and pay homage as a vassal, call off the western expedition, concentrate on coastal defense." There was only so much money. Which front to stop first demanded an answer on the spot. [QSG4121-E3]

## The Background: Pasha, Britain, Russia, and the Ground Between

Before judging whether to stop, look at what the western spending was protecting. The Muslim chief beyond the frontier whom the biography calls Pasha — a title, roughly governor — was by origin a hoşbeg, or clan chief, of the Andijan. Andijan had belonged to Kokand; Russia destroyed Kokand, and Andijan alone survived. Pasha, the record says, "feared Russian pressure and slipped across the border." Inside it he took Kashgar, nibbled away at the eight southern cities, and defeated Tuo Ming, the Muslim holding Ürümqi, so that he also possessed the Ili cities of the north and took their taxes. He commanded the Muslim population, kept envoys moving to draw in British and Russian support, and bought arms: "The English secretly helped him, wishing to set him up as a separate state, to serve as a buffer against Russia." [QSG4121-E1]

The north moved faster. Because the Muslims had raided its border repeatedly, Russia sent troops to drive them out, took Ili, and announced it would take Ürümqi too. By the first year of Guangxu, then, Ili was in Russian hands, and Ürümqi sat inside Russia's stated plans. [QSG4121-E2]

One reading of the position is that by now the table held not two parties but four — the court, Pasha, Britain and Russia, all entangled. For a pause to hold up, someone must first say who watches this ground during the pause, and who fills it. That is the author's inference; the biography records events and offers no such analysis.

## The Course: From Calling Off the Expedition to Recovering Both Halves

The coastal-defense case was stated flatly: the expedition was a vessel with a hole in it, and if trouble came at sea there would be nothing to meet it with. Better to let Pasha set up a tributary state and turn every resource to the coast. The biography says "Li Hongzhang pressed this hardest of all," but records none of Li Hongzhang's own specific arguments; for that side of the debate, the record preserves only one summary paragraph of the proponents' case. [QSG4121-E3]

Zuo's answer was arithmetic, not moral: carving territory loose did not save money: the territory would only shrink, every strategic point be lost, and the frontier troops could not be cut. The biography gives his words: "Guanlong has only just been pacified. Instead of recovering the lands the state once lost, we carve them off and let another state be founded there — that is to store up trouble of our own making. Should Pasha fail to hold them, they will be swallowed by England in the west or fold north into Russia. Our territory shrinks of its own accord, every strategic point is lost, the frontier troops cannot be reduced, and the spending goes on as before. It does nothing for coastal defense, it damages our prestige, and it feeds disorder. This must not be." The sting is in the middle: the troops cannot be cut, and the money flows out all the same. [QSG4121-E4]

The dispute ended quickly. Wenxiang, a Grand Council minister, was the only one to favor Zuo's plan; the decision went to march beyond the pass rather than disband, with Zuo named Imperial Commissioner for military affairs and Jin Shun as his deputy. The biography records only that Wenxiang agreed, and no reason why. [QSG4121-E5]

In the third month of the second year the army reached Suzhou. In the fifth, Jintang crossed the Tianshan northward, joined Jin Shun's force, and took Ürümqi first; Bai Yanhu fled to Toksun. In the ninth month the southern city of Manas fell. The north was pacified; the plan turned south. [QSG4121-E6]

There was a general order in the army: "The Muslims of the west were driven on by their Andijan chief and have long been sick of disorder. Wherever the great army goes, no rape or plunder, no slaughter. A kingly army is like a seasonable rain; this is the moment for it." Two prohibitions, and no more: no rape or plunder, and no killing. [QSG4121-E7]

In the third month of the third year Jintang took Dabancheng and released every captured Muslim of the south, sending them home. Panic spread through the south; the next day Toksun fell, other columns stormed the cities and passes and recovered Turpan, and more than ten thousand Muslims submitted. Then Pasha's end: he drank poison and died; his son Bokhuli killed his own brother and fled to Kashgar. The freeing of the captives and the alarm down south are set down side by side. [QSG4121-E8]

The call to stop came a second time. The minister at Khüree, in Urga, reported that the western question should be settled by fixing a frontier, and courtiers argued the expedition cost too much: with Ürümqi and Turpan in hand, the army could rest. Zuo sighed: "The moment offers an opening — and we would settle for drawing lines on the map and shrinking back?" He sent up a forceful memorial against it; the throne agreed. [QSG4121-E9]

There was also the matter of restraint. Russia was then at war with Turkey, and Jin Shun asked leave to strike Ili while it lay open. "No," said Zuo. "An army that does not act by right hands them their excuse." The army turned back to reduce Ushi, then took the four eastern cities of the south; He Buyun surrendered the Chinese city of Kashgar, and the four western cities fell in turn. Bokhuli and Bai Yanhu escaped into Russia, and Zuo was raised to a second-class marquis. [QSG4121-E10][QSG4121-E11]

Set side by side, the two lines show one test: recover the ground, but leave no handle for the other side. In the first year of Guangxu the fear was that cession would damage prestige and feed disorder; here, that an army acting without right would hand Russia its excuse. One reading is that he judged a move by whether it could be held afterwards and defended in argument, not by the land it gained. The biography draws no such conclusion.

## The Analysis: What the Ledger Has to Count

With north and south recovered, the question shifted from whether to fight to how to hold. In the first month of the fourth year Zuo laid out plans to make Xinjiang a regular province and asked that Russia be negotiated with over two things: the return of Ili and the surrender of fugitives. An Imperial Commissioner, Chong Hou, was sent to Russia. Russia pressed three demands — trade, boundaries, indemnity — and Chong Hou signed at once. Court officials impeached him, and the matter stayed open for a long time. [QSG4121-E12][QSG4121-E13]

Zuo's memorial laid out the ledger for trading land for peace. Russia's return of Ili would cost Russia nothing, and what China got back would be "only a stretch of empty outskirts"; Chong Hou was meanwhile offering Russia the Korgos and Tekes rivers, ceding the southwest of Ili outright. "Not a single arrow has been loosed," Zuo wrote, "and we are to give up a key position at once": the boundary terms must not be granted. Russian merchants were after trade, but their government was planting consulates everywhere, hoping to use commerce to reach deep into the interior; those trade terms must not be granted either. His order of operations: "first let there be discussion, subtle and flexible; then decide it in battle, patient and determined to win." [QSG4121-E14]

Chong Hou was disgraced and removed, and Zeng Jize sent to Russia to revise the treaty. Zuo asked to move out to Hami to prepare the recovery of Ili. In the fourth month of the sixth year he set out from Suzhou "carrying his coffin" — meaning to die on campaign — and reached Hami in the fifth. Russia, hearing the imperial army was moving in force, reinforced Ili and the Naryn and put warships to sea to shake the capital; Tianjin, Fengtian and Shandong were all on alert. In the seventh month Zuo was summoned to the capital as an adviser, with Jintang replacing him; the biography says Russia too was cowed by our military prestige. In the first month of the following year peace was concluded, Ili returned, and the coastal forces stood down. [QSG4121-E15][QSG4121-E17][QSG4121-E18]

In practice the ledger came down to logistics. Zuo "was quick to judge the moment in war and never kept to one set of tactics," and in western affairs he made saving troops and filling the stores his first principle. Twenty months beyond the pass, and every city in north and south recovered: the biography credits the abundance of supply. There was garrison farming too. He argued for military colonies early on and was thought pedantic; then he set out the abuses of the old garrisons inside and outside the pass — men on the army rolls who could not also farm — and proposed dividing the two, keeping the strong as soldiers, settling the willing and the weak on the land. Then people respected the long view behind it. [QSG4121-E19][QSG4121-E21]

After the victories, opinion abroad shifted. The empire had been at peace so long that its armaments had gone slack, and the overseas powers, talking of wealth and strength among themselves, still whispered that China was frail even after it had put down one great crisis after another. Once Zuo had dealt with Pasha, the foreigners began to spread the word about him. On his first arrival in the capital, a church tower in the Inner City overlooked the palace, and popular rumor said the Marquis Zuo would have it torn down; he posted a proclamation to explain, and the talk stopped. [QSG4121-E22][QSG4121-E23]

The author's reading is that unpacking that first-year argument yields three reusable tests: can what you were protecting still be held once you stop? Is the money you save actually saved? Does the cost of delay simply pass to whoever comes next? Zuo's objection held not because the expedition was worth more, but because cession left the cost structure untouched — less territory, the same troops, the same spending, and trouble added. That is the author's summary; the biography records events only.

## Today: Is a Pause the Same as Cutting Losses?

> Scenes like this usually turn up in budget clothing. A software company has run a ten-year-old system that eats most of the operations budget each year, and someone proposes freezing investment and moving everyone to a new platform. By the ledger of the first year of Guangxu, three questions come first. Once you stop, who keeps the obligations to existing customers, the historical data, the records the regulator requires? Does the frozen spending really come back as cash, or does it push the cost of rebuilding to the day the system fails, with breach and migration billed on top? And who carries the delay — this management's numbers, or whoever inherits it in three years?

> Or a city's finances. A suburban bus route that loses money every year is called a leaky vessel; someone wants to cut it and move the subsidy to a new metro extension. Before cutting: who picks up the commuters and the patients along the route? Will the new line's own early losses swallow the subsidy saved? And if the route must be revived in a few years, will reopening cost more than keeping it going? Only when all three have answers is there a trade-off to speak of.

## The Limits: Where This Material Comes From

Every fact here comes from a single source: the biography of Zuo Zongtang in the Draft History of the Qing, compiled and printed long after the events it describes. There are no contemporary memorials in their original form, no edicts to check against, and no account from Li Hongzhang, Pasha, the Russian side, or Chong Hou to set against it. The "Zuo said," "Zuo sighed," and the general order are the historian's rendering and need not be verbatim. Wenxiang's support and Li Hongzhang's advocacy each appear in a single sentence, without their arguments, and no motive is written in for either.

Numbers are reproduced as written and their basis cannot be verified: "several million a year" is not identified as standing military spending or as a shortfall in provincial remittances, and "more than ten thousand" who submitted is likewise unqualified. Dates stay in the original — the first, second, third, fourth and sixth years of Guangxu — and are not converted; "the first month of the following year" means the year after the one just named. One more point: "Pasha feared Russian pressure" is the source's own phrasing, flagged here as the biography's. The alternative option below and the two modern scenes are the author's additions, made to show a method of judgment, and appear nowhere in the record.

## Think about it

A small factory that has been running for twenty years: shareholders call the spending on upgrading its old equipment a leaky vessel, and just then a new order arrives that needs new equipment bought first. The money covers only one of the two, and cutting the old investment looks exactly like cutting losses.

**When resources can only go to one of the two, on what grounds do I call this a trade-off rather than evasion?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume that if the upgrade is stopped entirely, cash does loosen in the short term; but yield on the old line falls, and rework and downtime risks rise. The new order has a delivery deadline, and payment arrives only after delivery.
- **What you could do**: Write the three tests out, one line each. First, what has to be protected once you stop — delivery on the old orders, existing customer contracts — and can it still be protected? Second, is the spending genuinely saved, or are the upgrade cost, penalties and the eventual cost of restarting simply pushed downstream? Third, who absorbs the cost of delay, and does it fall on the next plant manager or the next crew? Then write each path as a single line of accounts: not stopping — where the money goes, what it buys; stopping — what is saved, what is owed. Both lines written out, and only then has the sum been done.
- **Another reasonable choice**: The alternative the author suggests: neither full funding nor a full stop, but break the old-line upgrade into its smallest first step, fix the single stage that most affects yield, and stage the equipment purchases against the new order's payment schedule. Or the reverse: protect the new order first, hold the old line at a minimum safe level, and set a review date at which the same questions are run again. None of this appears in the record; it is the author's addition to show the method.
