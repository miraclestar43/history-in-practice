# Six Miles of Walking, One Mile of Progress

*After the pack ice crushed their ship, Shackleton's party struggled across the floes, ferrying loads in relays; after four days of hard effort he decided to camp on a thick floe and wait. He wrote down his reasons: the snow was too soft to get far, and leads might open close by.*

## The Starting Point

Shackleton led the expedition. On the Antarctic pack ice, his ship was crushed by the ice beyond all hope of ever being righted, and the men had to abandon her. They were alive and well, and their stores and equipment were still in hand. He set down the new task in a single sentence: "The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition." [S-E2][S-E22]

But how to reach land? The surface was loose, wet snow, and loads had to be ferried back and forth in relays; a day's march gained about a mile in a straight line. On the morning after four days of hard effort, he decided to stop and camp. [S-E6][S-E7]

## The Original Plan, and the Two-Pound Rule

The plan he began with was far grander. If the landing was made in time, "the Transcontinental party will set out on their 1800-mile journey at once," he wrote, "in the hope of accomplishing the march across the Pole and reaching the Ross Sea base in five months." Should the landing be made too late in the season, the party would go into winter quarters, lay out depots during the autumn and the following spring, and set out as early as possible in 1915. Once the ice had crushed the ship, the task became something else entirely: get the whole party onto land. [S-E1][S-E23][S-E2]

The ship had been locked in the ice for 281 days and had drifted more than 570 miles to the north-west. When they left her he worked out where they stood. They were 346 miles from Paulet Island, "the nearest point where there is any possibility of finding food and shelter." A small hut built there by the Swedish expedition in 1902 held stores left by the Argentine relief ship, and he knew those stores well: he had bought them himself in London on behalf of the Argentine Government. The nearest barrier to the west was about 180 miles away, but a party going there would still be about 360 miles from Paulet Island, and "there would be no means of sustaining life on the barrier." [S-E9]

## From Dump Camp to Ocean Camp

At a place they named Dump Camp, the equipment went through a severe weeding-out. He decided to issue each man a complete new set of Burberrys and underclothing, along with a supply of new socks. He also fixed the rule: "personal gear must not exceed two pounds per man," which meant nothing but bare necessaries would go on the march. As he put it, they could not afford to cumber themselves with unnecessary weight. [S-E3][S-E4]

And so the detail that stays with you: "sovereigns were thrown away and photographs were kept." Queen Alexandra had given the ship a Bible with her own writing in it. He tore the fly-leaf out of it, and also a page of Job. [S-E4][S-E5][S-E20]

The real weight was the boats. Each of them, with its gear and the sledges beneath it, weighed more than a ton. "The sledges were the point of weakness." It seemed almost hopeless, he wrote, to keep them from smashing under their loads on rough pressure-ice "which stretched ahead of us for probably 300 miles." [S-E10]

The first day's method was the relay. A pioneer party with picks and shovels broke a road through the pressure-ridges for the sledges carrying the boats. The seven dog teams "took their sledges forward for half a mile, then went back for the other sledges." Worsley took charge of the two boats, "with fifteen men hauling, and these also had to be relayed." They crossed two opening cracks with killer whales pushing their snouts through, and by 5 p.m. they had covered a mile, north-north-west. [S-E10][S-E11]

The arithmetic of the march made ugly reading. On one of the first days they gained about one mile in a direct line; the detours made the ground actually covered at least two miles, and the relays pushed the day's marching up to six. Some of the dog teams had covered at least ten miles. That night they camped on flat ice that "proved to be young and salty." On the first night of the march, killer whales blew all night, and at 2 a.m. a crack opened about 20 feet from the camp. The ice was thin enough for the killers to break through, he wrote, "but there was no other camping-ground within our reach and we had to take the risk." [S-E6][S-E12]

The next morning "the snow was falling so heavily that we could not see more than a few score yards ahead, and I decided not to strike camp. A path over the shattered floes would be hard to find, and to get the boats into a position of peril might be disastrous." Men went back to Dump Camp for wood and blubber for the fire. When the weather cleared a little, he and Worsley went prospecting to the west and found a large floe that offered a fairly good road for at least another mile to the north-west. After lunch they struck camp. [S-E13]

At one stage the work went well. Two dog teams were hitched to the cutter and "started off in splendid style. They needed to be helped only once; indeed fourteen dogs did as well or even better than eighteen men." A dozen men with picks and shovels could cut a road through a ridge about 14 feet high in ten minutes and leave a smooth, or comparatively smooth, path behind them. [S-E14]

The overall account did not change. Through wet, deep snow, with constant halts to cut a road, they "managed to march the best part of a mile towards our goal, though the relays and the deviations again made the actual distance travelled nearer six miles." Seeing that the men were all exhausted, he had the tents pitched in the lee of the two boats. While they were at it, a sailor found a little pool of thawed snow on a sail lying in one of the boats: just a sip each. He quoted what one man wrote in his diary: "One has seen and tasted cleaner, but seldom more opportunely found water." [S-E15]

The next day, after four days of hard effort, broke cold and still, with the same wet snow. In the clearing light he could see the situation plainly. With the surface as loose as it was, and given how little they had to show for four days of strenuous effort, they would never get any great distance. And there was a second possibility to weigh: leads might open close to them, which would let them row north-west to where they might find land. The two considerations pointed one way. He decided to find a more solid floe, camp there, and stay until conditions were more favourable for a second attempt to escape from their icy prison. [S-E7]

Tents and everything else were moved to a thick, heavy old floe about one and a half miles from the wreck, and there they made their camp. They called it Ocean Camp. [S-E7]

He also recorded how hard it once was to shift the two boats. The surface was terrible, like nothing any of them had seen before: "We were sinking at times up to our hips, and everywhere the snow was two feet deep." [S-E16]

He also decided, in his words, "to conserve our valuable sledging rations, which would be so necessary for the inevitable boat journey, as much as possible, and to subsist almost entirely on seals and penguins." A party went back to Dump Camp for clothing, tobacco and whatever else they could find, but heavy snow and the thaw had swallowed a good many of the things left there. [S-E17]

This floating lump of ice, about a mile square at first and later splitting into smaller and smaller pieces, was their home for nearly two months. In that time they went back again and again to the wreck and retrieved clothing, food "and some few articles of personal value which in our light-hearted optimism we had thought to leave miles behind us on our dash across the moving ice to safety." [S-E18]

Later they did make that second attempt. At one point the ice ahead proved impassable, and, to the intense disappointment of all, they had to retreat half a mile to get onto a stronger floe and camp. They marched for seven days and gained seven and a half miles in a direct line; at that rate it would take over three hundred days to reach the land to the west, and they had food for only forty-two days. So they camped once more on the floe. They called the new camp Patience Camp and lived there for nearly three and a half months. [S-E7][S-E21][S-E24][S-E8]

## Making a Stop That Carries a Next Step

One reading, and it is the author's, is that Ocean Camp was not the first time he chose to stop. On the march's second morning, with snow too thick to see a path, he decided not to strike camp; when it cleared and a good floe turned up, they moved on after lunch. Both decisions turned on whether the way ahead could be made. Nor was camping to wait a new idea. Before Ocean Camp he had already written down the option of camping on the nearest heavy floe and awaiting the outward drift of the pack to open water, noting that it would also lessen the peril of the ice splitting under them. He wrote that at their "first camp" the ice had split twice in one night. His narrative indicates that this was the camp beside the ship: at about 7 p.m., after the tents were up, the ice split beneath them and the camp had to move to a bigger floe about 200 yards away, just beyond the bow of the ship; at midnight a crack ran right through the camp. That was before the march began, and it is not the same as the crack about 20 feet from the camp on the march's first night. But he felt sure it was right to attempt a march first, noting that there was a psychological aspect too: the men would be better off feeling they were on their way to land than simply sitting and waiting for the drift. Ocean Camp, after four days of hard effort, was a return to that option he had set aside. The camp went onto a more solid floe, and the reasoning carried a specific hope: leads opening nearby, boats in the water, land to the north-west. A second attempt was to follow once conditions improved. [S-E19][S-E13][S-E7][S-E26][S-E27][S-E12]

One more thing is worth noticing, and again it is the author's reading. Once they stopped, they could go back to the wreck again and again and retrieve clothing, food and some few articles of personal value; Shackleton later said it was in "light-hearted optimism" that they had thought to leave those articles miles behind on their dash across the moving ice to safety. He also decided they would live almost entirely on seals and penguins so that the sledging rations could be kept for the boat journey ahead. On this reading, stopping was not only a halt to the drain; it was time to gather their resources again. [S-E18][S-E17]

A second reading, and it is the author's, concerns the standard behind the choices. The sovereigns were thrown away and the photographs were kept, and he did give his reasons. He admits he rather grudged the two-pound allowance per man, so anxious was he to keep weights down, but some personal belongings could fairly be regarded as indispensable: the journey might be a long one, and there might be a winter in improvised quarters on an inhospitable coast at the other end. In his words, "A man under such conditions needs something to occupy his thoughts, some tangible memento of his home and people beyond the seas." He also tore out the Bible's fly-leaf and a page of Job. The author's understanding is that the limit screened for more than weight: on the ice the gold coins could buy nothing, while the photographs could bring home to mind. [S-E4][S-E5][S-E20]

Then there are alternatives the author raises for the sake of argument. The party could have been split in two, one half keeping up a minimal reconnaissance while the other held the camp and conserved its strength. Or a few men might have gone ahead lightly loaded, to feel out the leads. Both would have meant splitting the party. They are listed here only to show that the decision of that day was not the only possibility, though it did match the conditions in front of them. [S-E7]

## Two Scenarios Today

> Scenario one: a start-up's fundraising has fallen through and the original plan no longer stands. At the first all-hands meeting, the most useful thing the founder can do may be what the new task at the start of this piece did: state the new goal in one sentence, what has to be achieved now and how you will know.

> Scenario two: creative work of your own. You have been submitting steadily, the gaps between rejections keep stretching, and you have begun to wonder whether forcing out words every day is worth it. This is the moment for the two-pound sort. Keep what is genuinely necessary — reading, notes, revision. Drop what looks respectable but only slows you down. Then give the waiting a review date: in two months, you check the signals and decide again. Stopping does not mean putting down the pen. It means no longer trading six measures of effort for one measure of progress.

## Where These Claims Stop

Everything in this piece comes from Shackleton's own narrative, read here through the OCR text of the 1920 printing: the account of a participant who led the party. No other member's diary has been brought in for comparison. His narrative does report the men's state: they were all exhausted; at the second setback, when they had to retreat, there was intense disappointment among all of them; and an apathy that seemed to take hold of some of the men soon passed. These reports, too, come from his narrative. Whether anyone else in the camp agreed with the Ocean Camp decision is not in the evidence, and this piece does not put further feelings in their mouths. The line about the sip of water comes from an unnamed member's diary, as quoted by Shackleton. [S-E15][S-E24][S-E25]

The text is machine-read, and it carries errors. Quotations are taken only from clean stretches; a few damaged words are reproduced without quotation marks. The main figures — 1,800 miles, 281 days, 346 miles, two pounds, a ton, one mile, six miles, one and a half miles, two months, three and a half months — come from his account and have not been checked against any other record. Nor does this piece tell what happened after Patience Camp; that falls outside the evidence gathered here.

## Think about it

You lead a small team on a project with a delivery deadline. Weeks of rising input have moved the key metric by only a little. At the same time, an external condition — a partner's window, a season, an approval — may improve within a month or two. Do you keep adding people and pushing, or gather your resources and wait for the window, then make a second, concentrated push?

**What signal would tell you to turn continued effort into a deliberate wait? And what review date do you set for yourself?**
