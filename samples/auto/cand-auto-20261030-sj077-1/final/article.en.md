# Turning Back at the Yimen Gate: The Year Handan Was Besieged, and the Man Who Would Not Give It Away Free

*In the twentieth year of King Anxi of Wei, Qin's armies closed around Handan and the king of Wei sent General Jin Bi to relieve Zhao; Lord Pingyuan's envoys complained that Wei's relief had not come. The Wei prince resolved to go and die at the Qin army; after taking his leave at the Yimen gate and riding off, he turned back, and an old gatekeeper told him to his face that this was meat thrown to a starving tiger. Here is how he worked the problem when no option was clean — what he prepared, what it cost, and what he admitted he owed.*

## Messengers Who Would Not Stop Coming

In the twentieth year of King Anxi of Wei, after Qin had broken Zhao's army at Changping, its armies pressed on and encircled Handan. The king of Wei sent General Jin Bi with a hundred thousand troops to relieve Zhao. Envoys from Lord Pingyuan arrived in Wei one after another — caps of office and carriage canopies in an unbroken line — reproaching the Wei prince: Lord Pingyuan said he had joined himself to the prince by marriage because he valued the prince's high sense of duty and believed him a man who would rush to others in distress; now Handan was within days of surrendering to Qin, and Wei's relief still had not come — where, then, was that readiness to rush to others in distress? They added, in translation: had he no pity for his own sister? The prince's elder sister was Lord Pingyuan's wife. [SJ0771-E9][SJ0771-E11]

At this point the prince had three thousand retainers but no appointment to the governance of the state. The plan he settled on was to go and die at the Qin army. On his way out of the city, passing the Yimen gate, he took his leave of Hou Ying, the gatekeeper. [SJ0771-E2][SJ0771-E3][SJ0771-E13]

## Three Thousand Retainers and One Gatekeeper

The Wei prince was known in Wei for keeping retainers. The Grand Historian writes, in translation, that he was humane and humble toward men of talent, treating every scholar, worthy or not, with courtesy and respect, never presuming on wealth and rank — so that he had three thousand retainers, and men of ability from a radius of several thousand li competed to attach themselves to him. They were not mere pageantry. One day, while he was playing a board game with the king of Wei, beacon fires on the northern frontier signaled a Zhao raid about to cross the border. The prince said the king of Zhao was out hunting, nothing more. Word later came from the north confirming exactly that. Asked how he knew, he explained that among his retainers was a man who could ferret out the king of Zhao's private business. [SJ0771-E2][SJ0771-E3]

One reading is that the episode cuts both ways. His intelligence was proven — and the price went straight onto the ledger: in translation, from then on the king of Wei feared the prince's ability and dared not entrust him with the government of the state. The more capable he appeared, the less his king would hand him. By the time Handan cried for help he held no charge of the governance of the state, and raising troops was out of the question (the author's inference). [SJ0771-E3]

The other man the prince honored kept the Yimen, a gate of Daliang. Hou Ying was seventy, poor, and gatekeeper there. The prince called on him and wanted to make him a handsome gift, but he would not accept: in translation, he said he had kept himself and his conduct clean for decades and would not take the prince's money just because a gatekeeper's life left him poor. So the prince laid on a feast for his assembled guests, and when everyone was seated he drove out himself with his carriage and escort, leaving the seat of honor at the left of the carriage empty, to fetch Hou Ying in person. The old man turned up in his shabby clothes and worn cap, climbed straight into the seat of honor without yielding it, while the prince held the reins ever more respectfully. At home he seated Hou Ying in the place of honor and introduced him one by one to the assembled guests. The whole company was astonished. [SJ0771-E4][SJ0771-E5][SJ0771-E6]

Hou Ying later spelled out the game he had been playing. He had deliberately left the lord's carriage standing in the marketplace so that passers-by could watch: the longer the lord waited, the more deferential he became — and so, he said in translation, the townsfolk all took him for a petty man, and the lord for a true elder who could humble himself before men of talent. He also recommended a butcher named Zhu Hai, calling him, in translation, a worthy man whom the world did not know. The prince called on Zhu Hai several times; the butcher never came to return the courtesies, which the prince at that point simply found odd. In the author's view, all of these seemingly idle moves came due in the year Handan was besieged. [SJ0771-E7][SJ0771-E8]

## From a Death Ride to Seizing an Army

Lord Pingyuan's envoys left nothing to the imagination in Wei: with no relief army in sight, where was the lord's famed righteousness? He resolved to go and die against the Qin army. [SJ0771-E11][SJ0771-E13]

That day he rode out, passed the Yimen gate, stopped to see Hou Ying and told him the whole plan — going to die at the Qin army — then took his leave and set off. Hou Ying said only, in translation, that the lord should push on, and that this old servant could not follow him. A few li on, the lord felt displeased — the narrator's own phrase — and said, in translation, that he had treated Hou Ying handsomely in every way, as all the world knew; now he was going to his death, and Hou Ying had not half a word to see him off — had he fallen short somewhere? So he turned his carriage around and went back to question Hou Ying. [SJ0771-E13]

In the author's reading, that turn is the pivot of the whole story. Hou Ying put it to his face, in translation: with disaster upon him and no other plan, the lord meant to throw himself at the Qin army — like throwing meat to a starving tiger; what would it accomplish? Nothing gained — and what use, then, are retainers? He added that the lord had treated him well, and he had not seen the lord off; from this, he said, he had known the lord would resent it and come back. [SJ0771-E14]

Hou Ying's plan, as he laid it out: Jin Bi's army tally — a tiger tally — was normally kept in the king of Wei's bedchamber, and Lady Ruji, the king's favorite, came and went there and was able to steal it. He went on: her father had been murdered; for three years she had sought revenge, and no one, from the king down, had been able to avenge him. She had wept to the prince, who sent a retainer to cut off her enemy's head and presented it to her respectfully. Hou Ying said she was ready to die for the prince and would refuse him nothing; only, in translation, she had not yet had the opportunity. If the prince would but ask, she would certainly agree. Then, with the tiger tally in hand, he could seize Jin Bi's army, relieve Zhao in the north and drive Qin back in the west — in translation, an achievement to rank with the Five Hegemons. [SJ0771-E15]

The plan covered only the first half; the second half was the bad case. Hou Ying warned, in translation, that a general in the field does not accept every order from his sovereign: even with the tallies matching, if Jin Bi withheld the army and sent to the king instead, the whole enterprise would be in danger. So take the butcher Zhu Hai along: if Jin Bi obeyed, excellent; if not, Zhu Hai could be made to strike him. The prince wept on hearing it; in translation, he said Jin Bi was a battle-seasoned old general who he feared would not obey, and then he would have to be killed — that was why he wept, not because he feared death. What he cried for was the killing of his own country's veteran commander, not the risk to himself. Zhu Hai said he was a butcher who wielded the knife in the marketplace; he had never returned the prince's visits because he thought petty courtesies were of no use — but now that the prince was in need, this was his moment to give his life. Hou Ying, who had already said at the gate that he could not follow, here only asked to count the days of the journey, so that on the day the prince reached Jin Bi's camp he would face north and cut his own throat, to see him off. [SJ0771-E17][SJ0771-E18][SJ0771-E19][SJ0771-E20]

Zhu Hai, with a forty-catty iron mallet in his sleeve, struck Jin Bi dead. The prince then took command of Jin Bi's army and immediately issued an order to the troops, here in translation: where father and son were both serving, the father should go home; where brothers were both serving, the elder should go home; an only son with no brothers should go home and support his parents. He selected eighty thousand men and advanced against the Qin army. [SJ0771-E22]

The Qin army broke off and withdrew. Handan was relieved, Zhao preserved. The king of Zhao and Lord Pingyuan came out to the border to receive him in person, Lord Pingyuan carrying the quiver and arrow-case and leading the way. The king of Zhao bowed twice and said, in translation, that of worthies since ancient times none had been the prince's equal. At that moment Lord Pingyuan dared not compare himself with anyone. [SJ0771-E23]

King Xiaocheng of Zhao, grateful that the prince had seized Jin Bi's army on a forged royal order and saved Zhao, took counsel with Lord Pingyuan about granting him five cities. The prince's reaction, in the narrator's phrasing (here in translation), was a look of pride and self-congratulation. A retainer told him: some things must not be forgotten, others must be. Kindness done to the prince he should remember; kindness the prince had done to others, he should forget. And besides, forging the king of Wei's order and seizing Jin Bi's army to save Zhao was, in translation, a service to Zhao but not the act of a loyal minister to Wei — to swagger over such a credit was beneath him. The prince at once blamed himself, looking as if he had nowhere to hide. [SJ0771-E24]

The king of Wei was furious that the prince had stolen his tally and killed Jin Bi on a forged order, and the prince knew it. Having driven off Qin and saved Zhao, he had a general lead the army back to Wei and stayed behind in Zhao with his retainers. The part of the rescue of Zhao in which he stole the tally, seized the army and killed Jin Bi carried no royal authorization. [SJ0771-E25]

## Three Undignified Spots

In the author's reading, take the whole affair apart and the prince is never choosing between good and bad; what stood before him were two courses of action, both dirty: ride to die at the Qin army (which Hou Ying called throwing meat to a starving tiger), or forge an order and seize the army (proposed by Hou Ying; a wound to his own state). The author's inference is that Hou Ying's line about throwing meat to a starving tiger is what forced him to trade courage for a plan. The author's counterfactual: had he not turned back, he would most likely have died in front of the Qin lines, Handan might never have been relieved, and there would have been no army marching against Qin afterward. [SJ0771-E14]

The second undignified spot is writing the bad case into the plan. Hou Ying's reminder that a general in the field need not accept every order put Zhu Hai at his side with a mallet up his sleeve: if Jin Bi complied, well; if not, strike. The author's inference: the hard part here was not daring. The prince's response on hearing the plan was tears — he said he feared Jin Bi would not obey, and that he would then have to be killed, and that he was not afraid to die. In the outcome he took on a debt to his own side — killing his country's veteran commander, stealing the king's tally, angering the king — and he went through with it anyway. Whether he weighed this beforehand goes unrecorded; the only thing on the record is that answer. [SJ0771-E17][SJ0771-E18]

The third is the accounting afterward. The retainer's words — in translation, a service to Zhao but not the act of a loyal minister to Wei — keep both columns straight: the credit belongs to Zhao, the debt to Wei. The source records only that he blamed himself on the spot, and nothing of any argument; it also records that, once Qin was driven off and Zhao saved, he sent the army home to Wei and stayed in Zhao with his retainers. The author infers that this acknowledged the debt through departure (the source records only the act of staying in Zhao, and does not say whether it came before or after the five-city offer and the self-reproach). One reading: he first showed a look of self-congratulation, then blamed himself at once when rebuked, and this turn chimes with his staying in Zhao. [SJ0771-E23][SJ0771-E24][SJ0771-E25]

## How to Use This Today

> The commonest modern version: a team sees a clear risk, while the people with authority will not move. You hold a piece of hard evidence and you know a capable person. Before you start admiring your own nerve, put Hou Ying's question to your plan: is this move solving the problem, or throwing meat to a starving tiger? If all it does is get you out there to say your piece and offend everyone while the risk sits exactly where it was, that is a free throwaway.

> If you decide to act, write "the other side may not cooperate" into the plan: who steps in under which circumstances, who covers the worst outcome, what record stays behind. And when it works and the celebration starts, do not rush to stand in the middle. The process you bypassed, the person who signed for you, the superior you left out of the loop — all of that is an account. Saying it yourself first — a service here, a disloyalty there — is far more dignified than waiting for someone else to say it for you.

## Where Not to Take It at Face Value

Start with the sources. This piece rests on a single source: the Biography of the Wei Prince in the Records of the Grand Historian, with no corroboration to set against it. The dialogue and the proposed scheme — Hou Ying's plan to steal the tally, Zhu Hai's reply — are the historian's rendering and need not be verbatim. The prince's displeasure, self-congratulation and self-blame, as the narrative describes them, are narrative labels, not recorded psychology. The year is given as the twentieth of King Anxi of Wei, as the source has it, with no conversion to the common era. [SJ0771-E13][SJ0771-E24]

The detail most worth doubting: the figure of eighty thousand picked men appears here alone, with no corroboration; how the tallies were actually matched in Jin Bi's camp is not recorded in the evidence; Hou Ying's pledge to cut his own throat to see the prince off is recorded only as a single sentence — whether he carried it out, and why, is not in the evidence; and how Hou Ying's scheme to steal the tally was carried out is not recorded in the evidence. [SJ0771-E22][SJ0771-E20][SJ0771-E15]

## Think about it

The elevator in your building has broken down several times. You have an inspection report stating plainly that the main control board is dangerously defective. The property manager, afraid of offending the developer, refuses to take the elevator out of service for repairs and will only agree to "keep watching it." Among the residents is an elevator technician you know who is willing to help — but going through the proper approval route would take a month.

**In a situation where those above will not move and those below are heading for an accident, would you bypass the property manager and act directly? Before acting, what would you prepare in advance to handle the step where "the other side does not cooperate"? And once it is done, how do you account to the property manager and to the rules you bypassed?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The defect is real, and the written inspection report can be kept as evidence
The technician is willing to help, but has no authorization; acting without it is a violation
The property manager will not admit fault, and will not take responsibility for you
- **What you could do**: Judge the nature of the act first: shutting it down by force, or barging in — is that Hou Ying's "meat thrown to a starving tiger"? If nobody cooperates and the report is not accepted, the risk has not shrunk at all
Prepare for the bad case: if the technician arrives and the property manager blocks the work, who signs, who witnesses, what records are preserved
Lay out the costs clearly: the list of people who might be held responsible, and who covers the worst outcome
Afterwards, do not claim credit; volunteer the admission that you bypassed procedure, and hand the repair records and the inspection report to the property manager for filing
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's proposed alternative: publish the report to all the owners first, turning the risk into a collective decision and using shared pressure instead of personal daring. The cost is slowness — it may come too late; the benefit is that no single person has to carry the account of "forging the order and killing the general."
