# Turning Back at the Yimen Gate: The Year Handan Was Besieged, and the Man Who Would Not Give It Away Free

*In the twentieth year of King Anxi of Wei, Qin's armies closed around Handan and the king of Wei sent General Jin Bi to relieve Zhao, but the army never reached the city (retold from the briefing). The lord of Wei was set on marching his retainers to their deaths; after riding out and turning back at the Yimen gate, an old gatekeeper cut him to his face: that is meat thrown to a starving tiger. Here is how he worked the problem when no option was clean — what he prepared, what it cost, and what he admitted he owed.*

## Messengers Who Would Not Stop Coming

In the twentieth year of King Anxi of Wei, after Qin had annihilated Zhao's main force at Changping, its armies pressed on and encircled Handan. The king of Wei sent General Jin Bi with a hundred thousand troops to relieve Zhao, but the army never reached the city (retold from the briefing). Instead, envoys from Lord Pingyuan arrived in Daliang one after another — caps of office and carriage canopies in an unbroken line — each message harsher than the last: the relief Wei had promised still had not come, and was Lord Xinling not the man who prided himself on rushing to others in distress? The last message added a barb: "And have you no pity for your own sister?" Lord Xinling's elder sister was Lord Pingyuan's wife. [SJ0771-E9][SJ0771-E11]

Worse still, the king of Wei refused to send any more troops (retold from the briefing), and Lord Xinling had three thousand retainers but no military authority of his own. The plan he settled on amounted to suicide: take the retainers and fight the Qin army to mutual destruction. Before leaving the city, he went to say goodbye to an old man who kept one of the gates. [SJ0771-E13][SJ0771-E14]

## Three Thousand Retainers and One Gatekeeper

Lord Xinling's standing in Wei rested half on his retainers. The Grand Historian writes that "he was humane and humble toward men of talent, treating every scholar, worthy or not, with courtesy and respect," never presuming on wealth and rank — until he had three thousand retainers and men of ability from a radius of several thousand li competed to attach themselves to him. They were not mere pageantry. One day, while he was playing a board game with the king of Wei, beacon fires on the northern frontier signaled a Zhao raid about to cross the border. The lord said the king of Zhao was out hunting, nothing more. Word later came from the north confirming exactly that. Asked how he knew, he explained that among his retainers was a man who could ferret out the king of Zhao's private business. [SJ0771-E2][SJ0771-E3]

Read the episode both ways. His intelligence was proven — and the price went straight onto the ledger: "From then on the king of Wei feared Lord Xinling's ability and dared not entrust him with the government of the state." The more capable he appeared, the less his king would hand him. By the time Handan cried for help he could not command even a detachment: plenty of people, no authority at all. [SJ0771-E3]

The other man Lord Xinling honored kept the Yimen — the eastern gate of Daliang. Hou Ying was seventy, poor, and gatekeeper there. Lord Xinling called on him and wanted to make him a handsome gift, but he would not accept: he had "kept himself and his conduct clean for decades," he said, "and would not take the lord's money just because a gatekeeper's life left him poor." So Lord Xinling laid on a feast for his assembled guests, and when everyone was seated he drove out himself with his carriage and escort, leaving the seat of honor at the left of the carriage empty, to fetch Hou Ying in person. The old man turned up in his shabby clothes and worn cap, climbed straight into the seat of honor without yielding it — testing his host — while Lord Xinling held the reins ever more respectfully. At home he seated Hou Ying in the place of honor and introduced him one by one to the assembled guests. The whole company was astonished. [SJ0771-E4][SJ0771-E5][SJ0771-E6]

Hou Ying later spelled out the game he had been playing. He had deliberately left the lord's carriage standing in the marketplace so that passers-by could watch: the longer the lord waited, the more deferential he became — and so, he said, "the townsfolk all took me for a petty man, and the lord for a true elder who could humble himself before men of talent." He also recommended a butcher named Zhu Hai: "This is a worthy man; the world does not know him." Lord Xinling called on Zhu Hai several times; the butcher never came to return the courtesies, which the lord at that point simply found odd. All of these seemingly idle moves came due in the year Handan was besieged. [SJ0771-E7][SJ0771-E8]

## From a Death Ride to Seizing an Army

Lord Pingyuan's envoys left nothing to the imagination in Daliang: with no relief army in sight, where was the lord's famed righteousness? The first thing Lord Xinling could do was still to appeal to the king. The king would not budge, and neither the retainers nor the professional persuaders sent to argue the case changed his mind (this detail is retold from the briefing, not quoted from the source). That left only himself: roughly a hundred chariots and horsemen, intending to lead his retainers straight into the Qin army and die with Zhao (this detail, too, is retold from the briefing, not quoted from the source). [SJ0771-E11][SJ0771-E13]

That day he rode out, passed the Yimen gate, stopped to see Hou Ying and told him the whole plan — going to die at the Qin army — then took his leave and set off. Hou Ying said only: "Push on, my lord; this old servant cannot follow you." A few li on, the lord felt a gnawing unhappiness — the narrator's own phrase — reasoning that he had treated Hou Ying handsomely, as all the world knew, and now, going to his death, the old man had not half a word to see him off. Had he fallen short somewhere? So he "turned his carriage around and went back to question Hou Ying." [SJ0771-E13]

That turn is the pivot of the whole story. Hou Ying put it to his face: "With disaster upon you and no plan at all, you mean to throw yourself at the Qin army — that is like throwing meat to a starving tiger. What would it accomplish?" Nothing gained, and the retainers squandered besides. He added that he had counted on exactly this: knowing how well the lord treated him, going to die without so much as a parting word would make the lord "resent it and come back." The author's reading: he had foreseen that the lord would turn back. [SJ0771-E14]

Hou Ying's plan: the tally that authorized Jin Bi to move troops — a tiger tally — was normally kept in the king of Wei's bedchamber, and Lady Ruji, the king's favorite, came and went there and was able to steal it. Her father had been murdered; for three years she had sought revenge, and from the king on down no one could get it for her. She had wept to Lord Xinling, who sent a retainer to cut off her enemy's head and presented it to her respectfully. The source has her ready to die for Lord Xinling and to refuse him nothing; only "she had no way to repay him." If the lord would but ask, she would certainly agree. Then, with the tiger tally in hand, seize Jin Bi's army, relieve Zhao in the north and drive Qin back in the west: "that is an achievement to rank with the Five Hegemons." [SJ0771-E15]

The plan covered only the first half; the second half was the bad case. Hou Ying warned that "a general in the field is not bound by every order from his sovereign": even with the tallies matching, if Jin Bi withheld the army and sent to the king instead, the whole enterprise would be in danger. So take the butcher Zhu Hai along: "If Jin Bi obeys, excellent; if not, you may have him strike." Lord Xinling wept on hearing it: "Jin Bi is a battle-seasoned old general; I fear he will not obey, and then he must be killed. That is why I weep. Do you imagine I fear death?" What he cried for was the killing of his own country's veteran commander, not the risk to himself. Zhu Hai said he was a butcher who slapped the knife in the marketplace; he had never returned the lord's visits because "petty courtesies were of no use" — but "now that the lord is in a tight spot, this is the hour to spend my life." Hou Ying said he was too old to go, and asked to count the days of the journey, so that "on the day you reach Jin Bi's camp I shall face north and cut my own throat, to see you off." [SJ0771-E17][SJ0771-E18][SJ0771-E19][SJ0771-E20]

At Jin Bi's camp, Lord Xinling announced that he came on the king's orders to replace Jin Bi. The tallies matched, but Jin Bi grew suspicious and refused to hand over his command (this episode is retold from the briefing). Zhu Hai produced a forty-catty iron mallet from his sleeve and killed him. Lord Xinling then took command of Jin Bi's army and immediately issued an order to the troops: "Fathers and sons both serving: the father goes home. Brothers both serving: the elder goes home. An only son with no brothers: go home and support your parents." He selected eighty thousand men and advanced against the Qin army. [SJ0771-E22]

The Qin army broke off and withdrew. Handan was relieved, Zhao preserved. The king of Zhao and Lord Pingyuan came out to the border to receive him in person, Lord Pingyuan carrying the quiver and arrow-case and leading the way. The king of Zhao bowed twice and said: "Of worthies since ancient times, none has been the lord's equal." At that moment Lord Pingyuan dared not compare himself with anyone. [SJ0771-E23]

King Xiaocheng of Zhao, grateful that Lord Xinling had overridden the general and seized the army to save Zhao, took counsel with Lord Pingyuan about granting him five cities. The lord's reaction, in the narrator's phrasing, was "a look of pride and self-congratulation." A retainer told him: some things must not be forgotten, others must be. Kindness done to the lord he should remember; kindness the lord has done to others, he should forget. And besides, overriding the king's order and seizing the army to save Zhao was "a service to Zhao, but not the act of a loyal minister to Wei" — to swagger over such a credit was beneath him. The lord at once blamed himself, looking as if he had nowhere to hide. [SJ0771-E24]

The king of Wei was furious that his tally had been stolen and Jin Bi killed on a forged order, and Lord Xinling knew it. Having driven off Qin and saved Zhao, he sent the generals back to Wei with the army and stayed behind in Zhao with his retainers. From first to last the rescue of Zhao had carried no royal authorization, so its ending kept him out of the Wei court altogether. [SJ0771-E25]

## Three Undignified Spots

Take the whole affair apart and the lord is never choosing between good and bad; he is ranking options that are all dirty: appeal to the king (a dead end), go die alone (useless), forge an order and seize the army (wound his own state). The author's inference is that Hou Ying's line about throwing meat to a starving tiger is what forced him to trade courage for a plan. The author's counterfactual: had he not turned back, he would most likely have died with his hundred-odd chariots of retainers in front of the Qin lines, Handan might never have been relieved, and there would have been no army marching against Qin afterward. [SJ0771-E14]

The second undignified spot is writing the bad case into the plan. Hou Ying's reminder that a general in the field need not accept every order put Zhu Hai at his side with a mallet up his sleeve: if Jin Bi complied, well; if not, strike. The author's inference: the hard part here was not daring. The lord's response on hearing the plan was tears — he said Jin Bi had to be killed, and that he was not afraid to die. In the outcome he took on a debt to his own side — killing his country's veteran commander, stealing the king's tally, breaking with the king — and he went through with it anyway. Whether he weighed this beforehand goes unrecorded; the only thing on the record is that answer. [SJ0771-E17][SJ0771-E18]

The third is the accounting afterward. The retainer's words — "a service to Zhao, but not the act of a loyal minister to Wei" — keep both columns straight: the credit belongs to Zhao, the debt to Wei. His response was not to argue but to blame himself on the spot, then send the army home and stay in Zhao, acknowledging the debt through departure. One reading: his refusal to claim credit chimes with the self-blame that followed — the welcome at the border came first, and the retainer's rebuke, the self-reproach, and staying in Zhao came after. [SJ0771-E23][SJ0771-E24][SJ0771-E25]

## How to Use This Today

> The commonest modern version: a team sees a clear risk, while the people with authority freeze for fear of a bigger opponent. You hold a piece of hard evidence and you know a capable person. Before you start admiring your own nerve, put Hou Ying's question to your plan: is this move solving the problem, or throwing meat to a starving tiger? If all it does is get you out there to say your piece and offend everyone while the risk sits exactly where it was, that is a free throwaway.

> If you decide to act, write "the other side may not cooperate" into the plan: who steps in under which circumstances, who covers the worst outcome, what record stays behind. And when it works and the celebration starts, do not rush to stand in the middle. The process you bypassed, the person who signed for you, the superior you left out of the loop — all of that is an account. Saying it yourself first — a service here, a disloyalty there — is far more dignified than waiting for someone else to say it for you.

## Where Not to Take It at Face Value

Start with the sources. This piece rests on a single one: one account in the "Hereditary House of Lord Wei" in the Records of the Grand Historian, with no corroboration to set against it. The whispered schemes and dialogue — Hou Ying's secret counsel, Zhu Hai's reply — are the historian's rendering and need not be verbatim. The lord's "unease," "self-congratulation," and "blaming himself" are narrative labels, not recorded psychology. The year is given as the twentieth of King Anxi of Wei, as the source has it, with no conversion to the common era. [SJ0771-E13][SJ0771-E24]

The detail most worth doubting: the figure of "eighty thousand picked men" appears here alone, with no corroboration; how the tallies were verified in Jin Bi's camp and how the affair wound up after the killing of the general go unrecorded; Hou Ying's motive in cutting his own throat, and Lady Ruji's fate after stealing the tally, come down to an act and a single sentence, with nothing further. In addition, several key turns retold here — the king sending men to stop Jin Bi and hold the army at Ye in wait-and-see, the lord gathering chariots to go and die against Qin, Lady Ruji stealing the tally, Jin Bi growing suspicious when the tallies matched — rest on extracts that have not been checked word for word, and are retold rather than quoted. ## Think about it

The elevator in your building has broken down several times. You have an inspection report stating plainly that the main control board is dangerously defective. The property manager, afraid of offending the developer, refuses to take the elevator out of service for repairs and will only agree to "keep watching it." Among the residents is an elevator technician you know who is willing to help — but going through the proper approval route would take a month.

**In a situation where those above will not move and those below are heading for an accident, would you bypass the property manager and act directly? Before acting, what would you prepare in advance to handle the step where "the other side does not cooperate"? And once it is done, how do you account to the property manager and to the rules you bypassed?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The defect is real, and the written inspection report can be kept as evidence
The technician is willing to help, but has no authorization; acting without it is a violation
The property manager will not admit fault, and will not take responsibility for you
- **What you could do**: Judge the nature of the act first: shutting it down by force, or barging in — is that Hou Ying's "meat thrown to a starving tiger"? If nobody cooperates and the report is not accepted, the risk has not shrunk at all
Prepare for the bad case: if the technician arrives and the property manager blocks the work, who signs, who witnesses, what records are preserved
Lay out the costs clearly: the list of people who might be held responsible, and who covers the worst outcome
Afterwards, do not claim credit; volunteer the admission that you bypassed procedure, and hand the repair records and the inspection report to the property manager for filing
- **Another reasonable choice**: The author's proposed alternative: publish the report to all the owners first, turning the risk into a collective decision and using shared pressure instead of personal daring. The cost is slowness — it may come too late; the benefit is that no single person has to carry the account of "forging the order and killing the general."
