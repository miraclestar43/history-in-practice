# Saving Zhao with No Clean Option

*When your own king halts the relief army halfway, what can a prince with no troops do?*

unscheduled · Ancient China · cand-auto-20261031-sj077-2 rev 1

## A Turn at the East Gate

Prince Wuji of Wei rode out of Daliang, the Wei capital, with a hundred-odd chariots and horsemen, intending to hurl himself at the Qin army and die alongside the state of Zhao. Passing the Yimen, the eastern gate of the city, he stopped to see Hou Ying, the aged gatekeeper there, and told him the plan outright. Hou Ying saw him off with a single line — "Push on, my lord; I am old and cannot follow you". The column had gone a few li when the Records of the Grand Historian has Wuji "troubled in his heart"; he turned the chariots around and came back to question Hou Ying. [SJ0771-E13][SJ0771-E27]

The difficulty was plain to see. Qin forces had invested Handan, the Zhao capital. King Anxi of Wei, Wuji's own king, had already sent General Jin Pi with a hundred thousand troops to relieve Zhao, then ordered him to halt and camp at Ye and watch. Wuji had no army, only a hundred-odd chariots and a band of retainers. Riding out meant dying for nothing. Staying meant Handan could fall any day — and his own sister was the wife of Lord Pingyuan of Zhao. [SJ0771-E26][SJ0771-E27][SJ0771-E9]

## A Relief Army That Stopped Halfway

King Zhao of Qin, having destroyed Zhao's army at Changping, marched on and laid siege to Handan. Wuji's sister was the wife of Lord Pingyuan. She wrote letter after letter to the king of Wei and to Wuji begging for help, and the king of Wei sent General Jin Pi with a hundred thousand troops to rescue Zhao. [SJ0771-E9]

Qin's envoy put the matter bluntly to the king of Wei: Handan would fall within days, and whichever of the other states dared to come to Zhao's relief, Qin would turn on it first once Zhao was finished. The king was frightened. He sent men to stop Jin Pi and had the great army camp at Ye — "in name a rescue of Zhao, in fact holding both sides and waiting to see which way things broke." [SJ0771-E26]

Lord Pingyuan's envoys now arrived in Wei in an unbroken stream, official carriages crowding the road, and reproached the prince in their master's name: I attached myself to this marriage because I took the prince for a man of high principle, one who hurries to relieve another's distress. Now Handan may surrender to Qin any day and Wei's rescue has not come — where is that principle? "Even if you think little of me, have you no pity for your own sister?" [SJ0771-E11]

Wuji petitioned the king of Wei several times, and his retainers, debaters and persuaders alike, argued the case from every angle. The king of Wei feared Qin and never relented. Wuji judged that he would never get troops out of the king, and decided he would not "live on alone and let Zhao perish." So he gathered his retainers, readied a hundred-odd chariots, and meant to lead his men into the Qin lines to die with Zhao. That was the plan he rode out with. [SJ0771-E27]

Wuji's reputation rested on his habit of humbling himself before men of talent: humane and courteous to scholars whether able or not, never letting wealth and rank make him proud. Men competed to join him from a country several thousand li around, and three thousand retainers ate at his gate. Once, while he and the king were playing a board game, beacons flared on the northern frontier: "Zhao raiders are crossing the border." The king put down the game and wanted to summon his ministers. Wuji said it was merely the king of Zhao out hunting, not a raid. Before long word came from the north that it had indeed been a hunt. A retainer of mine, Wuji said, has a way of learning the king of Zhao's private business and reports it to me. From then on, the Records says, the king "dreaded the prince's ability, and did not dare entrust him with affairs of state." [SJ0771-E2][SJ0771-E3]

Wei had a recluse named Hou Ying, seventy years old and poor, a gatekeeper at the Yimen. Wuji sought him out and tried to present him with a handsome gift. Hou Ying would not take it: he had kept himself and his conduct clean for decades, he said, and would not accept the prince's money simply because gatekeeping left him poor. So Wuji laid on a feast for all his retainers, went out with his carriage and escort, left the seat of honor on the left of the chariot empty, and fetched Hou Ying himself. Hou Ying, in his shabby robe and cap, climbed straight up into that seat without a word of demur — a test of Wuji, this was — and Wuji took the reins and grew only more respectful. Hou Ying then got down to see his friend Zhu Hai, a butcher in the market, and deliberately stood talking with him for a long time, watching Wuji out of the corner of his eye. Meanwhile Wei's generals, ministers, royal kinsmen and guests filled the prince's hall, waiting for him to begin the feast, and Wuji's own riders cursed Hou Ying under their breath. Wuji's face only grew milder; his manner never changed. At the house Wuji showed Hou Ying to the seat of honor and presented him to the assembled guests one by one, and the guests were astonished. Hou Ying later spelled out his reasoning: he had acted this way to make the prince's name as a man who humbles himself before scholars. As a result, the townsfolk took Hou Ying for a lout and Wuji for a true elder who can humble himself before scholars. When the feast broke up, Hou Ying became one of Wuji's most honored guests. [SJ0771-E4][SJ0771-E5][SJ0771-E6][SJ0771-E7][SJ0771-E38][SJ0771-E39]

## The Tiger Tally, the Iron Mace, and Eighty Thousand Men

When Wuji came back to the Yimen, Hou Ying told him that charging the Qin army with his retainers would be like throwing meat to a starving tiger — it would accomplish nothing. The prince had treated him generously, he said, and when the prince left without being seen off, Hou Ying had known the prince would resent it and come back. Then Hou Ying laid out a plan. Jin Pi's command tally was normally kept in the king of Wei's bedchamber. Lady Ru, the king's favorite, came and went in the bedchamber freely and could steal it out. Hou Ying spelled out why she would. Her father had been murdered; for three years she had put up money to see her father's death avenged, and no one, the king included, had been able to bring it off. She had wept to Wuji, who sent a retainer to cut off her enemy's head and present it to her. Hou Ying's judgment was that Lady Ru would gladly die for Wuji and had simply never had the chance to serve him; one word from the prince and she would agree. Get the tally, take over Jin Pi's army, go north to rescue Zhao and push Qin back from the west — "an achievement worthy of the Five Hegemons." [SJ0771-E14][SJ0771-E15]

Wuji followed the plan and asked Lady Ru. She did steal Jin Pi's tally and give it to him. [SJ0771-E28]

Hou Ying then warned him about the step that could ruin everything. "A general in the field need not obey every command from his sovereign." Even if the tally matched, Jin Pi might refuse to hand over the army and send to the king instead, and then the whole thing was in danger. Hou Ying told him to take the butcher Zhu Hai along: if Jin Pi complied, well and good; if he did not, Zhu Hai could strike. [SJ0771-E17]

Wuji wept at that. Jin Pi was an old campaigner of the shouting, veteran stamp, he said, and would, he feared, refuse — in which case he would have to be killed. "That is why I weep — do you think I fear death?" Zhu Hai was the butcher Hou Ying had recommended earlier, a man of worth hidden in the marketplace. Wuji had called on him several times, and Zhu Hai had never once returned the courtesy, which Wuji had found odd at the time. [SJ0771-E18][SJ0771-E8]

This time Zhu Hai answered without hesitation: "I am a knife-wielding butcher in the market. You have come to see me in person again and again, and I never answered in kind because small courtesies are of no use. Now that you are in a fix, this is the moment for me to give my life." Hou Ying, for his part, asked to reckon the days of Wuji's journey: on the day Wuji reached Jin Pi's army he would cut his own throat facing north, to see him off. The Records later confirms that Hou Ying did cut his throat facing north. [SJ0771-E19][SJ0771-E20][SJ0771-E30]

At Ye, Wuji presented a forged order from the king of Wei and announced that he was replacing Jin Pi. Jin Pi matched the two halves of the tally and was still suspicious. He raised his hand and looked hard at Wuji: "I hold a hundred thousand men on the frontier, a grave charge for the state, and now a single carriage comes to relieve me. What is the meaning of this?" He was on the point of refusing. Zhu Hai drew from his sleeve an iron mace weighing forty catties and killed him. Wuji took over the army and gave orders in the camp: fathers and sons both serving, the father goes home; brothers both serving, the elder brother goes home; an only son with no brothers goes home to care for his parents. From the men left he picked eighty thousand, and advanced on the Qin army. [SJ0771-E29][SJ0771-E22]

The Qin army raised the siege and drew off; Handan was saved and Zhao preserved. The king of Zhao and Lord Pingyuan came out to the border to meet Wuji in person, Lord Pingyuan with a quiver of arrows on his back, leading the way. The king of Zhao bowed twice and said that no worthier man had appeared since antiquity. [SJ0771-E23]

King Xiaocheng of Zhao, grateful that Wuji had seized the army on a forged order and saved Zhao, took counsel with Lord Pingyuan and proposed to enfeoff him with five cities. When Wuji heard, he "showed a self-satisfied look, as if the credit were his own." A retainer came to him: kindness done to you must never be forgotten, he said; kindness you do to another is best forgotten. Forging the king of Wei's order and seizing Jin Pi's army — "toward Zhao you have done a service; toward Wei you cannot be called a loyal minister." To go on preening yourself over it is something I cannot approve. Wuji "at once blamed himself, as if there were nowhere he could hide." Later, when the king of Zhao received him with the rites of a host, Wuji stood to the side, declined the honor, and pronounced himself at fault, saying he had failed Wei and done nothing for Zhao. Because Wuji held back, the king of Zhao could not bring himself to hand over the five cities. In the end the king gave him the town of Hao to support his household, and Wei restored to him the city of Xinling. [SJ0771-E24][SJ0771-E31]

The king of Wei was angry over the stolen tally and the killing of Jin Pi, and Wuji knew it. Once Qin had been driven off and Zhao saved, he sent the Wei generals home with their troops and stayed on in Zhao with his own retainers. Later, when Lord Pingyuan sneered at him for keeping company with gamblers and ale-sellers, Wuji took leave of his sister to depart and said that he had first heard Lord Pingyuan was worthy, and so had failed the king of Wei to save Zhao, to live up to Lord Pingyuan. [SJ0771-E25][SJ0771-E36]

## Three Things Beyond Loyalty

The author's inference: treat this as a friend staking everything for a friend and you miss where the real difficulty lay. Wuji turned back at the Yimen to ask Hou Ying what fault he had committed; Hou Ying used that return to show him that riding to his death would accomplish nothing. His rebuke — it would be like throwing meat at a starving tiger; what would it accomplish, and what use would retainers be then? — traded courage for arithmetic: what would a hundred-odd chariots buy against the Qin army? If even his retainers could not be put to use, what were retainers for? [SJ0771-E13][SJ0771-E14]

Second, the bad case was staffed before anyone acted. Hou Ying warned Wuji that even with the tally matched, Jin Pi might refuse to hand over the army, and told him to bring Zhu Hai; hearing this, Wuji foresaw that the step would mean killing an old general of his own state, and wept and said so out loud first. The author's inference: naming the cost in advance does not make it smaller. It turns a shock into an execution of a decision already made. [SJ0771-E17][SJ0771-E18]

Third, how he kept the books after it worked. Zhao was about to grant five cities; a retainer reminded him that he could not count as a loyal minister to Wei, and he blamed himself on the spot. Before the king of Zhao he declared he had failed Wei and done nothing for Zhao. While he was staying in Zhao he still said he had failed his king to save Zhao. On this reading he did not write the debt off: the army went home to Wei, and he stayed in Zhao. But the record also says he stayed in Zhao ten years without returning; when the king of Wei sent men to fetch him, he feared the king's anger and even barred his household from admitting the king's envoys, so staying there need not mean he was owning up to the debt. [SJ0771-E24][SJ0771-E31][SJ0771-E36][SJ0771-E25][SJ0771-E32]

The other course was to keep waiting for the king of Wei to change his mind. But Lord Pingyuan had already said that Handan might surrender to Qin any day. On this reading, the price of holding back would be paid by the people inside the walls, and what Wuji chose was not a clean option; it was one that could change the situation, at the price of killing his own general and breaking faith with his king. [SJ0771-E11][SJ0771-E22]

What did Sima Qian make of it? The Grand Historian writes that he passed through Daliang and made a point of finding the Yimen. Calling Wuji by his title, Lord Xinling, he says Wuji's readiness to seek out recluses in mountain caves and to befriend men beneath him had good cause, and that his name stood first among the lords — no empty fame. One reading is that this praise lands on the end where Wuji honored recluses like Hou Ying, not on the forged order and the slain general. What came afterward ran as follows. Wuji stayed in Zhao ten years. When Qin attacked Wei, Master Mao and Master Xue, two recluses he had befriended in Zhao, told him that he was valued in Zhao and known among the lords only because he had Wei behind him. He went home at once to command Wei's army and led the forces of five states to defeat Qin. Qin then spent heavy sums of gold buying off Jin Pi's former retainers to slander him; the king of Wei did send a replacement to take over the army, and Wuji excused himself on illness and took to drink, dying of it four years later. After his death Qin attacked Wei and took twenty cities, and eighteen years later captured a later king of Wei. On one interpretation, this closing episode writes Wuji's survival and Wei's survival on the same line. [SJ0771-E37][SJ0771-E32][SJ0771-E34][SJ0771-E33][SJ0771-E35]

## The Limits of the Record

This piece draws only on the "Biography of the Prince of Wei" in the Records of the Grand Historian. Hou Ying's plan, Zhu Hai's reply, and the retainer's remonstrance are all speeches recorded by the historian, not necessarily verbatim transcripts. [SJ0771-E15][SJ0771-E19][SJ0771-E24]

Phrases in the original such as "troubled in his heart," "showed a self-satisfied look" and "at once blamed himself" are the historian's narration; this piece relays them as he tells it. [SJ0771-E13][SJ0771-E24]

The troop figures cited here — Jin Pi's hundred thousand and the eighty thousand picked men — are taken from this biography's account. [SJ0771-E9][SJ0771-E22]

## Think about it

Your team faces an obvious risk. Leadership, afraid of a bigger rival, has stopped already-committed resources halfway and told them to hold and watch. You have front-line evidence in hand and two capable people; pushing forward means bypassing the normal chain of command, and it may cost your own side.

**Before you act, which is the first question to ask: is this throwing meat at a starving tiger, or what if the person who has to comply refuses?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Leadership stopped out of fear of a bigger rival, in the way the king of Qin's threat stopped the king of Wei — not because they failed to see the risk. You have verifiable front-line evidence and capable people. If it works, the credit and the debt to your own side will both be real.
- **What you could do**: First, replace the death-ride with concrete arithmetic: what would the resources at hand actually buy? Second, before acting, line up a person and a stop-loss for the case where the other party will not cooperate — Hou Ying had Wuji bring the butcher Zhu Hai. Third, after it works, put the debt to your own side on your own account and do not bank the credit — as Wuji did once the retainer spoke to him.
- **Another reasonable choice**: You could also stay inside the process, pass the evidence up and wait for approval, or do only your own job and leave your position untouched. The author's point: both preserve procedure, but the cost falls on the party under siege.

</details>
