# After the Key Went Down the Drain

*The plan had a hole no one could get around, and at daybreak the window would close. Wait, and the chance might never come again. Move now, and the gates might not open.*

## Before Dawn

Beijing, the night of the sixteenth day of the first lunar month. A group of men sat in Xu Youzhen's house, plotting to put a retired emperor back on the throne, and they had to decide before dawn whether to move. Their plan had a flaw. They held no keys to the palace, and at night the palace gates did not open. Yet if they waited until morning, a memorial about to go before the court might leave their plan with nothing left to do. [BK-MING-U511-E8][BK-MING-U511-E11][BK-MING-U511-E12]

The story that follows comes from Those Things of the Ming Dynasty (Mingchao naxie shir), a popular history by the writer known as Dangnian Mingyue. The book reads like a novel, and much of what its characters think and many of its scenes are the author's own dramatisation. Wherever this piece describes what someone was thinking, or how they should be judged, it is passing on what the book says. [BK-MING-U511-E3][BK-MING-U511-E14]

## Who Is Who

The man held in the Southern Palace was the retired emperor, Zhu Qizhen. His jailer was his own younger brother, the reigning emperor Zhu Qiyu, who by now had kept him there for seven years. The reigning emperor was gravely ill and had no son. Most ministers at court wanted to reinstate the retired emperor's son, Zhu Jianshen, as heir. [BK-MING-U511-E5][BK-MING-U511-E18][BK-MING-U511-E23]

The plot began with a general, Shi Heng, and the book explains his motive. After the defence of Beijing, Shi Heng was made a marquis. Yu Qian, whose contribution had been the greatest, received only an empty honorific. Uneasy at the imbalance, Shi Heng sent up a memorial recommending Yu Qian's son, Yu Mian, for office. To his surprise, Yu Qian told the emperor Zhu Qiyu: "Shi Heng, a senior general, is recommending his private connections for office; he should be disciplined." From then on, the book says, Shi Heng was convinced that Yu Qian could never be his friend. Yu Qian's backer was Zhu Qiyu, so dealing with Yu Qian meant first dealing with the emperor. Later the ailing Zhu Qiyu summoned Shi Heng to perform the sacrificial rites in his place, and Shi Heng saw his opening. [BK-MING-U511-E24]

Shi Heng controlled the capital's defences and the gates of the inner city. He brought in two accomplices: Cao Jixiang, a eunuch, and Zhang Yue, a man of weighty family as the younger brother of Zhang Fu. Even with the three of them together, the scheme looked set to fall apart. So Shi Heng went for advice to Xu Bin, an old acquaintance who headed the Court of Imperial Sacrifices. Xu Bin said he was too old for such a dangerous line of work but could recommend someone: "As long as this man agrees to join, the great enterprise will succeed." The man he named was Xu Youzhen. [BK-MING-U511-E1][BK-MING-U511-E2][BK-MING-U511-E8]

In the book, Xu Youzhen is a man nursing a grievance. Dangnian Mingyue writes that he had sharp judgement and real ability, yet everyone had reviled him for one wrong remark. Nine years earlier, the book recounts, he had read the night sky and correctly foretold the defeat at Tumu. That accurate prediction brought him humiliation and exclusion rather than credit. He now held high office, but the old shame never left him: "he meant to reclaim the justice that was his due." [BK-MING-U511-E3][BK-MING-U511-E14]

## From the Thirteenth to Daybreak

The moment Xu Youzhen joined their meetings, he pointed out that the first priority was to reach Zhu Qizhen in the Southern Palace. The book gives the reasoning bluntly: even if they got rid of Zhu Qiyu, none of them could make himself emperor. Without the retired emperor's consent, the whole enterprise had no legitimacy. Only then did the three send someone to contact him. It was the thirteenth day of the first month, and the plan was formally settled. [BK-MING-U511-E4]

On the morning of the fourteenth, the court assembled. Ill as he was, Zhu Qiyu insisted on attending, because the question before it was the succession. Most ministers favoured reinstating Zhu Jianshen. The grand secretaries Wang Wen and Chen Xun, the emperor's confidants, flatly objected. Better, they said, to bring in some prince from an outlying fief than to restore Zhu Jianshen. The hall descended into argument. Zhu Qiyu issued a single decree: "I am now ill; this will be discussed again at the morning court on the seventeenth." Then he added a phrase that the book gives in its original form, suo qing bu yun: "what you request is not granted." With those words he turned down Zhu Jianshen's reinstatement without naming any heir, and pushed the whole matter back three days. [BK-MING-U511-E5][BK-MING-U511-E6]

That night, at Shi Heng's house, Xu Youzhen first asked whether Zhu Qizhen knew. He did, Shi Heng said, and he had agreed. Xu then laid out a plan in three steps. First, use a report of alarm on the frontier as cover for Zhang Yue, then a military commissioner, to lead a thousand troops into the capital. Second, open the inner-city gates with the keys Shi Heng kept and bring the thousand men inside as a reserve and a guard. Third, go to the Southern Palace, bring out Zhu Qizhen, escort him into the inner palace and, while Zhu Qiyu lay gravely ill, proclaim his restoration. The trouble was that Shi Heng controlled only the inner-city gates. They had no keys to the Southern Palace or to the inner palace. That palace, the book explains, is the one the Qing later called the Forbidden City, and "without the emperor's order, the palace gates would never open at night." If they tried to storm it, the noise would bring guards and garrison troops at once. [BK-MING-U511-E7][BK-MING-U511-E8]

Dangnian Mingyue believes that a man as clever as Xu Youzhen must have seen the hole. In his explanation, Xu was not careless of his life. Rather, life seemed to him too short, so short that he would no longer endure or wait: "live or die, stake everything on this one throw." In the Southern Palace, the book says, Zhu Qizhen also knew how great the risk was. If it failed, he could not even go back to being a prisoner. He agreed anyway, without the slightest hesitation, because he had no other choice. [BK-MING-U511-E9][BK-MING-U511-E10]

On the morning of the sixteenth, Yu Qian, Hu Ying and Wang Zhi agreed to propose that Zhu Jianshen be reinstated as crown prince. They asked Shang Lu to draft a memorial for the next day's court. Dangnian Mingyue reckons that Zhu Qiyu, sonless and dying, would very likely have accepted it. Zhu Qizhen would then have had to fight his own son for the throne, and the plot would have lost its purpose. That night every conspirator gathered at Xu Youzhen's house. Shi Heng and the others, usually so overbearing, lost their nerve and looked to Xu. In the book's telling, Xu paced the room for a while and then said: "I am going to look at the heavens." He climbed onto his roof, knowing full well that reading the stars was nothing but a way of fooling people, and there he made his decision: "The great deed is tonight. The moment must not be lost. We move!" As he left, he gave the family who had gathered to see him off one sentence: "If I come back, I'll be a man; if I don't, I'll be a ghost." [BK-MING-U511-E11][BK-MING-U511-E12][BK-MING-U511-E13][BK-MING-U511-E14][BK-MING-U511-E15]

The key to the Chang'an Gate was in Shi Heng's charge. He let Zhang Yue's thousand men into the inner city, shut the gate behind them, and then panicked. The book spells out why. The soldiers had no idea they had been brought in to stage a rebellion, and they might mutiny at any moment. If they were discovered, Shi Heng would be guilty of treason before anything had even happened. Xu Youzhen said to him: "Is the gate locked? Give me the key." Puzzled and suspicious, Shi Heng handed it over. Xu took the key and threw it into the drain. Shi Heng lunged at him, grabbed his robe and demanded to know whether he had gone mad. Xu answered: "Only forward, no retreat; only life, no death." [BK-MING-U511-E16]

As the book describes it, at that very moment the starry, moonlit sky suddenly went dark. Shi Heng and Zhang Yue feared it was a sign that Heaven did not want them to act, and they stopped where they stood. Xu Youzhen demanded to know why they were not moving. Zhang Yue asked in a small voice, shi ji fou: "Will it succeed?" Xu walked up to him and growled, low, bi ji: "It will." Zhang Yue was asking about the odds. Xu answered with a verdict that was not open to discussion, and he offered no new reason. [BK-MING-U511-E17]

At the Southern Palace the gates were shut tight, and no one answered their calls. Xu said: "No need to call at the gate; just knock the wall down." The soldiers rammed the palace wall with timbers until it gave way. The book records this in the original words hui qiang ru, "broke the wall and went in", and Zhu Qizhen was brought out. At the Donghua Gate, however, the guards would not open, and the conspirators had no key. The book explains why the two gates were different. The Southern Palace was out of the way, and no one would come after them for a broken wall there. The Donghua Gate guarded the heart of the inner palace and had its own dedicated watch, and the slightest disturbance would bring the guards. Shi Heng turned to Xu Youzhen, and this time Xu, too, had nothing to say. They pleaded through the gate, the gatekeepers ignored them, and dawn was almost upon them. Then Zhu Qizhen let out a great shout, recorded in the book as wo taishanghuang ye: "I am the retired emperor. Open the gate!" The Donghua Gate opened. [BK-MING-U511-E18][BK-MING-U511-E19][BK-MING-U511-E20]

Zhu Qizhen walked to the Fengtian Hall and struck the bells and drums that summoned the court to audience. At the sound, the palace gates opened one after another. Xu Youzhen stood alone before the hall and told the senior grand secretaries who came hurrying at the news: "The retired emperor has already been restored. Gentlemen, you had better go and offer your congratulations." The book also describes Zhu Qiyu in his bedchamber, hearing the bells and drums and asking his attendants who was sounding them. Told that it was his elder brother summoning the ministers, he fell silent for a moment, then smiled and said only: "Good, good, good." Zhu Qizhen sat on the throne once more. On the second day after his restoration, Xu Youzhen entered the Grand Secretariat as minister of war, and Shi Heng was made Duke Zhongguo. Zhang Yue became Marquis of Taiping, and Cao Jixiang was made director of ceremonial eunuchs. On that same day, the book says, Xu Youzhen ordered the arrest of Yu Qian, Wang Wen and others. [BK-MING-U511-E21][BK-MING-U511-E22][BK-MING-U511-E23][BK-MING-U511-E25]

## The Hole, the Window and the Shout

Xu Youzhen faced more than the question of whether the plan was sound. He also had to ask whether, if he waited any longer, there would be any plan left to carry out. In the book's account, two forces pushed him to act. One came from outside: once the memorial to reinstate Zhu Jianshen reached the next morning's court, the plot would lose its point. The other is Dangnian Mingyue's reading of his character, which says he was no longer willing to wait. The first is the situation as the book lays it out, though the claim that the memorial would likely be accepted is itself Dangnian Mingyue's judgement. The second is his reading of Xu's character. Still, a closing window shows only that waiting is costly. It does not prove the plan could work, and the hole at the Donghua Gate did not vanish because time was short. [BK-MING-U511-E9][BK-MING-U511-E11][BK-MING-U511-E19]

Throwing away the key filled none of the holes in the plan. It was aimed at people. Shi Heng's fear was being branded a traitor before anything had been done. The gate had already been shut behind the troops. With the key gone, the gate could not be reopened to send the men back out, so calling it off was no longer possible, and only the road forward remained. One reading is that the throw crushed the most dangerous hesitation among Xu's partners. But it also threw away a possible chance to pull back. [BK-MING-U511-E16][BK-MING-U511-E19]

The detail most worth remembering is how each gate opened. The Southern Palace fell to a battered wall, an expedient Xu came up with on the spot. The Donghua Gate could not be battered, and Xu had no answer for it. What finally opened it was Zhu Qizhen's cry, "I am the retired emperor." In the whole affair, the retired emperor's standing was the one thing that needed no key. Reaching him was exactly the first step Xu had insisted on the moment he joined. So it is not quite right to say Xu won because his plan was meticulous, nor quite right to say he won on luck alone. His plan could not open the last gate, but he had brought to that gate the one man who could call it open. [BK-MING-U511-E4][BK-MING-U511-E18][BK-MING-U511-E19][BK-MING-U511-E20]

Nor is this gamble best read as an inspirational tale. In the book, Zhu Qizhen's shout stunned everyone, the gatekeepers included, and the Donghua Gate swung open. What came straight after the winning throw was rewards, and the arrest of Yu Qian. [BK-MING-U511-E20][BK-MING-U511-E22][BK-MING-U511-E25]

## What This Story Can Show

This piece rests entirely on a single work of popular history, Those Things of the Ming Dynasty. Xu's thoughts on the rooftop, the conspirators' feelings when the sky went dark and Zhu Qiyu's expression when he heard the bells all belong to the book's novelistic writing. The short phrases suo qing bu yun, shi ji fou, bi ji, hui qiang ru and wo taishanghuang ye are what the book presents as original quoted wording. [BK-MING-U511-E6][BK-MING-U511-E14][BK-MING-U511-E17][BK-MING-U511-E18][BK-MING-U511-E20][BK-MING-U511-E22]

One success does not prove that this kind of gamble is right. The story can show only two things: when a window really is closing, waiting has a cost too, and success or failure may rest on someone outside the plan. It cannot show that cutting off your own retreat will bring victory. [BK-MING-U511-E11][BK-MING-U511-E20]

## Think about it

On Thursday evening, Zhou, a product lead, hears that tomorrow morning's review meeting will very probably sign off on a rival team's proposal. One link in Zhou's own team's proposal still isn't working. The integration with the payment system needs help from another department, and that department's lead can't be reached tonight. A colleague suggests pushing a demo version live overnight and deleting the old rollback branch, so that nobody wavers any more.

**Would you force it through tonight? And if you did, would you delete the rollback branch?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The news about tomorrow's decision is basically reliable. Someone else may or may not be found tonight to handle the payment integration temporarily. A failed demo would cost only this one opportunity and would not harm the existing business.
- **What you could do**: First, work out whether the window really will close. If tomorrow is only a discussion, there is no need to gamble. Next, look for your own Donghua Gate and your own Zhu Qizhen. Which link can planning alone not open? Is there a person or a resource who could open it at the critical moment, and can you reach them tonight? Only then decide about the rollback branch. If the team's problem is hesitation, a clear division of labour and a firm deadline can do that job instead of deleting the branch. Consider cutting it off only if the fallback itself would drag down execution.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Don't force it. At tomorrow's meeting, say plainly which link isn't working yet and how many days it needs, and ask for time before the decision is final. Zhu Qiyu's move to push the succession back three days is a reminder that delay is itself an option.

</details>
