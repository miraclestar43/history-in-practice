# Two Clauses He Thought of Midway Through Writing

*At Appomattox, the officers' side-arms and their private horses and effects were never negotiated: Grant wrote them into the terms on his own, partway through the draft. When Lee asked about the soldiers' own horses and mules, Grant first answered that as written the terms did not allow it; Lee reread the terms and said that was clear, and Grant then offered on his own, verbally and without writing it into the terms, to let the men take the animals home.*

## A Sick Headache, Mustard, and a Letter

On April 8th, 1865 (the source says only "the 8th"; the month and year are inferred from the telegraph it reproduces from the following day, dated April 9th, 1865), Grant, on the Union side, followed his Army of the Potomac in pursuit, in rear of Lee, the commander of the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia. Grant was in the grip of a sick headache. He spent the night at a farmhouse on the road, some distance behind the main body of the army, bathing his feet in hot water and mustard and putting mustard plasters on his wrists and the back of his neck, hoping that by morning he would be cured. A letter from Lee proposed a meeting, but, as Grant tells it, for a different purpose from that of surrendering his army. Grant's answer was brisk. He had no authority to treat on the subject of peace, and the proposed meeting could lead to no good. The terms on which peace could be had, he wrote, were well understood: let the South lay down its arms, and the most desirable event would come sooner, sparing thousands of lives and hundreds of millions of property not yet destroyed. [GRANT671-E1][GRANT671-E25][GRANT671-E2][GRANT671-E28][GRANT671-E18]

Later Lee asked Grant for an interview, as Grant records, for the purpose of getting from him the terms he proposed to give Lee's army. In the writer's view, the difficulty was that the actual terms of surrender were Grant's to draft on the spot. The basic conditions had already appeared in Grant's letter of the 8th, and Lee said he had so understood it; but the written clauses were Grant's to set down. Two items in the terms he finally set down were afterthoughts, added only once his pen was already moving: the officers' side-arms, and the officers' private horses and effects. The horses and mules belonging to the Confederate enlisted men were not in the written terms at all: when Lee asked, Grant first answered that as written they could not be kept; Lee said that was clear, and Grant then offered on his own, verbally, to let the men take them home. ## Doubts About Halting the Fighting, and a Letter Sent Through the Lines

After Grant's refusal, Lee wrote again. Grant records that Meade and Sheridan, two commanders on his own side, both hesitated very considerably about suspending hostilities at all. They were afraid the overture was not in good faith; and Lee's Army of Northern Virginia was where it could not escape except by some deception. They finally consented, however, to a suspension of hostilities for two hours, to give an opportunity of communicating with Grant in that time if possible. But the message was hard to deliver: from the route Grant had taken, it would probably not be possible to reach him and get an answer back within the time fixed unless the messenger passed through the rebel lines. Lee therefore sent an escort with the officer bearing the message through his own lines to Grant. [GRANT671-E2][GRANT671-E3][GRANT671-E4][GRANT671-E26]

The officer found Grant still suffering with the sick headache; in Grant's own telling, "the instant I saw the contents of the note I was cured." He wrote back at once. Lee's note had reached him only at 11:50 a.m., he explained, because he had switched to a different road; he would push forward to the front for the meeting, and a message left on the road he was on, naming the place, would reach him. When he rode up to Sheridan's position, the men there were very much excited. They expressed their view that the whole thing was a ruse to let the Confederates get away. They believed that Johnston was marching up from North Carolina, and that Lee was moving to join this Johnston; and they would whip the rebels where they stood in five minutes if only Grant would let them go in. Grant had no doubt of Lee's good faith, and was soon conducted to where he was. [GRANT671-E5][GRANT671-E6][GRANT671-E7]

## The Meeting at the McLean House

The meeting took place at the house of a Mr. McLean at Appomattox Court House, where Lee waited with Colonel Marshall, one of his staff officers. Grant had left camp that morning with no inkling that the end would come so quickly, and so he was in rough garb: a soldier's blouse for a coat, with the shoulder straps of his rank so the army would know who he was, and no sword, as he usually went without one when riding the field. [GRANT671-E8][GRANT671-E9]

Grant was explicit that he did not know what Lee was feeling. In Grant's description, Lee was a man of much dignity with an impassible face; it was impossible to say whether he was inwardly glad the end had finally come, or sad at the result and too manly to show it. Whatever his feelings, they were entirely concealed from Grant's observation. Grant's own were a different matter: jubilant on receipt of the letter, he now found them sad and depressed. [GRANT671-E10]

The two men first talked, so pleasantly that Grant almost forgot the object of their meeting. After a while Lee brought the conversation back to it: he had asked for this interview, he said, to get from Grant the terms he proposed to give his army. Grant answered that he meant merely that the army should lay down their arms, not to take them up again during the continuance of the war unless duly and properly exchanged. Lee said he had so understood the letter. [GRANT671-E11][GRANT671-E12]

They talked on a little longer, and then Lee interrupted again to suggest that the terms ought to be written out. Grant called to General Parker, secretary on his staff, for writing materials and began to write. As he later told it, when he put pen to paper he did not know the first word he should use. He knew only what was in his mind and wanted to express it clearly, so there could be no mistaking it. As he wrote on, it occurred to him that the officers had private horses and effects that were important to them and of no value to the Union, and that "it would be an unnecessary humiliation to call upon them to deliver their side arms." [GRANT671-E13][GRANT671-E15]

What he wrote ran roughly as follows. Rolls of all the officers and men were to be made in duplicate, one copy given to an officer designated by Grant, the other retained by whoever Lee designated. Officers would give individual paroles not to take up arms against the United States until properly exchanged, and each company or regimental commander would sign a like parole for the men of his command. The arms, artillery and public property were to be parked and stacked and turned over to the officer appointed to receive them — but this did not embrace the officers' side-arms, nor their private horses or baggage. That done, each officer and man would be allowed to return home, not to be disturbed by United States authority so long as he observed his parole and the laws in force where he resided. [GRANT671-E14]

Not one word had passed between the two men about private property, side-arms or anything of the kind. Lee appeared to have no objection to the terms first proposed, or if he had a point against them he preferred to wait until they were in writing. When he read the passage about side-arms, horses and the officers' private property, he remarked with some feeling, Grant thought, that this would have a happy effect upon his army. As for the famous story of Lee surrendering his sword and Grant handing it back, Grant dismissed it, and much else that had been said about it, as "the purest romance." Until he wrote it into the terms, neither man had mentioned sword or side-arms. There was no premeditation: the thought did not occur to him until the moment he set it down. Had he happened to omit it and Lee called his attention to it, he said, he would have put it in precisely as he acceded to the provision about the soldiers keeping their horses. [GRANT671-E16][GRANT671-E20]

Then Lee remarked that their army was organized a little differently from that of the United States — still implying, Grant noted, that they were two countries — and that in their army the cavalrymen and artillerists owned their own horses. Was he to understand that the men who owned their horses could keep them? As the terms stood, Grant said, they could not; only the officers were permitted to take their private property. Lee read over the terms a second time and remarked that that was clear. Grant then went on. He thought this would be about the last battle of the war, and sincerely hoped so. He took it that most of the men in the ranks were small farmers, and the whole country had been so raided by the two armies that it was doubtful whether they could put in a crop to carry themselves and their families through the next winter without the horses they were riding. The United States did not want the animals, and he would therefore instruct the officers he left behind, while taking the paroles of Lee's troops, to let every man of the Confederate army who claimed to own a horse or mule take the animal home. Lee remarked again that this would have a happy effect. [GRANT671-E17][GRANT671-E27][GRANT671-E18]

Lee wrote accepting the terms: being substantially the same as those expressed in Grant's letter of the 8th, they were accepted, and he would designate the proper officers to carry the stipulations into effect. Before taking his leave he added that his army was in a very bad condition for want of food, and without forage; his men had been living for days on parched corn exclusively, and he would have to ask Grant for rations and forage. Grant agreed at once and asked how many men needed rations; about twenty-five thousand, Lee answered. Grant authorized him to send his own commissary and quartermaster to Appomattox Station, two or three miles away, where out of the trains the Union had stopped they could have all the provisions wanted. Grant designated Generals Gibbon, Griffin and Merritt to carry into effect the paroling of Lee's troops before they started for their homes, and Lee left Generals Longstreet, Gordon and Pendleton for them to confer with in order to facilitate the work. The two men then separated as cordially as they had met, Lee returning to his own lines. Soon after Lee's departure Grant telegraphed Secretary of War Stanton that Lee had surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia that afternoon on terms proposed by Grant himself. [GRANT671-E19][GRANT671-E21][GRANT671-E28]

When news of the surrender reached the Union lines, the men began firing a salute of a hundred guns in honor of the victory. Grant at once sent word to have it stopped: "The Confederates were now our prisoners, and we did not want to exult over their downfall." The next day, in the course of a conversation between the two men, Lee said the South was a big country, and Union troops might have to march over it three or four times before the war entirely ended — though it could now be done, since the South could no longer resist. He earnestly hoped, however, that the Union would not be called upon to cause more loss and sacrifice of life; he could not foretell the result. Grant then suggested that no man in the Confederacy had such influence with the soldiery and the whole people as Lee, and that if he would now advise the surrender of all the armies, his advice would be followed with alacrity. Lee said he could not do that without consulting the President first. [GRANT671-E22][GRANT671-E23][GRANT671-E24]

## The One Item Never Written Into the Terms

One reading of the side-arms and private horses is that what matters is not the size of the concession but where it appears: it was not traded for at the negotiating table but written in by the drafter himself. The inference here is that the cost was close to nil — arms, artillery and public property had to be surrendered anyway — while the payoff was less resistance to the clauses that actually had to be carried out, the rolls and the paroles. When Lee read that passage, Grant recorded, he remarked that this would have a happy effect upon his army. [GRANT671-E14][GRANT671-E15][GRANT671-E16]

The horses and mules were the more practical item. The reason Grant gave on the spot was the winter crop: animals taken in surrender were of no use to the Union, but to small farmers going home they were the tools of survival. One reading is that this item addressed not face but whether the settlement could actually be wound up afterward — the men had to go home, eat, and plant, and only then were there people to carry out everything already written down. A caution: this was not in the terms as written; Grant told Lee he would instruct the officers left behind. [GRANT671-E18]

As for halting the salute, one reading is that the last clause of any surrender is never set on paper: it is the stance the winning side takes once news of the surrender spreads. The alternative the writer offers is to keep demands such as handing over side-arms on the books as a reminder of who is in charge. The writer infers that the cost might be an unwillingness to cooperate in the carrying out, since parts of it needed the other side's cooperation — company or regimental commanders signing paroles for the men of their commands, and the generals Lee left behind conferring with the Union generals in charge of the paroling to help with that work. [GRANT671-E22][GRANT671-E14][GRANT671-E28]

## Today: The Wrap-Up Clause

> You run operations at a manufacturing company and are ending a contract early with a logistics provider you have worked with for eight years. You draft the termination terms. On the list: return the uniforms, scrape the decals off the trailers, hand in the badges with the company logo, photograph each item for sign-off. Ask one question first: what use are these things to you? If the honest answer is almost none, they are closer to side-arms than to public property. What deserves to be spelled out instead is the handover checklist for the last truckload, the formula for unsettled charges, and a transition window for repainting the vehicles on both sides.

> Another scenario: two teams merge. Access to the old systems obviously has to be revoked, and the handover documents obviously have to be listed. But what about the old grade titles, the internal honorifics, the names printed on years of staff photos — should those be scrubbed as well? The same question lands on the announcement: on the day the merger closes, does the first paragraph of your all-staff email celebrate the win, or lay out where the other side goes next and the handover schedule?

## One Source Only

The sole source for this article is Grant's own memoirs, dated 1885–86 and written after the events. Every conversation in them is his retelling and need not be verbatim, and the words of Meade, Sheridan and Lee all reach us through him alone. [GRANT671-E1][GRANT671-E11][GRANT671-E12]

He states plainly that he did not know Lee's feelings, and where he says Lee spoke with feeling he flags it as his own guess at the time. Neither man's inner state should be filled in on his behalf. [GRANT671-E10][GRANT671-E16]

The indulgence on horses and mules was not in the terms as written; Grant promised Lee he would instruct the officers left behind to allow it. Grant records that Gibbon, Griffin and Merritt were designated to carry out the paroling, with Lee leaving Longstreet, Gordon and Pendleton for them to confer with; but the passages this piece draws on do not describe how the paroling or the horse-and-mule allowance was actually carried out. ## Think about it

You are negotiating the terms for ending a long-running engagement with a consultant you have worked with for years, someone who will go on working in the same industry, and you are the one drafting the terms.

**Which requirements in the draft would make the agreement easier to carry out if they were deleted? Which indulgences belong on paper, and which can be left verbal with a named person responsible for seeing them through? And on the day it is settled, what is the first thing you say?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The other side may well work with you, or with your competitors, again
The clauses that actually have to be executed — handover, confidentiality, final settlement — need their people to cooperate
Some on your side want to see a posture of total victory in the terms
- **What you could do**: Sort every requirement into three piles: things you must get back, things useless to you but humiliating to them, and things that affect their livelihood
Cut the second pile; for the third, write down the scope of the indulgence and name who implements it and when
Keep the tone of celebration in check at the close, and in the announcement set out where the other side goes next and the execution timetable
- **Another reasonable choice**: The alternative the writer proposes: go the other way and keep the humiliating clauses as a show of force, so the other side remembers who gives the orders. The price is an absence of cooperation during execution, and the items most likely to stall are the small ones that only the other side's people can handle.
