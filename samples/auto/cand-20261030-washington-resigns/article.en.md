# The Man Who Bowed and the Men Who Kept Their Hats On: Handing Back Power, 1783

*The commander in chief came before Congress to hand back his commission. The day before, Congress wrote into its journal exactly who would sit, who would bow, and who would lift a hat. The form of the handover was part of what was being handed over.*

unscheduled · U.S. History · cand-20261030-washington-resigns rev 1

## I. Wrapping Up Takes More Rules Than Waging War

On Monday, 22 December 1783, the Continental Congress settled the order of the next day's public audience with its commander-in-chief, George Washington. A man who had commanded armies was about to hand delegated power back, in public, to a body that did its business sitting down. Who would sit, who would stand, who would bow and who would lift a hat: Congress wrote each of these into its journal. [U-E1][U-E2]

## II. Write the Moves First, Then Wait for the General

It began on Saturday, 20 December. Congress read a letter written that day by the Commander in Chief. He had arrived in the city "with the intention of asking leave to resign the commission he has the honor of holding in their service", and he asked how they wished him to offer his resignation, "whether in writing or at an audience". Congress resolved at once that he be admitted to a public audience on Tuesday next, at twelve o'clock. [U-E10]

Congress was thin at the time. The same day, a motion asked a prior question: "Is it the sense of Congress that seven states in Congress Assembled are competent to receive the resignation of a Commander in Chief?" The journal kept the roll call. Nearly every member listed voted ay; Partridge voted no, and Ellery's no was changed into ay at his own request. On the 22nd the states present were Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia and North Carolina, with one member each from New Hampshire and South Carolina. And on the morning of the audience itself, the 23rd, Congress resolved to write at once to the executives of New Hampshire, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, South Carolina and Georgia, demanding the immediate attendance of their delegates: during the sitting at this place there had never been more than seven states represented, most of them by only two delegates, and the ratification of the definitive treaty, among other matters, required the assent of at least nine states. [U-E11][U-E12][U-E13][U-E16][U-E17]

The agreed order of the audience ran to three clauses. First, the President and the members were to sit, covered — that is, wearing their hats — with the secretary standing at the President's side. Second, the General's arrival was to be announced to the secretary by a messenger, who would then introduce the General, attended by his aides, into the Hall of Congress. Third, the secretary would conduct the General to a chair and he would be seated, an aide standing on either side of him, while the secretary resumed his own place. Sitters, hat-wearers, standers, entrants: everyone had an assigned position. [U-E2]

The last three clauses dealt with the speech and the handover. After a proper time for the spectators to settle, silence was to be ordered if necessary, and the President was to address the General in set words: "Sir, The United States in Congress assembled are prepared to receive your communications." The General would rise, speak and sit again; the President would deliver the answer of Congress, which the General was to receive standing; and the secretary would hand him a copy of the answer and receive the commission, after which the General would take his leave. The draft as printed carries changes. In the sentence about the commission and the copy of the address, the text has them going to his aide to be presented to the secretary, with "[to the President]" in brackets. The editors note that the report is in the writing of Elbridge Gerry, "the words in brackets being in the writing of Elias Boudinot". [U-E14][U-E15]

One further rule governed gesture alone: "When the General rises to make his address, and also when he retires, he is to bow to Congress, which they are to return by uncovering without bowing." Under this rule only the General bowed; Congress answered by raising their hats. [U-E3]

One reading of this is that Congress wrote the nature of the transaction into posture before a word was spoken. The speaker rises, bows, gives his account, and withdraws; the listeners remain seated throughout and do no more than lift a hat. Power is not returned only in the text of a speech; half of it is already returned in the asymmetry between the bow and the uncovered head. That is the author's reading. The journal records the rules and gives no reasons. [U-E2][U-E3]

## III. December 23: The Pause, the Address, the Commission

The next day, Tuesday, December 23, the audience proceeded in the agreed order. The journal is spare about it: according to order, the Commander in Chief was admitted to a public audience. [U-E4][U-E5]

Once he was seated, silence was ordered, and the President let a pause go by before telling him that the United States in Congress assembled were prepared to receive his communications. The journal puts it this way: "the President, after a pause, informed him, that the United States in Congress assembled, were prepared to receive his communications". The pause made it into the record. The President did not rush to fill the silence; the silence itself was entered into the official account alongside the words. Then the General rose and spoke. [U-E5]

The address declared its business in the first line: "The great events on which my resignation depended, having at length taken place" — the events his resignation had been waiting on had finally occurred. He offered Congress his sincere congratulations and presented himself before them "to surrender into their hands the trust committed to me", returning the trust placed in him to their hands, and at the same time "to claim the indulgence of retiring from the service of my country", asking their indulgence to withdraw from the service of his country. Note the register of the request: he asks leave to retire rather than announcing that he has. [U-E6]

He went on: happy in the confirmation of independence and sovereignty, and pleased that the United States now had the opportunity of becoming a respectable nation, he resigned with satisfaction the appointment he had accepted with diffidence — "I resign with satisfaction the appointment I accepted with diffidence". Diffidence at the start, satisfaction at the end: those are his own words. What tone his voice carried is beyond the reach of the journal, and this piece will not guess. [U-E7]

In the middle of the address he gave thanks for Providence and for the help of his countrymen, and he singled out the officers who had served at his side through the war: "It was impossible the choice of confidential officers to compose my family should have been more fortunate." He asked Congress to give its favorable notice to those still in service. Then: "I consider it an indispensable duty to close this last act of my official life by commending the interests of our dearest country to the protection of Almighty God". [U-E18]

The close said the rest: "Having now finished the work assigned me, I retire from the great theatre of action" — the work assigned to him was finished, and he was withdrawing from the great theatre of action. He bid an affectionate farewell to the august body whose orders he had so long followed, "I here offer my commission", and took his leave of every employment of public life. [U-E8]

With that, he stepped forward and placed his commission, together with a copy of his address, in the President's hands. The journal closes the entry in one line: "He then advanced and delivered to the President his commission, with a copy of his address". The commission was the token of authority; the copy was the account of it, kept on file. Two objects, delivered at once. The draft had been revised at exactly this step; what the journal records is that he himself stepped forward and put the commission into the President's hands. [U-E9]

The author's inference is that the point of the procedure was that it depended on nobody's character. The order was written the day before, executed to the letter the next day, the token changed hands on the spot, and the words went into the archive along with it. A man's loyalty cannot be proven by a ceremony. Whether power has been put back where it belongs, however, can be entered in black and white on a particular day. That is an interpretation of how the record was made; the journal itself states no such intention. [U-E2][U-E9]

## IV. What the Form Is Arguing

Set this handover beside an ordinary spoken one and three things are happening at once. First, the sequence was fixed in advance: agreed on the twenty-second, carried out on the twenty-third, with no record of last-minute adjustment. Second, the etiquette was asymmetric: the General was to bow when he rose to speak and when he withdrew, and Congress to answer by uncovering: the standing man bowed, the seated body answered. The vertical distance between them was kept deliberately visible. Third, what changed hands was physical: the commission, plus a copy of the address. Ceremony, posture, and token together make up this documented transfer of power. [U-E2][U-E3][U-E9]

On one reading, the gap between bowing and uncovering is not an insult but a placement. Congress wanted those present to see the commander of the army incline himself to this institution, while the institution owed him no inclination in return. The covered, seated members are the striking detail. As the author reads it, men sitting with their hats on while another stands to address them draw a line in the room: we are the ones receiving the report. That explanation is the author's. The journal notes covering and seating, and no more. [U-E2][U-E3]

There is another layer, and it is the author's reading. The Congress receiving this resignation was not sure it was competent to do so: only seven states were present, and it took a formal vote on whether seven were enough; on the very morning of the audience it was still writing to absent states to send their delegates. At such a moment a procedure written in advance, carried out in public and recorded on the spot may have lent weight to a body short of hands. The journal does not connect these things; the connection is the author's. [U-E11][U-E16][U-E2]

Other forms were available. Washington's own letter offered two: a resignation in writing, or an audience. Congress chose the public audience. The author can imagine other arrangements too, and they are not history: the General and the President facing each other and speaking in turn, or Congress simply taking the commission with no speech at all. Each would have returned the authority, perhaps faster, and each would have cost a visible difference in rank and a complete first-person account on the record. [U-E10]

## V. Two Handovers Today

> A modern scene, first. The lead maintainer of an open-source project decides to step back and hand the repository's administrative rights back to the community council. The handover is scheduled for a video call, and a queue of questions appears at once: who chairs, who speaks first, which window gets shared, and whether the rights are transferred by clicking "transfer" live on the call or by each side poking at settings afterwards. Put those answers into the agenda in advance and the handover depends on nobody's good manners. Leave them out and it becomes a contest of volume and availability.

> A modern scene, second. A consulting engagement closes, and the consultant returns, item by item, the accounts, documents, and building passes the client issued over the life of the project. An administrator ticks them off a receipt sheet, each line naming the item and its date. Nobody looks at that sheet on an ordinary day. Two years on, if someone has to answer the question of whether those access rights were in fact recovered, the sheet is the only thing that can be produced. A token plus a list outlasts a warm and cordial goodbye.

## VI. What This Record Does Not Tell Us

Everything in this piece rests on one source: the official Journals of the Continental Congress, volume 25 — the order of audience agreed on December 22, the audience of the twenty-third, and the text of the address. No private account by anyone present was consulted, and so the speaker's face and tone, and the reactions in the room, are simply absent. The journal records gestures, sequence, and speech; it does not record feeling. Nor does this piece follow the man beyond that day. Motives, private thoughts, and options he may have considered are never supplied by this writer where the source is silent. [U-E1][U-E4]

Two technical reservations follow. First, the President's reply is not in this article's evidence pack and is not quoted. Second, the material comes from OCR of a facsimile and contains misreadings, so the English quotations here take only clean stretches, with doubtful spellings left outside the quotation marks. Third, the printed journal marks revisions to the draft protocol with brackets and deletions; the OCR text shows the bracketed words but not which words were struck out, so this piece reports only what is in the brackets.

## Think about it

A company dissolves a temporary project team, and the team lead must hand back the approval authority and external signing power granted during the project to the departments that lent it. The handover has to be completed in one twenty-minute meeting, and both sides will keep working together afterwards.

**At the handover meeting, do you have the person giving up authority stand and walk through each item while the receiving side sits and confirms, or does everyone chat around the table for two minutes and send a follow-up email afterwards? If both get the job done, which one do you choose for the record it leaves — when someone someday asks whether those permissions were actually recovered, do you want to be holding a signed receipt sheet, or a memory no one can reconstruct?**
