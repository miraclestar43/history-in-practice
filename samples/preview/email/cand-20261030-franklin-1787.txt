看不出是升是落：1787年9月17日，签字前的最后一讲
一部明知有缺点的宪法摆在面前，签还是不签、怎么签？制宪会议最后一天：一个有意含糊的签署格式、几种对签名的不同理解、三个拒签的人，和议长椅背上画着的那颗太阳。

== 最后一天，稿纸和签名 ==
1787年9月17日，制宪会议的最后一天。宪法的誊清本宣读完毕，本杰明·富兰克林站起身，手里拿着一份写好的讲稿；麦迪逊的笔记说，他是为了自己方便才把话写下来的，随后由詹姆斯·威尔逊代为宣读。会要散了，桌上这份文件不少人心知有缺点，问题只剩一个：签不签，怎么签。

他开口先说实话：宪法有几处他现在并不赞成。但他接着说，不敢保证将来永远不会赞成——活了这么久，他有过太多次因为更好的信息或更充分的考虑而改变看法的经历，哪怕是在自己曾以为想对了的重要问题上。所以他承认：“年纪越大，我越容易怀疑自己的判断。”一个当众说自己可能错的人，即将在一份文件上签下名字。

== 为什么还要签 ==
他给出的同意理由很朴素：他认为“我们需要一个全国政府”。他愿意连同宪法的“全部缺点（如果那算缺点的话）”一起接受它。到这里，争的已经不是文本好不好，而是手上有没有更好的东西可拿。

对于“再开一次会、写一部更好的”这条出路，他也打了问号。把一群人聚在一起取集体智慧之便，也就同时把他们的偏见、激情、错误意见、地方利益和自私打算一并聚来。“从这样的会议里，能指望产出完美的作品吗？”

有保留的人远不止他一个。古弗尼尔·莫里斯说他也有异议，只是视这份方案为能得到的最好方案；汉密尔顿则说，众所周知，没有谁的想法比他离这个方案更远。于是当天真正的争点浮出水面：签名这件事本身，到底算什么。

== 一天里的签名之争 ==
讲稿里最硬的一段是关于沉默的。富兰克林说，他对宪法缺点的意见，已为公共利益而放弃，从未在外面透露一个字：“它们在这几面墙里产生，也将在这里消亡。”会议室内的话，留在会议室内。

然后他向仍有异议的成员提出请求：和他一起“稍稍怀疑一下自己的不会出错”，把名字签上去，以“显示我们的一致”。他要的不是人人说好，而是名字出现在同一份文件上。

他随即动议成员签字，并提出一个现成的格式：“经出席各州一致同意，于会议中完成，9月17日……为此，我们在此签名作证。”麦迪逊记下一笔内情：这个有意含糊的格式由古弗尼尔·莫里斯起草，为的是争取异议者，交到富兰克林手上提出，好让它有更大的成功机会。

就在收尾的同一段时间里，会议还在改一个数字。戈勒姆提出，趁还来得及，把“众议员人数不得超过每四万人一名”中的四万改为三万，以减少反对意见——这不是绝对规则，只是给国会更大的余地。会议主席（签名栏作乔治·华盛顿）起身付表决时说，自己的位置一向使他不就待决问题发表意见，这次却忍不住表示希望通过这项修改，并承认代表比例偏小，一直是他自己认为方案中可以挑剔的地方。修改无异议通过，宪法誊清本付签获全体州赞成。

伦道夫随即起身，影射着富兰克林的话，为自己拒签致歉：尽管有压倒多数和德高望重的名字为这份方案背书。他说，拒签不等于他要在会外反对宪法，他只是要保留按日后的判断、照自己的职责行事的自由。他拒签，是因为他认为方案交给民众的“要么全收、要么全拒”会让会议的目的落空：九个州批准不了，混乱必然随之而来。

接着几位成员各自说明为什么签。莫里斯说，他也有异议，但视此为能得到的最好方案，愿连同缺点照收；多数已经决定，他服从这个决定；方案一出手，其余考虑都会退居其次，问题只剩“要不要有一个全国政府”，否则便是无政府。他并指出，按所提格式签名，只关乎出席各州一致这一事实。汉密尔顿说，他希望人人都签：几个有分量的人若反对甚至拒签，可能点燃潜藏的火星；在无政府与动荡、和方案可能带来的好处之间，没有什么可犹豫的。布朗特说，他本已声明不会以承诺支持的方式签名，但这个格式让他如释重负——他可以不承诺自己，只证明“方案是会议中各州一致的行动”这一事实。

富兰克林随即说明：写那篇讲稿时，他并不知道会有哪位成员拒签，希望大家这样理解；他感谢伦道夫最早提出方案、一路襄助，希望他放下异议，免得他的名字缺席带来大的祸害。伦道夫没有被说动。他说，以这个格式签名，与签宪法本身无异，换形式对他没有区别；拒签可能是他一生中最可怕的一步，但出于良知，他不能犹豫，更不能改变。

格里接着讲了自己的处境：他担心眼前的局面会引发内战，尤其在马萨诸塞，那里一派倾向民主——他认为那是最坏的政治祸害——另一派走向相反的极端，两派相撞，混乱可忧。格式对他同样无关紧要。但他说，如果别处不会让人知道，拒签的事绝不会由他传出去；他又说，富兰克林的话他只能看作是针对他和其他不签名的人。

也有人嫌这个格式不够痛快。平克尼认为靠措辞的模糊争取不到多少人，不如坦白，让形式直接说明实质：他自己将以支持宪法的立场签名，也愿意如此承诺。富兰克林答：“在国会和我们的选民认可这份方案之前，就承诺还太早。”英格索尔则给出另一种读法：签名既不只是证明事实，也不是无论如何都要支持，而是“通盘考虑之后，对最可取方案的推荐”。

对富兰克林动议的签署格式表决：新罕布什尔、马萨诸塞、康涅狄格、新泽西、宾夕法尼亚、特拉华、马里兰、弗吉尼亚、北卡罗来纳、佐治亚10个州赞成，南卡罗来纳代表团意见分裂。

最后，除伦道夫、梅森、格里三人外，所有成员都签了字，会议随即无期休会。最后几人签字时，富兰克林望着议长椅背上画着的太阳，对身边几位成员说，画家很难在画里分清朝阳与落日：“会期之中，随着我对结果时而希望、时而担心，我一次又一次地望着主席身后那个太阳，却分不出它是在升起还是在落下；如今，我终于欣慰地知道，那是一轮升起的太阳，而不是落下的。”

== 一张签名，几种读法 ==
这一天的争议几乎都绕着同一个动作：在文件末尾写上名字。一种解释是，莫里斯起草的那个格式把“签字”拆成了两个问题——你证明什么，你承诺什么。莫里斯和布朗特只认“出席各州一致”这个事实；英格索尔读作推荐；平克尼要求签名就该是承诺；伦道夫则认为两者拆不开，签了就是签了，格式再软也一样。作者推断，正因为它对不同的人意味着不同的事，多数人才能站到同一行名字底下；代价是，这行名字的含义从此需要解释。

另一种解释针对富兰克林请大家“稍稍怀疑一下自己的不会出错”的那句话。作者的引申是：这句话提供的也许不是“方案是对的”这种理由，而是一种在不确定中签字的态度——当另开一次会未必更好时，先承认自己可能错，再把名字放上去。作者提出的替代选择是要求在签名旁写明保留意见；但作者同样承认，那样做会削弱“一致”的分量，而那正是当天有人最想要的东西。

== 今天的签字场合 ==
现代虚构场景一：一个评审委员会刚刚否决了一位同事力推的方案。散会后，有成员在走廊里跟人说，自己其实也不同意，只是被多数压了下来。富兰克林那句“在这几面墙里产生，也将在这里消亡”提供了另一种做法：会议室里把反对讲透、记进纪要，出了门不把已经结束的分歧当作谈资。这不是隐瞒结果，而是不在外面把同一场仗再打一遍。

再设想一份对外的联合声明要署名，你同意大部分立场，只对一段措辞保留意见。有人劝你别计较，有人说你签了就等于全盘认可。这里可以引入的不是勇气或圆滑，而是区分：署名是证明事实、表达推荐，还是作出承诺。

== 我们能知道什么 ==
这场会议的记录主要来自詹姆斯·麦迪逊的笔记，后收入1845年埃利奥特编本（维基文库），是一位与会者写下的记录。1793年出版的《富兰克林文集》收有这篇讲稿的印本（题作 Final Speech of Dr. Franklin in the late Federal Convention），可以拿来对照，但本篇没有逐字比对两版。正文的英文引语都取自麦迪逊笔记一版；富兰克林讲稿的措辞在两版间是否有出入，本篇不作断言。

记录本身的来路也值得留意。同日的笔记里，金建议销毁会议记录或交主席保管，理由是记录若公开，会被想阻挠宪法的人利用；威尔逊反对销毁，说虚假说法可能流传，不应让反驳无从谈起；最终决定把记录交主席保管，听候依宪法成立的国会处置。至于富兰克林关于太阳的那段话，笔记记的是他望着椅背，对身边几位成员说的——那是对身边几个人说的话，不是对全场的正式演说。另外，本篇只写到1787年9月17日会议散会为止，不涉及宪法此后的批准与修正。

== 想一想 ==
部门合并方案的定稿会上，你是表决成员之一：方案里有一条你明确反对的规定，但今天必须定稿，明天就要对外宣布，主持人请你在决议上签字。

在这种场合，你会把签名理解成什么——证明“方案已获多数通过”、推荐“这是目前最好的选择”，还是承诺“我认可它的全部条款”？你愿意在哪一种理解下签字，在哪一种理解下宁可不签？

Rising or Setting: The Final Address Before Signing, September 17, 1787
A constitution with known flaws lay on the table: sign it or not, and how? On the last day of the Constitutional Convention: a deliberately ambiguous signing formula, several readings of what a signature meant, three refusals, and a sun painted on the back of the presiding officer's chair.

== Last Day: Draft and Signatures ==
September 17, 1787, the last day of the Constitutional Convention. The engrossed Constitution had just been read aloud when Benjamin Franklin rose with a speech in his hand; Madison's notes say he had written it out for his own convenience, and James Wilson read it to the hall. The meeting was about to dissolve. Many in the room knew the document on the table had flaws. One question was left: sign it or not, and how.

He opened with plain talk. There were several parts of this Constitution, he said, which "I do not at present approve", but he was not sure he would never approve them. He had lived long enough to change his mind again and again, even on important questions he had once thought settled, once better information or fuller consideration came along. So he confessed: "the older I grow, the more apt I am to doubt my own judgment." A man who stood up in public to admit he might be wrong was about to put his name to a document.

== Why Sign Anyway ==
His reason for consenting was down-to-earth: "I think a general government necessary for us." He would take the charter with all its faults, if they were such. By now the argument was not about whether the text was good, but about whether anything better lay within reach.

Nor was he sold on the escape route of calling another convention to write a better charter. Assemble a group of men for the advantage of their joint wisdom, he said, and you inevitably assemble with them all their prejudices, passions, errors of opinion, local interests and selfish views. "From such an assembly can a perfect production be expected?"

Plenty of others had reservations of their own. Gouverneur Morris said he had objections too, but regarded this plan as the best attainable and would take it with all its faults. Hamilton allowed that "No man's ideas were more remote from the plan than his own were known to be" — and asked what the alternative was. So the day's real dispute came into view: what did the act of signing actually mean?

== The Argument Over Signing ==
The hardest passage of the speech is about silence. Franklin said the opinions he had held of the Constitution's errors he sacrificed to the public good, and had never whispered a syllable of them abroad: "Within these walls they were born, and here they shall die." What was said in the chamber stayed in the chamber.

Then came the request. To any member who still objected he asked one small thing: join him in a readiness to "doubt a little of his own infallibility", and put his name to the instrument, "to make manifest our unanimity". Note the ask — not that everyone praise the document, but that every name appear on the same page.

He then moved that members sign, and offered a convenient form: "Done in Convention by the unanimous consent of the states present, the 17th of September, &c. In witness whereof, we have hereunto subscribed our names." Madison adds a backstage note: this ambiguous form had been drawn up by Gouverneur Morris in order to gain the dissenting members, and put into Franklin's hands so that it might have the better chance of success.

In the same closing stretch the convention was still adjusting a number. Gorham asked, if it was not too late, to reconsider the clause capping representation at one member for every forty thousand people — a clause that had produced so much discussion — and to strike out forty thousand in favour of thirty thousand. That would fix no absolute rule, he remarked, only give Congress greater latitude. When the president rose to put the question — the signature block names him George Washington, deputy from Virginia — he said his office had restrained him from speaking on questions before the House, yet he could not forbear this wish. Many members had thought the small proportion of representatives thin security for the rights and interests of the people; he acknowledged it had always appeared to himself among the exceptionable parts of the plan. The change passed without opposition, and the engrossed Constitution was agreed to for signing, every state answering aye.

Randolph then rose and, alluding to Franklin's observations, apologized for refusing to sign, notwithstanding the vast majority and venerable names that would give the plan their sanction. Refusing, he said, did not mean he would oppose the Constitution outside the convention's doors: "He meant only to keep himself free to be governed by his duty, as it should be prescribed by his future judgment." He refused because he thought the alternative put to the people would frustrate the object of the convention: nine states would fail to ratify the plan, and confusion must follow.

Then several members explained why they would sign. Morris said he had objections, but this was the best that was to be attained, and he would take it with all its faults; the majority had decided and he would abide by that determination. The moment the plan went forth, every other consideration would be laid aside and the great question would be whether there was to be a national government at all — with anarchy as the alternative. The signing in the proposed form, he pointed out, related only to the fact that the states present were unanimous. Hamilton urged that every member sign: a few characters of consequence, by opposing or even refusing, might do infinite mischief, kindling the latent sparks that lurked under an enthusiasm for the convention which might soon subside. Blount said he had declared he would not sign so as to pledge himself to support the plan, but the form relieved him: without committing himself, he could attest the fact that the plan was the unanimous act of the states in convention.

Franklin explained that when drawing up that paper he had not known that any particular member would refuse to sign, and hoped to be so understood. He felt a high sense of obligation to Randolph for bringing the plan forward in the first instance and for his help along the way, and hoped he would yet lay aside his objections and prevent the great mischief that the refusal of his name might produce. Randolph was not moved. To sign in the proposed form was, for him, the same as signing the Constitution; the change of form made no difference. Refusing might be the most awful step of his life, but conscience dictated it, and he could not hesitate, much less change.

Gerry then described the painful feelings of his situation and his fear that the present crisis might end in civil war — in Massachusetts especially, where one party was devoted to democracy, which he thought the worst of all political evils, and the other as violent at the opposite extreme. The form meant nothing to him either. But if it were not otherwise apparent, the refusals to sign should never be known from him. As for Franklin's remarks, he said, he could only view them as levelled at himself and the other gentlemen who meant not to sign.

Others found the form too coy. Pinckney thought ambiguity would win few converts; better to be candid and let the form speak the substance. He meant to sign with a view to supporting the Constitution with all his influence, and would pledge himself accordingly. Franklin's reply was light in tone but decisive: "It is too soon to pledge ourselves, before Congress and our constituents shall have approved the plan." Ingersoll offered another reading: the signature was neither a bare attestation of the fact nor a pledge to support the plan at all events, but "a recommendation of what, all things considered, was the most eligible".

On Franklin's motion for the signing form, ten states voted ay: New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina and Georgia. South Carolina was divided.

In the end every member signed except Randolph, Mason and Gerry, who declined to give the Constitution the sanction of their names; the convention then dissolved itself by an adjournment sine die, without naming a day to return. While the last members were signing, Franklin looked toward the president's chair, at the back of which a rising sun happened to be painted, and remarked to the few members near him that painters had found it difficult in their art to tell a rising sun from a setting one. "I have," said he, "often and often, in the course of the session, and the vicissitudes of my hopes and fears as to its issue, looked at that behind the president, without being able to tell whether it was rising or setting; but now, at length, I have the happiness to know that it is a rising, and not a setting sun."

== One Signature, Several Readings ==
Almost the whole day's argument circles a single act: writing a name at the foot of a page. On one reading, Morris's form split signing into two questions — what do you attest, and what do you commit to? Morris and Blount acknowledged only the fact that the states present were unanimous; Ingersoll read the signature as a recommendation; Pinckney held that it ought to be a commitment; Randolph thought the two inseparable, a signature however softly worded being still a signature. The author's inference: precisely because the act meant different things to different men could a majority stand beneath one row of names. The price was that the meaning of that row would need explaining ever after.

A second reading turns on Franklin's plea to "doubt a little of his own infallibility". The author's extension, not Franklin's words: what he offered may have been less a reason to call the plan right than a posture for signing under uncertainty. When another convention might well do no better, first admit you may be wrong, then put your name down. The author's alternative would be to require a written reservation beside each signature, but the author also grants that this would weaken the unanimity that some in the room wanted most that day.

== Signing Moments Today ==
Fictional modern scene one. A review committee has just turned down a plan that one colleague pushed hard for. Afterwards a member tells someone in the corridor that they had disagreed too and were simply outvoted. Franklin's line, "Within these walls they were born, and here they shall die," points to another way: argue the objection fully in the room and get it into the minutes, then stop treating a settled disagreement as gossip once you are outside. That is not hiding the outcome; it is declining to fight the same battle again in the hallway.

Or take a joint statement to be published, where you agree with most of its positions and object to the wording of one passage. One person tells you not to fuss; another warns that signing means endorsing all of it. What helps here is neither courage nor tact, but a distinction: is the signature attesting a fact, recommending a choice, or committing you?

== What We Can Know ==
The meeting is known chiefly from James Madison's notes, printed in Elliot's 1845 edition (Wikisource), a participant's record. The 1793 Works of Franklin carries a printed text of the speech under the title "Final Speech of Dr. Franklin in the late Federal Convention", which can be set beside it, but this piece has not collated the two word by word. All English quotations in the body of the piece are taken from Madison's notes; whether the wording of Franklin's speech differs between the two versions is not asserted.

The record's own provenance deserves a look. In these notes King suggests that the journals be destroyed or left in the president's custody, since made public they would be put to bad use by those wishing to prevent the Constitution's adoption; Wilson prefers the second course — false suggestions may be spread, and it should not be made impossible to contradict them — and the papers are left with the president, subject to the order of Congress, if ever formed under the Constitution. As for Franklin and the sun, the notes record that he observed to a few members near him: an aside to those beside him, not a formal address to the whole hall. In any case, this piece stops at the adjournment of 17 September 1787 and touches neither ratification nor amendment.

== Think about it ==
At the meeting that finalizes a department merger plan, you are one of those voting: the plan contains a provision you clearly oppose, but it must be settled today and announced tomorrow, and the chair asks you to sign the resolution.

In that setting, what would your signature mean — attesting that the plan has passed by a majority, recommending that this is the best available choice, or committing you to endorse every clause? Under which reading would you sign, and under which would you rather not?

Pause for ten seconds and decide what you would do.

--------------------
One possible approach
Assumptions: The plan must be finalized today; the cost of delay falls on everyone
Your objection is on the record of the meeting but was not adopted
Others will read your signature as full-throated endorsement
What you could do: First establish what a signature conventionally means on this occasion, separating attestation from commitment
Write your reservation into the minutes so that it is filed alongside the signature
When you sign, state in one sentence which meaning you intend, so it cannot be over-read afterwards
Another reasonable choice: The author's proposal: request a short reservation beside the signature, such as attesting only that the plan passed due process and not endorsing every clause. It shares something with the ambiguous form of 1787 but is not the same device; and the author adds a caution that it weakens the effect of unanimity, which has to be weighed.

== Sources and uncertainty ==
The record comes chiefly from Madison's notes (Elliot's 1845 edition, Wikisource), a participant's account; the speech as printed in the 1793 Works of Franklin can serve as a check, and the two have not been collated word by word here. All English quotations are verbatim fragments of Madison's notes; speeches are reported as the notes give them, and no motive or psychology has been supplied for anyone.
