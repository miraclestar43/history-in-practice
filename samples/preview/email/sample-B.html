一口井、一瓶捎到汉普斯特德的水，和一个本想推翻水泵说的牧师。

拆掉水泵把手，宽街的霍乱就停了吗？

1854年伦敦宽街，一口公共水泵的把手被拆掉。提出建议的医生自己提醒：病例在那之前已经在下降。把证据链补上的，是一位本以为能推翻水泵说的牧师。

十天，五百人，一口井

1854年8月31日起，伦敦宽街一带突然暴发霍乱。按斯诺的统计，剑桥街与宽街交汇处250码以内，十天里有五百多人因霍乱死亡；不到六天，灾情最重的几条街走空了四分之三以上的居民。

医生约翰·斯诺怀疑街角那口公共水泵。他拿不出能给人看的病原体，只有死亡名单和打听来的饮水情况：在这口泵供水的范围内，有61名死者据说常喝或偶尔喝宽街泵水，6例因亲友死的死、走的走而问不到，6例据说发病前不喝。

时间、地点、人物

地点是伦敦威斯敏斯特的圣詹姆斯教区。能下令处置水泵的是教区济贫委员会；教区的霍乱调查委员会后来增补了两名成员：斯诺，和亨利·怀特黑德牧师。当时还没有今天的细菌学说，斯诺只能说水里有“霍乱毒物”，它是什么、怎样致病，谁也拿不出实物。

先问谁没事

斯诺还去看谁没事。波兰街济贫院四分之三以上被死过人的房子包围，535名在院者只有5人死于霍乱；院里有自己的泵井，从不去宽街取水。按周围街道的死亡率，这里本该死一百多人。宽街啤酒厂有七十多名工人，没有人重症，只有两人轻微不适；老板说工人有麦芽酒配给，他相信他们根本不喝水，厂里还有一口深井。

最能说明问题的病例不在宽街。住在汉普斯特德西区的E太太几个月没到过宽街一带，只是喜欢那口泵的水，每天有车从宽街出发，给她捎一大瓶。8月31日捎来的水，她当晚喝了，周五又喝，周五傍晚发病，周六去世。来看她的侄女也喝了这水，回到伊斯灵顿地势高、环境好的家中后发病身亡。斯诺注明，当时这两处都没有霍乱。

9月7日晚，斯诺向教区济贫委员会陈述了这些情况，第二天，水泵把手被拆下。但他自己的统计显示，疫情在那之前已经转弱：每天新发病例从143降到116，再到54；把手拆下的9月8日，新发是12例。斯诺写道，到9月8日每日死亡已大为减少，井水“很可能”因为某种原因已经不再含有霍乱毒物。

怀特黑德牧师起初并不相信。他后来写道，刚听说暴发被归咎于水泵时，他对一位医生朋友说，仔细调查会推翻这种说法；他也坚持把每个难解的病例查到底：“这个问题太重要，不能为了一套理论的整齐而牺牲。”转折出现在他为别的事翻阅登记署周报的时候：宽街40号一个五个月大的女婴，9月2日死亡，死前腹泻已四天。他认识这家人，却一直没问她何时发病。他立刻上门，从孩子母亲那里得知：女婴8月28日发病，排泄物到8月30日停止；洗尿布的水，一部分倒进后院水槽，一部分倒进前院的化粪池，而化粪池紧挨着泵井。他马上报告委员会，委员会下令调查，结果“清楚地确立”了化粪池液体渗入井中的事实。

1855年，委员会一致认为，“霍乱区”内8月31日起那几天突然、猛烈而集中的暴发，“以某种方式”归因于饮用该井的不洁之水；至于水是怎样起作用的，委员会明确表示不支持任何一种假说。

为什么会这样

这次调查有说服力，靠的不是拆把手，而是问法：不只问死者喝了什么，也看没喝的人怎么样。济贫院和啤酒厂离井很近，却几乎没人受害；E太太和侄女远离宽街，却喝了那口井的水。一种解释是（作者推断）：这些“例外”比几十个典型病例更难用别的说法解释，比如“这一带空气不好”。

怀特黑德的转变同样要紧。他本以为调查会推翻水泵说，结果找到了最早的病例和污物进井的路径。一个抱着反对意见去查的人，查完改变了看法，这样的结论往往比一群本来就同意的人互相印证更可信。

证据也有边界。斯诺的饮水情况多是从别人那里打听来的，有问不到的，也可能有记错的。拆把手时疫情已在消退，所以不能说是拆把手终止了疫情；一种可能是，它挡住的是之后继续来喝这口井的人。

今天可以怎么用

假设你在一家做配送调度的小公司，连续三天傍晚订单大面积超时，人人都怀疑前天夜里那次发布。先别急着回滚：学斯诺先问一句，谁没事——那两个没超时的片区，是不是没走同一套调度服务？把没出事的人群列成清单，常常比盯着出事的那批人更快找到边界。

复盘会上，一位原本想证明不是新流程之过的同事翻出更早的日志，发现了真正的触发点。把他的查证过程原样记下来，哪怕结论不讨喜；同时把结论分成已确认和机制推测两栏，别让一份整齐的叙述掩盖证据到底有多硬。

别照搬的地方

这是一次靠观察和打听的调查，没有对照实验；今天排查公共卫生问题有化验和更严格的流行病学方法，方法本身不必照搬。也别把它讲成孤胆英雄的故事：斯诺提出了问题，济贫委员会拆了把手，调查委员会和怀特黑德补上了关键证据，而委员会对“水怎样致病”始终没有下结论。

想一想

虚构情境：一家连锁烘焙坊的三家门店在同一个周末出现顾客腹泻投诉，公司初步怀疑一批奶油；A门店已停用该批次，之后仍有零星投诉。负责采购的质量经理最初想证明不是奶油的问题。

在化验结果出来之前，你会依据什么证据停用这批奶油、通知顾客，还是保持沉默？停用的代价是销毁库存并承认风险，不停用的代价是可能继续有人受害——哪一种错误你觉得更难挽回？

先停十秒，想想你会怎么做。

一个参考思路

假设：假设投诉例数尚少、因果未确认，但该批次奶油确实覆盖三家门店；假设公开通知会触发监管上报流程。

可以怎么做：先列谁没事：未使用该批次的门店或产品线有无同类投诉；核对发病时间是否晚于批次到货时间；请一位不想要既定结论的同事独立复核订单与制作记录；设定临时下架的触发线，例如出现第二例与该批次相关的病例即停用。

另一种合理选择：另一种合理选择是先只在A门店小范围停用并留样化验、暂不公开通知：理由是证据尚弱，避免错误归因和恐慌；代价是若判断错误会延误。

出处与不确定性

主要事实与数字出自斯诺1855年的著作及他提交调查委员会的报告；怀特黑德的转变、40号女婴和化粪池调查出自同一份委员会报告里他本人的记述与委员会的调查。两者是不同的来源基础，但并非完全独立：斯诺本人也是委员会成员。饮水情况多为转述，有问不到的病例；E太太与侄女两例来自她儿子的转述。拆把手与疫情下降的因果无法确定。1854年没有细菌学说，“霍乱毒物”是斯诺当时的说法。本篇未取得中央卫生机构对此事的意见，因此不写其立场。

Did Removing the Pump Handle Stop the Broad Street Cholera?

In 1854 the handle of a public pump on Broad Street, London, was taken off. The doctor behind it pointed out that cases were already falling. The evidence was completed by a clergyman who expected to disprove the pump theory.

Ten Days, Five Hundred Dead, One Well

From 31 August 1854, cholera broke out around Broad Street in London. By John Snow's count, within 250 yards of the corner of Cambridge Street and Broad Street more than five hundred people died of cholera in ten days. In less than six days, the worst-hit streets were deserted by more than three-quarters of their inhabitants.

Dr John Snow suspected the public pump on the corner. He had no germ to show anyone, only a list of the dead and what he could learn about what they drank. In the area the pump served, he was told that 61 of the dead had drunk Broad Street pump water, regularly or now and then; in 6 cases nobody connected with the dead could be found; and in 6 he was told they had not drunk it before falling ill.

Time, Place, People

The place was the parish of St James, Westminster. The body that could act on the pump was the parish Board of Guardians; the parish's Cholera Inquiry Committee later added two members: Snow and the Reverend Henry Whitehead. Germ theory did not yet exist. Snow could only speak of a "cholera poison" in the water; nobody could show what it was or how it worked.

First Ask Who Was Fine

Snow also looked at who was spared. The workhouse in Poland Street was more than three-quarters surrounded by houses where people had died of cholera, yet of its 535 inmates only 5 died of it. It had its own pump-well and never sent to Broad Street for water; at the death rate of the surrounding streets, more than a hundred would have died there. The brewery on Broad Street had more than seventy workmen; none fell seriously ill and only two were slightly unwell. The owner said the men had an allowance of malt liquor and he believed they did not drink water at all; the brewery also had a deep well of its own.

The most telling case was not on Broad Street at all. Mrs E, who lived at West End, Hampstead, had not been near Broad Street for months, but she liked the water from that pump, and a cart going out from Broad Street each day brought her a large bottle. She drank the water brought on 31 August that evening and again on Friday, fell ill on Friday evening and died on Saturday. Her niece, who was visiting, drank it too; she went home to a high, healthy part of Islington, fell ill and also died. Snow noted that there was no cholera at the time in either place.

On the evening of 7 September Snow laid these facts before the parish Board of Guardians, and the next day the pump handle was removed. Yet his own figures show the outbreak was already weakening: new attacks fell from 143 a day to 116, then to 54, and on 8 September, the day the handle came off, there were 12. Snow wrote that "the daily number of fatal attacks was already much diminished by September the 8th" and that "it is not improbable that the water had, from some cause or other, ceased to contain the cholera poison".

The Reverend Henry Whitehead started out a sceptic. He later wrote that when he first heard the outbreak blamed on the pump, he told a medical friend that a careful investigation would disprove that theory; and he insisted on chasing every awkward case: "It is far too important a subject to be sacrificed to the symmetry of a theory." The turn came while he was reading the Registrar's returns for another purpose. An entry recorded the death on 2 September of a five-month-old girl at 40 Broad Street, after four days of diarrhoea. He knew the family but had never asked when she fell ill. He went straight to the house. The mother told him the baby had been taken ill on 28 August and that the discharges stopped on 30 August; the water used to soak her napkins had been poured partly into a sink in the back yard and partly into a cesspool in the front area, close to the pump well. He reported this to the committee, which ordered an inquiry. It "clearly established the general fact of percolation of fluid from the cesspool into the well".

In 1855 the committee was "unanimously of opinion" that the sudden, severe and concentrated outbreak in the "Cholera area" from 31 August "was in some manner attributable to the use of the impure water of the well". On how the water acted, it refrained "from expressing an opinion in favour of any hypothesis".

Why It Worked

What made the inquiry persuasive was not the handle but the questions: not only what the dead had drunk, but what happened to those who had not. The workhouse and the brewery stood next to the well and were barely touched; Mrs E and her niece lived far away but drank its water. One interpretation, the author's, is that such exceptions are harder to explain any other way, for example by bad air in the district, than dozens of typical cases.

Whitehead's change of mind matters just as much. He had expected the inquiry to disprove the pump theory, and instead found the earliest case and a route by which waste reached the well. A conclusion reached by someone who went looking to refute it is often more convincing than agreement among people who were convinced from the start.

The evidence has limits. Most of what Snow knew about drinking habits came second-hand; some cases could not be traced and some answers may have been wrong. The outbreak was already fading when the handle came off, so it cannot be said that removing it ended the epidemic. One possibility is that it kept later visitors from drinking at the well.

How You Can Use It Today

Suppose you work for a small delivery-scheduling company, and for three evenings in a row a large share of orders has run late, and everyone suspects the release from the night before last. Don't rush to roll it back: do as Snow did and ask first who was fine — did the two districts with no delays run a different scheduling service? Drawing up a list of the groups that were untouched often finds the boundary faster than staring at the group that broke.

In the review meeting, a colleague who originally wanted to prove it was not the new process dug out earlier logs and found the real trigger. Write up his verification process exactly as it happened, even if the conclusion is unwelcome; and at the same time split the findings into two columns, confirmed and mechanism conjecture, so that a tidy narrative doesn't hide how solid the evidence really is.

What Not to Copy

This inquiry rested on observation and second-hand reports, with no controlled experiment; public-health investigations today have laboratory tests and stricter epidemiological methods, so the method itself is not a template. And do not tell it as a lone-hero story: Snow posed the question, the Board of Guardians removed the handle, the inquiry committee and Whitehead supplied key evidence, and the committee never settled how the water caused disease.

Think about it

Fictional situation: three stores of a bakery chain receive customer complaints of diarrhoea over the same weekend, and the company first suspects a batch of cream; store A has already stopped using that batch, yet sporadic complaints continue. The quality manager in charge of purchasing first wants to prove that the cream is not at fault.

Before the lab results come back, on what evidence would you stop using this batch of cream and notify customers, or stay silent? The cost of stopping is destroying stock and admitting a risk; the cost of not stopping is that more people may be harmed — which mistake do you think is harder to undo?

Pause for ten seconds and decide what you would do.

One possible approach

Assumptions: Assume the number of cases is still small and causation is unconfirmed, but that batch of cream really does cover all three stores; assume that a public notice would trigger a regulatory reporting process.

What you could do: First list who was fine: are there similar complaints from stores or product lines that did not use this batch; check whether the onset times come after the batch arrived; ask a colleague who does not want the settled conclusion to review the orders and production records independently; set a trigger line for temporary withdrawal, for example stopping the batch as soon as a second case linked to it appears.

Another reasonable choice: Another reasonable choice is to withdraw the batch only at store A on a small scale, keep samples for testing, and not make a public notice for now: the reason is that the evidence is still weak and this avoids wrong attribution and panic; the cost is that a wrong call delays action.

Sources and uncertainty

Most facts and figures come from Snow's 1855 book and his report to the inquiry committee. Whitehead's change of mind, the infant at No. 40 and the cesspool inquiry come from his own account and the committee's inquiry in the same 1855 report. These are different source bases but not fully independent: Snow himself sat on the committee. Drinking habits were mostly reported second-hand, and some cases could not be traced; the cases of Mrs E and her niece come from her son. Whether removing the handle caused the decline cannot be established. Germ theory did not exist in 1854; "cholera poison" is Snow's own term. This article has not drawn on the views of national health authorities, so it does not describe their position.
