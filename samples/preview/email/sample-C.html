快要毕业时，一百个留学生被叫回国

一百二十个孩子被派去美国，计划读十五年。第九年，约一百人被召回。叫停他们的不是一件事，而是一连串报告、拒绝、奏折与征询。

答复与撤回

1870年代末，容闳为几名学业已经足够好的中国留学生，向美国国务院申请进入西点军校和安纳波利斯海军学院。答复只有一句：“没有为中国学生准备的位置。”容闳后来写道，他把这次碰壁报告给李鸿章，李的回信让他立刻明白，这个计划的命运已经注定。

1881年，大约一百名学生被召回国。耶鲁校长波特等人联名致信总理衙门，说这些年轻人恰在“就要从此前的学业中收获最重要成果”的时候被带走。

时间、地点、人物

这个计划始于同治十一年正月（1872年）：两江总督曾国藩、直隶总督李鸿章奏请挑选幼童赴美肄业，由陈兰彬和容闳管理学生、常驻美国。按容闳的记述，计划共送一百二十人，分四批、每年三十人；年龄十二到十五岁；家长要签字，同意孩子出洋读书十五年。1872年夏末，第一批三十人准备横渡太平洋。

办法不是凭空来的。按容闳的记述，他到南京见曾国藩，把几件要紧事当面定了下来：设一所预备学校；选派多少人；学生在外的经费从哪里来；可以在国外待多少年。这四条落定，计划才有骨架。

从招人到撤回

第一个难题是招人。容闳写道，当时没有中文报纸可以把消息传到民间，预备学校开办之初报名者寥寥；来报名的都是广东人，尤其香山县。一百二十名官费学生中九成来自南方，他把原因归到消息传不出去。

预备学校开办几个月后，按容闳的记法，曾国藩于“1871年冬”在南京去世，享年七十一岁，容闳称之为“无法弥补的损失”。这一条照容闳所记写。按《清史纪事本末》，曾国藩与李鸿章联名奏请是在同治十一年正月（1872年初）；两份材料的日期对不上，本篇不自行调和。

到了新英格兰，分歧先出现在日常小事上。容闳列出他与陈兰彬反复争执的事项：学生的学费和零用、假期开销、改换服装、参加家庭礼拜、去主日学和教堂、户外运动和球类。容闳说，他夹在中间当“缓冲”，为学生的合理要求辩护。

1876年秋，Woo Tsze Tung 接任监督。以下都出自容闳的转述：此人一上任就处处挑错，又不当面提出，而是不断向北京写信，说学生管理失当、被容闳纵容，模仿美国学生搞体育，玩多学少，结成宗教和政治性质的秘密团体，不听新监督的话，许多人上教堂、成了基督徒，再这样下去会忘了自己的国家；越早解散、全部撤回，对中国越好。容闳还说此人对化学有兴趣却从无长进，一向被朋友们“视为怪人”——这是当事一方的敌意描述。

军校申请碰壁后，Woo 再次发力，据容闳说，这一次有陈兰彬暗中配合。一位御史借美国的排华情绪上奏，请求解散留学事务、撤回全部学生。朝廷先征询李鸿章，他同意撤回；再问陈兰彬，他认为学生在美已经够久；Woo 主张立即撤回、回国后严加看管。容闳则被完全排除在征询之外。1881年，约一百名学生全部回国。

抗议也不是自发涌出来的。容闳记下组织者：他的老友特威切尔牧师和莱恩牧师；两人反复奔走，说动耶鲁校长波特、西利校长，以及塞缪尔·克莱门斯（笔名马克·吐温）、弗里林海森、约翰·拉塞尔·杨等人出面，想拦住这股倒退。

这封致总理衙门的抗议信，措辞出自波特，容闳形容它“最为庄重、坦率而有丈夫气”。信中写道，这些年轻人恰在“就要从此前的学业中收获最重要成果”的时候被带走；“我们尚未完成交给我们的工作”，成果却在完成之前就被否定；至于学生若忘了中文，“我们从未承担教他们母语的责任”。这封信，我们今天是通过容闳书中的转载读到的。

记住一个具体的人：詹天佑。据1917年的《游美同学录》，他同治十一年（1872）以官费赴美，光绪四年（1878）进耶鲁大学（原书作「耶路大學」）学土木工程，光绪七年（1881）得学士学位，以数学成绩获奖，同年回国。回国后，他先在福州船政局的水师学堂就读；从光绪十四年（1888）起，才历任京张铁路等多条铁路的工程师、总工程师。

召回之后的路并不只有一条。据1917年的《游美同学录》，唐绍仪，广东香山人，官费赴美，进哥伦比亚大学，光绪七年（1881）回国；此后历任朝鲜海关帮办、驻朝鲜领事、津海关道、赴藏特使、署外务部右侍郎、奉天巡抚、赴美钦差大臣，宣统三年署邮传部尚书，又充全权代表，与民军首领议和，民国元年任内阁总理。梁敦彦，广东顺德人，先在香港维多利亚学校读书，同治十一年官费赴美，在哈特福德读小学、中学，光绪四年（1878）进耶鲁，光绪七年（1881）回国，先在天津电报学堂教书，后任外务部侍郎、尚书，民国三年（1914）任交通部总长；该书另记宣统三年（1911）耶鲁赠予他学士及文学博士学位。

为什么会这样

这不是一个人的决定。容闳记下的是一条链：监督的报告、军校的拒绝、御史的奏折、朝廷对三位官员的征询。一种解释是（作者推断）：决定所依据的，主要是学生“变成了什么样的人”，而不是他们学会了什么；最能说明学业的容闳被排除在征询之外，材料里也没有朝廷征询美国教师的记载。不过清廷撤回的奏折原文本篇没有取得，决策者当时究竟怎样权衡，现有材料证明不了。

另一点是时间。按原计划，学生要在美国读十五年；召回时，最早一批去了大约九年。詹天佑恰好赶上毕业，而他学到的本事，又过了七年才用上。长期投入最容易在“快见效、还没见效”的时候被叫停——这是作者的观察，不是史料的结论。

今天可以怎么用

现代虚构场景：一家零售公司试点新的库存系统，厂商说要十四个月才见效，第六个月的例会上，大家只看到“账更乱了”和几条操作别扭的抱怨。可以先做一件事：把试点前写下的验收指标翻出来对照，请天天用这套系统的店长参加评估，把“过渡期的混乱”和“方案无效”分开写进结论。

现代虚构场景：一个五年期的社区阅读项目由一位副校长力主，开办第二年他调走了，新领导上任第一件事就是重新审议。可以借的做法是：项目设立时就把目标、期限、中期检查节点和由谁检查写下来，让一个长期计划不只靠一位支持者撑着。

别照搬的地方

本篇关于撤回经过与理由的叙述，主要来自容闳1909年的回忆录，他是当事一方，对陈兰彬与 Woo Tsze Tung 带有强烈敌意；清廷撤回奏折的原文本篇未取得。“Woo Tsze Tung 一般认为即吴子登”这条对应关系，本证据包没有直接证明，只能列为待核实背景。美国排华氛围同样是容闳的描述，波特抗议信的文本也只见于容闳书中的转载。计划起步、招生和曾国藩去世的细节，同样出自容闳单方记述；唐绍仪、梁敦彦的履历出自1917年《游美同学录》，为单一来源。所以“为什么撤回”这个问题，现有材料给出的是一面之词加一封抗议信，不足以定论，更不宜当作组织决策的范本。

作者提出的替代做法只放在这里备查：若今天复盘此事，可以提出“先派小批试读、设复查节点”这类方案，用来对照原计划一次定下十五年期限的做法。这类替代是作者的设想，史料里没有人这样讨论过。

想一想

一家公司的三年期数据工程外派培养计划进行到第二年半，业务线主管反映学员“文化上不像自己人”，而学员刚要进入核心项目。

中途评估一个长期培养计划时，如果“是否融入组织文化”和“专业产出何时兑现”指向相反结论，该以什么证据决定继续、收缩还是叫停？谁有资格下这个判断？

先停十秒，想想你会怎么做。

一个参考思路

假设：计划目标是培养能独立负责核心系统的人才，三年为约定期限，已有两年半的过程数据；文化感受主要是印象，专业产出有可核对的记录。

可以怎么做：先把两类指标分开列：可核对的产出（交付、考核、证书）与主观印象（“像不像自己人”），分开计分；让用人方、执行方、学员三方到场陈述；最后设定明确的兑现时间点和止损线，再决定是否继续。

另一种合理选择：另一种合理选择是立即收缩、只留少数人走完最后半年：理由是文化摩擦已经影响团队协作，即便专业产出即将兑现，组织代价也可能太高。这是作者提出的替代方案。

出处与不确定性

撤回奏折原文本篇未取得；撤回的经过与理由主要出自容闳（Yung Wing）1909年回忆录，他是当事一方，对陈兰彬与 Woo Tsze Tung 有强烈敌意，其转述的 Woo 报告内容、御史奏请的动机、美国排华氛围均未经独立佐证。波特等人的抗议信文本经容闳书转载，未见原件。Woo Tsze Tung 一般认为即吴子登，但本证据包未直接证明该对应，列为待核实。曾国藩去世的时间与年龄照容闳所记（“1871年冬”、七十一岁），通行纪年不同，本篇不自行改正；计划起步、招生与预备学校的细节同样出自容闳单方记述。詹天佑履历出自1917年《游美同学录》，为单一来源；其1881年获耶鲁土木工程学士、同年回国属该书记载（光绪七年）。唐绍仪、梁敦彦履历亦出自该书，官职名称照原书。陈兰彬与容闳争执事项的列举出自容闳单方记述。人名对应：陈兰彬即容闳书中的 Chin Lan Pin，《清史纪事本末》误作“陳蘭斌”，并把容闳误作“容閑”；这类对应属通行考订，非本篇证据直接给出。“马克·吐温”是塞缪尔·克莱门斯的笔名，属背景说明，不出自本篇证据。

History in Practice · 2026-11-04 · Modern China

Just Before Graduation, a Hundred Students Were Called Home

One hundred and twenty boys were sent to America on a fifteen-year plan. In the ninth year, about a hundred were recalled. What ended it was not one event but a chain of reports, refusals, a memorial and a round of consultations.

The Answer and the Recall

In the late 1870s Yung Wing, the mission's Chinese commissioner, applied to the U.S. State Department to have several of the students — by then advanced enough in their studies — admitted to West Point and the Naval Academy at Annapolis. The reply came in a single sentence: "There is no room provided for Chinese students." Yung Wing later wrote that when he reported the rebuff to Viceroy Li Hongzhang, the Viceroy's answer convinced him at once that the fate of the mission was sealed.

In 1881, about a hundred students were summoned home. President Porter of Yale and other American well-wishers wrote jointly to the Zongli Yamen — the Qing office for foreign affairs — to protest that the young men had been taken away precisely when they were "about to reap the most important advantages from their previous studies."

Time, Place, People

The scheme began in the first month of the eleventh year of Tongzhi (1872): Zeng Guofan, Governor-General of Liangjiang, and Li Hongzhang, Governor-General of Zhili, memorialized the throne to select boys for study in the United States, with Chen Lanbin and Yung Wing put in charge of the students and resident in America to manage everything. By Yung Wing's account the plan called for 120 boys in all, sent in four installments of thirty a year; candidates had to be between twelve and fifteen; and parents or guardians had to sign a paper agreeing to let their sons go abroad to study for fifteen years. In the late summer of 1872 the first installment of thirty was ready to cross the Pacific.

None of the rules were improvised. Yung Wing records that he went to Nanjing to see Zeng Guofan and settled the essential points face to face: a preparatory school would be established; how many students would be selected and sent abroad; where the money to support them there would come from; and how many years they would be allowed to remain. With those four fixed, the scheme had its skeleton.

From Recruiting to Recall

The first difficulty was finding anyone to recruit. Yung Wing writes that with no Chinese-language newspapers to carry the news to the public, few applications arrived when the preparatory school opened. All of them came from Cantonese families, and especially from the district of Heang Shan (Xiangshan). Nine-tenths of the 120 government students were southerners, and he puts the skew down to the news failing to travel.

A few months after the school opened, Zeng Guofan died in Nanjing — Yung Wing dates it to "the winter of 1871" — at the age of seventy-one, a blow he records as "an irreparable loss" to China. The date and age are Yung Wing's. The Qing history dates Zeng and Li's joint memorial to the first month of the eleventh year of Tongzhi (early 1872); the two sources do not line up, and this piece does not try to reconcile them.

In New England the disagreements surfaced first over small daily matters. Yung Wing lists what he and Chen Lanbin quarrelled over again and again: the students' school and personal expenses, vacation costs, changes of costume, attendance at family worship, attendance at Sunday school and church services, outdoor exercise and athletic games. He says he "had to stand as a kind of buffer between Chin and the students," defending their reasonable claims.

In the fall of 1876 a new commissioner took office, Woo Tsze Tung. Everything that follows is Yung Wing's retelling: Woo found fault with everything from the day he arrived, and rather than raise his complaints in person, he sent a clandestine stream of letters to Peking alleging that the students had been mismanaged and spoiled by Commissioner Yung; that they imitated American students in athletics, played more than they studied, and formed secret societies, religious and political; that they ignored their teachers and would not heed the new commissioner; that many went to church, attended Sunday school and had become Christians; and that left to their own devices they would soon lose all love of their country and be good for nothing on their return. The sooner the enterprise was broken up and everyone recalled, he argued, the better for China. Yung Wing also notes that Woo had "a decided taste for chemistry" but never made progress in it and "was regarded by all his friends as a crank" — one interested party's hostile characterisation of another.

After the military academies' refusal, Woo pressed again — this time, Yung Wing says, with Chen Lanbin's covert cooperation. A censor from the reactionary ranks took advantage of strong anti-Chinese prejudice in America to memorialize the government to dissolve the mission and recall every student. The court first consulted Li Hongzhang, who refused to stand up for the students and consented to the recall; then Chen Lanbin, whose experience abroad was supposed to make him the authority, pronounced that the students had been in the United States long enough; and Woo urged recall without delay and strict supervision afterwards. Yung Wing was ruled out of the consultation altogether. In 1881, about a hundred students went home.

The protest did not spring up by itself. Yung Wing names its organizers: his life-long friend the Rev. J. H. Twichell and the Rev. John W. Lane, through whose persistent efforts Presidents Porter and Seelye, Samuel Clemens — better known as Mark Twain — T. F. Frelinghuysen, John Russell Young and others were enlisted to try to halt the retrogression on the Chinese side.

Yung Wing describes the protest letter to the Zongli Yamen as couched in "the most dignified, frank and manly language of President Porter of Yale." The letter said the young men had been taken away just when they were "about to reap the most important advantages from their previous studies"; that "We have not finished the work which we were expected to perform," yet its results were judged unfavourably before it could possibly be finished; and that if the students had neglected their native language, "we never assumed the duty of instructing them in it." We read this letter today only through the text reprinted in Yung Wing's book.

Keep one specific life in mind: Zhan Tianyou. According to the Record of Students Returned from America, compiled by Tsinghua School in 1917, he first studied at the mission's Shanghai office; in the eleventh year of Tongzhi (1872) he went to America at government expense; in the fourth year of Guangxu (1878) he entered Yale — printed in the original as "耶路大學" — to read civil engineering; in the seventh year of Guangxu (1881) he took a bachelor's degree, was elected to an honour society and won a prize for mathematics, and returned home the same year. He then studied at the naval academy of the Fuzhou shipyard. Only from the fourteenth year of Guangxu (1888) did he serve, in turn, as section engineer, deputy engineer and chief engineer on the Beijing–Zhangjiakou railway and other lines.

The road after recall did not run in only one direction. According to the same 1917 record, Tang Shaoyi of Xiangshan, Guangdong, went to America at government expense, entered Columbia University and returned in the seventh year of Guangxu (1881); he went on to serve as assistant in the Korean customs, consul in Korea, intendant of the Tianjin customs circuit, special envoy to Tibet, acting junior vice-president of the Foreign Office, governor of Fengtian and imperial envoy to the United States; in the third year of Xuantong he was acting Minister of Posts and Communications and also plenipotentiary for peace talks with the revolutionary leaders, and in the first year of the Republic he became premier. Liang Dunyan of Shunde, Guangdong, had studied first at the Victoria School in Hong Kong; he went to America at government expense in the eleventh year of Tongzhi, attended primary and secondary school at Hartford, entered Yale in the fourth year of Guangxu (1878) and returned in the seventh (1881). He taught at the Tianjin telegraph school, later served as vice-minister and then minister of the Foreign Office, and in the third year of the Republic (1914) became Minister of Communications. The same book also records that Yale conferred a degree on him in the third year of Xuantong (1911).

Why It Ended This Way

This was not one man's decision. Yung Wing's account traces a chain: the commissioner's reports, the military academies' refusal, the censor's memorial, and the court's round of consultations with three officials. One explanation — the author's inference — is that the decision turned mainly on what kind of people the students had become rather than on what they had learned; Yung Wing, best placed to speak for the students' studies, was shut out of the consultation, and the sources record no consultation of the American teachers. But the text of the memorial that ended the mission could not be obtained for this piece, and what the decision-makers actually weighed is not something the surviving material can prove.

Then there is timing. The original plan gave the students fifteen years in America; the recall came after about nine years for the earliest batch. Zhan Tianyou just managed to graduate — and seven more years passed before the skills he had learned were put to use. Long-term investments are easiest to cancel in the window when results are nearly in hand but not yet visible. That is the author's observation, not a conclusion the sources draw.

How This Can Be Used Today

Modern fictional scenario: a retail company pilots a new inventory system. The vendor says it will take fourteen months to show results, and at the sixth-month review all anyone can see is "the books are messier than before" plus a handful of complaints about awkward procedures. One thing to do first: dig out the acceptance criteria written down before the pilot and measure against them; invite the store managers who live in the system every day to take part in the evaluation; and write the conclusion so that "transition-period chaos" and "the scheme does not work" are kept apart.

Modern fictional scenario: a five-year community reading programme was championed by a deputy head teacher, who moved to another post in its second year; the new head's first act was to put it up for review. The move worth borrowing is to write down, at the start, the goals, the term, the mid-point checks and who carries them out, so that a long programme does not rest on one supporter.

What Not to Copy

The account here of how and why the recall happened rests mainly on Yung Wing's 1909 memoir. He was one of the parties involved and writes of Chen Lanbin and Woo Tsze Tung with open hostility; the text of the Qing memorial ordering the recall could not be obtained. The usual identification of Woo Tsze Tung with Wu Zideng is not directly established by this evidence, so it is listed as background to be checked. The anti-Chinese mood in America is likewise Yung Wing's description, and the text of the Porter protest survives only in the reprint in his book. The details of the mission's start, its recruiting and Zeng Guofan's death also come from Yung Wing's one-sided account; the career records of Tang Shaoyi and Liang Dunyan come from the 1917 Record of Students Returned from America, a single source. So on the question of why they were recalled, what we have is one side's story plus one protest letter — not enough to settle the matter, and certainly not a model of organizational decision-making to imitate.

The alternative the author offers is recorded here only for reference: reviewing the episode today, one might propose sending a small trial group first and setting review checkpoints, as a counterweight to the original plan's single, fifteen-year commitment. This alternative is the author's invention; no one in the historical record discussed anything of the kind.

Think about it

A company's three-year data-engineering training secondment is two and a half years in. The business-line manager reports that the trainees "don't quite fit our culture" — just as they are about to move onto a core project.

When you assess a long-term training program midway, and "do they fit the organization's culture" and "when will the professional output land" point to opposite conclusions, what evidence should decide whether to continue, scale back, or stop? And who is qualified to make that call?

Pause for ten seconds and decide what you would do.

One possible approach

Assumptions: The goal is to produce people who can independently take charge of core systems; three years is the agreed term; two and a half years of process data already exist; the cultural judgements are impressions, while the professional output is backed by checkable records.

What you could do: First list the two kinds of measure separately: checkable output (deliverables, assessments, certificates) and subjective impressions ("do they fit in"), and score them apart. Bring the hiring side, the program side and the trainees into the room to state their cases. Then set explicit dates for the payoff and a stop-loss line, and only afterwards decide whether to continue.

Another reasonable choice: Another defensible choice is to scale back at once and keep only a few trainees for the final six months: the reasoning is that cultural friction has already damaged team collaboration, and even if professional output is about to land, the organizational cost may be too high. This is the author's alternative.

Sources and uncertainty

The text of the memorial ordering the recall could not be obtained for this piece. The course and reasons of the recall come mainly from Yung Wing's 1909 memoir; he was a party to the affair and strongly hostile to Chen Lanbin and Woo Tsze Tung, and his retelling of Woo's reports, the censor's motives, and the anti-Chinese mood in America all lack independent corroboration. The text of the Porter protest is known through the reprint in Yung Wing's book; no original has been seen. Woo Tsze Tung is generally taken to be Wu Zideng, but this evidence pack does not directly establish that correspondence and it is listed as unverified. Zeng Guofan's date and age of death are given as Yung Wing gives them ("the winter of 1871," seventy-one); the standard chronology differs, and this piece does not correct it. The details of the mission's launch, its recruiting and the preparatory school likewise come from Yung Wing's one-sided account. Zhan Tianyou's record comes from the 1917 Record of Students Returned from America, a single source; his 1881 Yale bachelor's degree in civil engineering and his return home the same year are that book's entry (the seventh year of Guangxu). The career records of Tang Shaoyi and Liang Dunyan also come from that book, with official titles as printed there. The list of the points Chen Lanbin and Yung Wing quarrelled over comes from Yung Wing alone. Name correspondences: Chen Lanbin is "Chin Lan Pin" in Yung Wing's book; the Qing shi jishi benmo (Complete Accounts of Qing History) misprints his given-name character and renders Yung Wing's name incorrectly; such correspondences are standard identifications, not something this evidence directly supplies. That Samuel Clemens wrote as Mark Twain is background knowledge, not stated in the evidence used here.
