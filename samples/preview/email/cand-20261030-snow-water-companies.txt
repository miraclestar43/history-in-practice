同一条街上的两根水管
1854年，伦敦南部两家自来水公司的管道铺在同一批街道上，隔壁两户人家喝的水可能完全不同。约翰·斯诺决定用这场没人设计的实验，检验自己倡导了五年的学说。

== 一场没人设计、却有人记录的实验 ==
1854年7月，霍乱又回到了伦敦。约翰·斯诺为一种学说辩护已经五年，这一次他写下的决心很直白：不惜一切力气，弄清供水对疫情进程的确切影响；地点选在“一切条件都如此恰好适合这项调查”的街区；而且要亲自去查，因为他要的是“最令人满意的证明”，来检验这个学说究竟是对还是错。

他看中的是伦敦南部的一片街区。那里有两家自来水公司，水管各自铺进所有街道，几乎深入每一条巷子和院落；哪栋房子接哪一家，是当年两家公司激烈竞争时由房主或住户定下的。结果，很多房子的水和左右两边的邻居都不一样。斯诺看到的，是一场没有人安排、却已经摆在眼前的对照。

== 两家公司，两处取水口 ==
先看水从哪里来。1832年到1849年间，伦敦的供水变动很多：南华克公司与南伦敦公司合并，成立南华克和沃克斯霍尔公司，撤掉伦敦桥的水厂，改从沃克斯霍尔桥上游约半英里、巴特西原野一带的泰晤士河取水；兰贝斯公司则一直在亨格福德市场对面取水。

从1849年下半年到1853年8月，伦敦没有霍乱。就在这段间隙里，兰贝斯公司把水厂从亨格福德市场对面迁到泰晤士迪顿，于是得到一份“完全不含伦敦污水”的水。迁址的年份，斯诺在一条脚注里专门纠正过巴利医生：巴利以为兰贝斯公司1849年就已在泰晤士迪顿取水，其实到1852年才迁过去。在两家混供的地区，南华克和沃克斯霍尔公司的管道依旧和兰贝斯的管道一起铺在每一条街上。

在斯诺看来，这个格局再理想不过。两家公司都给富人也给穷人供水，大房子小房子都有；他断言，接这家水和接那家水的人，在房屋、人口和周围的一切物质条件上都没有任何差别，因此想不出比这更能彻底检验供水作用的实验——它是环境“现成摆在观察者面前”的。规模也大得惊人：不少于三十万人，不分男女老幼、职业身份，从体面人家到赤贫者，在没有选择、多数也不知情的情况下分成了两组：一组的水里有伦敦的污水，以及霍乱病人排出的任何东西；另一组的水没有这类污物。

== 挨家挨户查水 ==
他并不是一开始就看清了这一点。斯诺承认，前一年年底白天短，他抽不出时间去查；而且那时他也没有充分意识到两家公司的供水混得这么密，因而没意识到这项调查有多重要。要把这场“大实验”用起来，需要的其实只有一件事：查清每一栋发生霍乱死亡的房子，用的是哪家的水。

1854年，他从一份名单入手。他向登记总署申请，拿到了两家公司混供地区霍乱死者的住址：一部分刊登在每周公报上，其余的获准抄录。8月中旬，他先查兰贝斯的两个分区——肯宁顿第一区和第二区。到8月12日，这两个分区共死亡44人；他查出其中38户由南华克和沃克斯霍尔公司供水，4户由兰贝斯公司供水，另有2户用自家院里的水井，不接任何一家公司的水。

他一弄清这些数字，就告诉了法尔医生（Dr. Farr）。斯诺记下法尔对结果“深受触动”，并在法尔的建议下，伦敦南部各区的登记员被要求在每一例霍乱死亡中登记发病所在房屋的供水。这项规定从8月26日以后才执行，所以斯诺决定把自己的调查一直做到这一天，好让整场疫情的事实都有据可查。一个人在街上查出的苗头，就这样变成了官方登记里的一栏。

他把调查扩展到兰贝斯、南华克和纽因顿其他混供的分区，结果与肯宁顿“非常相似”。有些病人发病后被送进济贫院或别处，他就去查发病时所住那栋房子的水，而不是死在哪里。

麻烦出在归类。很少有人家能当场答出自家用哪家公司的水；就算水费是住户自己交，他们通常也得翻出收据才想得起公司名字。按周交房租的工人家庭更难：水费一律由房东或其代理人缴纳，房东往往住得很远，住户对此一无所知。斯诺写道，要不是发现能用化学检验把两家的水确定无疑地分开，这项调查几乎不可能完成。

检验靠的是两种水里氯化钠——也就是食盐——含量的巨大差别。往水里加硝酸银，两家的水反应差得很远，一眼就能分辨。住户说不清时，他就用小瓶装一点水，在瓶盖上写下地址，回家再验。他也看水的样子，尤其是刚流进来、还没进水桶或水箱的时候；他还摸清了两家公司的放水工各在几点到哪条街，水来的时辰也能透露是哪家的水。不过这些只用来佐证更确凿的证据，不单独作数。

疫情头七周的结果，他整理成一张表。南华克和沃克斯霍尔公司供水40,046户，霍乱死亡1,263人；兰贝斯公司供水26,107户，死亡98人；伦敦其余地区256,423户，死亡1,422人。折算成每10,000户的死亡数，分别是315、37和59。他的结论是，前者的死亡率是后者的八到九倍。

作者的解读：两组数字方向一致。肯宁顿的38户对4户，来自同一片混供街区；表里的315对37，覆盖两家公司的全部供水户，而伦敦其余地区的59恰好落在两者之间。接兰贝斯水的人家，死亡率比全城其余地方还低；接南华克和沃克斯霍尔水的人家，高出好几倍。如果是街区或空气在起作用，同一片街区里的两组人家不该分得这么开。这层推理是作者补的；斯诺本人的论证集中在供水与死亡的对应上。

== 并行本身就是证据 ==
一种解释是，这项调查的说服力来自“并行”：两套管道铺在同样的街道上，分组早在两家公司竞争的时候就定下了，没有人为了做实验去改变什么，也就很难说结果是被谁的愿望安排出来的。斯诺本人强调的正是这一点：两组人没有选择，多数也不知情。

另一种做法（作者设想的替代方案，不是史实）：只比较各区的平均死亡率，或者拿富区和穷区对比。那样贫富、住房、职业都会混进来，水的作用就分不清了。斯诺选了更费事的路——挨家查死者住址、查水、必要时装瓶回家化验——换来的是在同一批街道里比较两种水。

还有一点容易被忽略（作者的解读）：斯诺没有等调查全部做完才开口。他在肯宁顿查出头一批数字，就交给了法尔；登记办法随之改变，此后每一例死亡都要记下供水。早期的结果先被用来改进收集证据的办法。

== 今天怎么用 ==
现代虚构场景一：一家连锁药店的区域主管发现，几家门店的退货投诉似乎集中在同一批货上，手里只有几十条记录。这位主管没有急着宣布结论，而是先把记录交给总部的数据组，请他们在所有门店的退货单上加一栏“批次号”。两周后，答案不再取决于一个人能问到多少家店。

现代虚构场景二：一栋公寓楼里，两家网络公司同时布了线，租客签合同时各选了一家，很多人早忘了自己选的是哪家。半年后楼里有人抱怨视频会议老掉线。物业没有挨户发问卷，而是去查每户墙上的接线盒到底接的是哪家的线，再对照报修记录——同楼、同电梯、同一天的雨，剩下的差别就只有线路。

== 这份证据的边界 ==
必须说清楚：本篇所有数字与叙述都出自斯诺本人的书和表格，本篇的证据里没有当时卫生部门或第三方的独立核查。提出学说、做调查、编统计的是同一个人，这不意味着数据有错，但意味着我们读到的是一方的记录。

其次，“两组人没有任何差别”是斯诺的断言，不是随机分组的结果。哪栋房子接哪家水，由当年的房主或住户决定，并非抽签；这种选择会不会和房租、房龄、地段有关，本篇的证据无法回答，这是作者提出的疑问。斯诺自己的调查也显示，按周交租的工人家庭连水费由谁交都不清楚——“选择”往往不在喝水的人手里。

最后，本篇用的是扫描本的OCR文本，书中不少数字在识别时已经错乱，例如兰贝斯迁址的年份被识别成“185S”，化验部分的数值也有乱码。因此本篇只用清楚可辨的数字：表九的户数与死亡数，以及肯宁顿的分户数字；化验部分只写定性的差别。

== 想一想 ==
你负责的流程恰好有两个版本在同一时期并行运行：因为系统迁移的遗留安排，一部分客户走A流程，一部分走B流程，两批人的情况大体相似，但不是随机分配的。现在要定下季度的统一做法，而你手上的数据只跑了几周。

在证据还不完整的时候，你会根据什么改变做法？是等到差异大到无法反驳，还是在差距足够悬殊、且替代解释都能被压住时就行动？你更怕“错在太早”，还是“错在太晚”？

先停十秒，想想你会怎么做。

Two Water Pipes on the Same Street
In 1854 the mains of two south London water companies ran down the same streets, so that next-door neighbours might be drinking entirely different water. John Snow set out to use this experiment nobody had designed to test the doctrine he had been arguing for five years.

== An experiment nobody designed, and somebody recorded ==
In July 1854 cholera came back to London. John Snow had been advocating a doctrine for five years, and this time he wrote down his resolve without ornament. He would "spare no exertion which might be necessary to ascertain the exact effect of the water supply on the progress of the epidemic", in places where "all the circumstances were so happily adapted for the inquiry". And he would do the work himself, because he wanted "the most satisfactory proof of the truth or fallacy of the doctrine which I had been advocating for five years."

What he had in view was a stretch of south London served by two water companies. "The pipes of each Company go down all the streets, and into nearly all the courts and alleys." Which company a house took had been settled by the owner or occupier back "when the Water Companies were in active competition", and, as Snow put it, "In many cases a single house has a supply different from that on either side." Nobody had arranged a comparison, yet one was already laid out in front of him.

== Two companies, two intakes ==
Start with where the water came from. Between 1832 and 1849 London's supply changed a good deal. The Southwark Water Company merged with the South London Water Company to form the Southwark and Vauxhall Company; the works at London Bridge were abolished, and the new company drew from the Thames at Battersea Fields, about half a mile above Vauxhall Bridge. The Lambeth Company went on taking its water opposite Hungerford Market.

London was free of cholera from the latter part of 1849 to August 1853. In that interval the Lambeth Company moved its works from opposite Hungerford Market to Thames Ditton, "thus obtaining a supply of water quite free from the sewage of London." Snow was careful about the date. In a footnote he corrected Dr Baly, who had supposed that the Lambeth Company was already drawing from Thames Ditton in 1849; the move, Snow pointed out, did not happen until 1852. Where the two supplies were mixed, the Southwark and Vauxhall pipes still ran down every street alongside Lambeth's.

To Snow the arrangement could hardly have been better. "Each Company supplies both rich and poor, both large houses and small", he wrote, and since there was "no difference whatever" in the houses, the people or their physical surroundings, no experiment could have been devised that would more thoroughly test the effect of water supply on cholera "than this, which circumstances placed ready made before the observer." It was enormous, too: "No fewer than three hundred thousand people of both Sexes, of every age and occupation, and of every rank and station, from gentlefolks down to the very poor, were divided into two groups without their choice, and, in most cases, without their knowledge" — one group drinking water that carried the sewage of London and whatever had come from cholera patients, the other water free of it.

== House by house ==
He had not seen all this at once. Snow admitted that in the short days at the end of the previous year he "could not spare the time to make the inquiry", and that he had not then been fully aware of how intimately the two supplies were mixed, nor therefore how much the inquiry mattered. To turn the experiment to account, one thing alone was needed: "to learn the supply of water to each individual house where a fatal attack of cholera might occur."

In 1854 he started from a list. He asked the General Register Office for the addresses of people who had died of cholera in the districts where the two supplies were intermingled; some had been printed in the Weekly Returns, and he was allowed to copy the rest. About the middle of August he began with two sub-districts of Lambeth, Kennington first part and Kennington second part. There had been 44 deaths there down to 12 August. Of the houses where they occurred, he found 38 supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company, 4 by the Lambeth Company, and 2 with pump-wells on the premises and no company supply at all.

As soon as he had these particulars he passed them to Dr Farr, who, Snow wrote, "was much struck with the result". At Farr's suggestion the registrars of all the south districts of London were asked to return the water supply of the house in every fatal case. The order took effect only after 26 August, so Snow resolved to carry his own inquiry down to that date, "so that the facts might be ascertained for the whole course of the epidemic." What one man had found on the street became a column in the official returns.

He carried the inquiry over the other intermixed sub-districts of Lambeth, Southwark and Newington, "with a result very similar to that already given". Where a patient had been moved to a workhouse or anywhere else after the attack began, he asked about the water of the house where the person had been living when it started, not the place where they died.

The hard part was classification. Very few households could name their water company on the spot: "Even when the water-rates are paid by the residents, they can seldom remember the name of the Water Company till they have looked for the receipt." For working families who paid weekly rents it was worse. The rates were paid by the landlord or his agent, who often lived at a distance, "and the residents know nothing about the matter." Snow wrote that it would have been almost impossible to finish the inquiry had he not found that a chemical test could tell the two waters apart with certainty.

"The test I employed was founded on the great difference in the quantity of chloride of sodium contained in the two kinds of water" — common salt. Add nitrate of silver and the two waters looked so different that they "could be at once distinguished without any further trouble." When a resident could not say for certain, Snow put some water in a small phial, wrote the address on the cover and examined it at home. He looked at the water itself, especially as it came in, before it reached the butt or cistern; and once he had learned the hours when each company's turncocks visited a street, the time the water arrived told him something too. These signs he used only to corroborate more decisive proof, never on their own.

The first seven weeks of the epidemic went into a single table. Houses supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company: 40,046, with 1,263 deaths from cholera. Lambeth Company: 26,107 houses, 98 deaths. The rest of London: 256,423 houses, 1,422 deaths. Per 10,000 houses that came to 315, 37 and 59. Snow's conclusion: "The mortality in the houses supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company was therefore between eight and nine times as great as in the houses supplied by the Lambeth Company".

The author's reading: the two sets of figures point the same way. Kennington's 38 houses against 4 came from the same intermixed streets; the table's 315 against 37 covers every house each company supplied, with the rest of London's 59 falling between them. Houses on Lambeth water died at a lower rate than the rest of the city, houses on Southwark and Vauxhall water at several times it. If the neighbourhood or the air were doing the killing, two sets of houses in the same streets should not have split so far apart. That step is the author's; Snow's own argument rested on the correspondence between the water and the deaths.

== The parallel run is itself evidence ==
One reading is that the inquiry draws its force from running in parallel. Two sets of pipes lay along the same streets; the split had been fixed back when the companies were competing, and nobody altered anything to make an experiment, so it is hard to say the result was arranged by anyone's wishes. That is the point Snow himself pressed: the two groups were divided "without their choice" and mostly without their knowledge.

Another approach, and this is the author's counterfactual rather than history, would have been to compare average death rates by district, or rich districts against poor ones. Wealth, housing and occupation would then have crowded in, and the effect of the water would have been hard to separate out. Snow took the more laborious road — death addresses, house by house, water carried home in phials — and in return got a comparison of two waters within the same streets.

One more point is easy to miss, and it is the author's reading. Snow did not wait for the inquiry to be finished before speaking. Once he had the first figures from Kennington he passed them to Farr, the registration practice changed, and from then on every death came with its water supply recorded. The early result went first into improving how the evidence was gathered.

== How this works today ==
Fictional modern scene one. A regional manager at a pharmacy chain notices that returns at a few branches seem to cluster on one batch of stock, with only a few dozen records to go on. Instead of announcing a finding, the manager hands the records to head office's data team and asks for a "batch number" field on every return slip in every branch. Two weeks later, the answer no longer depends on how many shops one person can visit.

Fictional modern scene two. Two internet providers cabled an apartment block at the same time, and tenants chose one or the other when they signed their leases; many have long forgotten which. Six months on, residents complain that video calls keep dropping. The property manager sends round no questionnaire. Instead, the manager checks which provider's line each flat's wall box is actually connected to, and lays that against the fault reports. Same building, same lifts, same rain — the only difference left is the wire.

== The edges of this evidence ==
Plainly: every figure and every account in this piece comes from Snow's own book and his own tables, and the evidence used here contains no independent check by a health authority or anyone else at the time. When the same man proposes the doctrine, conducts the inquiry and compiles the statistics, that does not make the data wrong, but it does mean we are reading one side's record.

Second, "no difference whatever" between the groups is Snow's assertion, not the product of random assignment. Which company a house took was decided by the owner or occupier, not drawn by lot; whether that choice tracked rents, the age of the houses or their location is a question the evidence here cannot answer, and it is the author's question, not Snow's. His own inquiry shows that working families on weekly rents did not even know who paid their water rates. The choice was often not in the hands of the people drinking the water.

Finally, this piece works from OCR text of a scanned copy, and many figures in the book came through garbled: the year of the Lambeth move reads "185S", and the numbers in the chemical test are corrupted too. Only clearly legible figures are used here, the house and death counts of Table IX and the Kennington figures; the chemical test is described only qualitatively.

== Think about it ==
You own a process that happens to be running in two versions at once. Because of legacy arrangements from a system migration, some customers go through version A and some through version B. The two groups broadly resemble each other, but the split was not random. Next quarter you must settle on one standard way of working — and your data is only a few weeks old.

When the evidence is still incomplete, what would make you change course? Would you wait until the gap is too large to argue away, or act once the difference is stark enough and the rival explanations can be held down? Which mistake frightens you more — being too early, or being too late?

Pause for ten seconds and decide what you would do.

Changing the process has a cost; so does not changing it.
No perfect experiment is coming; you can only judge on the comparison in front of you.
What you could do: Write down first where the differences between the two groups could come from: besides the process itself, what else could explain the gap in results?
Pick the most dangerous rival explanation and find one piece of evidence that pins it down — a comparison within the same class of customer, the equivalent of Snow's single street.
Compare only data you are confident in; skip suspect breakdowns, and turn the gap into plain language (one runs at several times the rate of the other) before reaching for a complicated model.
Set a line for action: how large the gap must be and how well the rival explanations must be pinned down before you switch. Below the line, keep watching — and write down the date to look again.
Another reasonable choice: The author's other option: keep both versions running for now, treat the split as an ongoing comparison, and decide when the data has run longer. The cost is that one group of customers goes on using the weaker process in the meantime; the benefit is avoiding a wholesale change made on incomplete data. Which way you go depends on which error you mind more.

== Sources and uncertainty ==
Every figure and account comes from John Snow's On the Mode of Communication of Cholera, 2nd edition (1855), read through archive.org's OCR text; the evidence here contains no independent check by a health authority or anyone else at the time. "No difference whatever" between the two groups is Snow's own assertion, and the split was not random. The OCR is garbled in places: only clearly legible figures are used, and the chemical-test values are not cited. Later assessments and the development of bacteriology are not covered.
