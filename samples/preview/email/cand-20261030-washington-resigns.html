鞠躬的人与脱帽的人：1783年的一次权力交还

总司令到国会交还委任状。国会在前一天把谁坐着、谁鞠躬、谁脱帽逐条写进日志——交接的形式本身，就是交接内容的一部分。

一、收场比开战更需要规矩

1783年12月22日，星期一，大陆会议议定了第二天公开接见总司令乔治·华盛顿的次序。一个统帅军队的人要当众把受托的权力交回一个坐着开会的机构；这个场面里谁坐着、谁站着、谁鞠躬、谁脱帽，国会都逐条写进了日志。

二、先写好动作，再等将军上门

事情从12月20日星期六开始。国会读到总司令当天写来的信：他已到达本城，打算请求准许辞去他荣幸地为国会担任的职务，并询问国会希望他以什么方式提出辞呈——书面，还是当面接见。国会当即议决：下周二12点，公开接见总司令。

这时的国会人很少。同一天，有议员动议先问一个问题：七个州出席的国会，是否有权接受总司令的辞职？日志留下了点名表决：名单上列出的议员几乎都投赞成，帕特里奇投了反对，埃勒里则应本人要求把反对改成了赞成。22日的出席记录是马萨诸塞、罗得岛、宾夕法尼亚、特拉华、马里兰、弗吉尼亚和北卡罗来纳七个州，另有新罕布什尔和南卡罗来纳各一名议员。到了23日接见当天上午，国会还议决立即写信给新罕布什尔、康涅狄格、纽约、新泽西、南卡罗来纳和佐治亚六个州的行政长官，催他们的代表马上到会：国会在此开会期间，出席的从未超过七个州，其中多数只有两名代表，而批准正式和约等几件要务都需要至少九个州同意。

议定的接见次序一共三条。第一条：议长与议员就座，并且戴着帽子，秘书立于议长身旁。第二条：将军到达时，由传信人通报秘书，秘书随即引入将军及其随行副官进入会议厅。第三条：将军由秘书引导至座位，落座，两侧各立一名副官，秘书回到自己的位置。坐着的、戴帽的、站着的、入场的，各有各的位置。

后三条写讲话与交接。待旁听者安顿好，必要时由秘书宣布肃静，议长对将军说：“合众国国会已准备好听取您的陈述。”将军起身讲话，然后坐下；议长宣读国会的答词，将军站着听；最后秘书把答词副本交给将军，收下委任状，将军告辞。日志所印的这份议定稿留着改动的痕迹：在讲委任状和讲稿副本交给谁的那一句，正文写的是交给他的副官，再呈给秘书，方括号里另有“交给议长”的字样。编者注说，这份报告是埃尔布里奇·格里的笔迹，方括号里的字出自埃利亚斯·博迪诺之手。

另一条规矩专门管身体动作：将军起身致辞时、退场时，都要向国会鞠躬；国会以脱帽回礼，不鞠躬。按这条规矩，鞠躬的只有将军一人。

一种解释是，国会把这场交接的性质先写进了姿态里：讲话的人起身、鞠躬、陈述、退出，听取的人自始至终坐着，只把帽子摘一下。权力不是在讲话文本里才交还的，在鞠躬与脱帽的不对称里已经交还了一半。这是作者的解释；日志只记规则，不写理由。

三、十二月二十三日：停顿、致辞、交出委任状

第二天，12月23日，星期二，接见按议定次序进行。日志的写法很省：按既定次序，总司令获准入内，举行公开接见。

他落座之后，会场宣布肃静。议长停顿了片刻，才告诉他：合众国国会已准备好听取他的陈述。他随即起身发言。那片刻停顿被写进了官方记录。

讲话开宗明义交代来意：他辞职所等待的那些大事终于发生了；他向国会致贺，并来到国会面前，把所受的托付交还到他们手中，同时请求恩准他退出为国效力的职务。请辞用的是请求允准的说法，而不是通知已经生效。

接着他说，独立与主权已得到确认，合众国有了成为一个受尊重国家的机会，他欣然辞去当初怀着疑虑接受的任命。接受时怀着疑虑，交还时感到满意，这是他自己的说法；说这话时是什么语气，日志无从体现，本篇也不猜。

讲话中段，他感谢天意和同胞的帮助，并特别提到战时在他身边的那些亲信军官：挑选他们组成自己的幕僚，结果再幸运不过；他请国会对其中仍在服役的人给予关照。随后他说，以把国家的利益托付给全能上帝的保护，来结束他公职生涯的最后一幕，是他不可推卸的责任。

结尾把话说完：交给他的工作已经完成，他从此退出伟大的行动舞台；他向这个他长期奉命行事的庄严机构深情道别，在此交出委任状，并辞去公职生涯中的一切职务。

说完，他走上前，把委任状和讲稿副本一并交给议长。委任状是权力的凭证，副本是这番说法的留档，两件东西一次交清。议定稿里这一步写得几经改动，日志记下的结果是：由他本人走上前，交到议长手里。

作者推断，这套流程的要害在于它不依赖任何人相信谁的品格：仪式前一日成文，次日照章执行，凭证当场易手，文字随凭证入档。一个人是否忠诚，一场仪式无法证明；但权力是否已归位，可以在这一天被记成白纸黑字。这是作者对记录方式的解释，日志本身并未陈述其用意。

四、形式在论证什么

把这次交还和一般的口头表态分开看，可以看出三件事同时在进行。其一，次序是事先写定的：22日议定，23日执行，现场没有临时调整的记录。其二，礼节是不对称的：将军起身致辞和退场时鞠躬，国会以脱帽回礼，站着的鞠躬、坐着的回礼，高低位差被明确保留。其三，交出的是实物——委任状，外加讲稿副本。仪式、姿态、凭证三者叠加，才构成这一次有记录的权力交还。

一种解释是，鞠躬与脱帽之间的差额不是羞辱，而是定位：国会要让在场者看见，军队的统帅向这个机构低头，而这个机构不必向统帅低头。议员们戴着帽子坐着这一点尤其醒目——作者的理解是：戴帽落座、让对方站着讲话，像是在现场画出“我们是听取汇报的一方”。这条解释是作者提出的；日志只写了戴帽与就座，没写为什么。

还有一层（作者的解读）：接受这份辞呈的国会，自己都不太确定是否够格——出席的只有七个州，还专门表决了一次七个州是否有权接受；接见当天上午，它还在写信催缺席的州派人来。在这样的时候，一套事先写定、公开执行、当场留档的程序，也许正替一个人手不齐的机构撑住了分量。日志没有把这几件事联系起来，这是作者的连接。

别的做法并非没有。华盛顿自己的信就给了两个选项：书面辞职，或者当面接见；国会选了公开接见。作者还可以设想其他安排（非史实）：将军与议长相向而立、各自致辞，或者只收下委任状、不设讲话。这些做法同样能完成权力归位，甚至更快，但都会少掉一个看得见的高低位差和一段完整记录在案的自述。

五、今天的两种交接

现代场景一：一个开源项目的首席维护者决定退居幕后，把仓库的管理权限交回社区委员会。交接排在一次视频会议里，马上冒出一串问题：谁主持、谁先说话、屏幕共享显示哪一块、权限是当场点“移交”还是散会后各自操作。把这些提前写进议程，交接就不靠任何人的风度；不写，交接就变成谁嗓门大、谁在线谁说了算。

现代场景二：咨询项目结项，顾问把客户在项目期间给过的账号、文档、门禁卡逐项交还。行政人员在签收单上一条条打钩，每件写清名称和日期。这张单子平时没人看，但两年后若要回答“那批权限当时到底收回没有”，能拿出来的东西只有它。凭证加清单，比一场气氛融洽的道别更耐用。

六、这份记录没有告诉我们的

本篇所据只有《大陆会议日志》第25卷的官方记录：12月22日议定的接见次序、23日的接见经过与讲话文本。没有取得任何在场者的私人记述，因此讲话者的神情与语气、在场者的反应，一律付之阙如。日志记动作、记次序、记讲话，不记心情；本篇也不涉及他此后的事。凡是原文没写的动机、念头和“他考虑过的选项”，本篇都不替他补。

另有两处技术性保留。第一，议长的答词没有收进本篇证据包，本篇不予引用。第二，这份材料取自影印本的OCR识别，存在讹字，本篇英文引文只取清晰片段，可疑拼写一律留在引号之外。第三，日志印本用方括号和删改标出议定稿的修改；OCR文本只能看出方括号里的字，看不出删去的是哪些，本篇只转述方括号中的字样。

想一想

公司里一个临时项目组解散，组长要把项目期间被授予的审批权和对外签字权交回原部门。交接要在一次二十分钟的会上完成，双方之后仍要共事。

交接会上，是让交权的人站起来逐项说明、坐着的人确认接收，还是大家围坐闲聊两句、散会后补一封邮件？如果两种都能把事办完，你会选哪一种来留下记录——你希望日后被追问“权限到底收回来没有”时，手里握着的是一张签收单，还是一段谁也说不清的记忆？

一个参考思路

假设：交接双方都要在场，且此后仍需合作 权限收回必须留下可核查的记录 会议只有二十分钟，做不成大仪式

可以怎么做：会前一天把交接次序写成几条发给双方：谁主持、谁先说、交出哪几项 会上由接收方坐着听取说明，交出方逐项宣读要交回的权限与物品 当场在权限清单上签字，副本同时归档，散会后不再以口头方式补充

另一种合理选择：作者提出的另一种做法：不开专门会议，只发一封附清单的交接邮件，双方各自点确认。更快、更不显眼，但可核查的只剩那封邮件，没有一个双方共同在场的确认时刻。

The Man Who Bowed and the Men Who Kept Their Hats On: Handing Back Power, 1783

The commander in chief came before Congress to hand back his commission. The day before, Congress wrote into its journal exactly who would sit, who would bow, and who would lift a hat. The form of the handover was part of what was being handed over.

I. Wrapping Up Takes More Rules Than Waging War

On Monday, 22 December 1783, the Continental Congress settled the order of the next day's public audience with its commander-in-chief, George Washington. A man who had commanded armies was about to hand delegated power back, in public, to a body that did its business sitting down. Who would sit, who would stand, who would bow and who would lift a hat: Congress wrote each of these into its journal.

II. Write the Moves First, Then Wait for the General

It began on Saturday, 20 December. Congress read a letter written that day by the Commander in Chief. He had arrived in the city "with the intention of asking leave to resign the commission he has the honor of holding in their service", and he asked how they wished him to offer his resignation, "whether in writing or at an audience". Congress resolved at once that he be admitted to a public audience on Tuesday next, at twelve o'clock.

Congress was thin at the time. The same day, a motion asked a prior question: "Is it the sense of Congress that seven states in Congress Assembled are competent to receive the resignation of a Commander in Chief?" The journal kept the roll call. Nearly every member listed voted ay; Partridge voted no, and Ellery's no was changed into ay at his own request. On the 22nd the states present were Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia and North Carolina, with one member each from New Hampshire and South Carolina. And on the morning of the audience itself, the 23rd, Congress resolved to write at once to the executives of New Hampshire, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, South Carolina and Georgia, demanding the immediate attendance of their delegates: during the sitting at this place there had never been more than seven states represented, most of them by only two delegates, and the ratification of the definitive treaty, among other matters, required the assent of at least nine states.

The agreed order of the audience ran to three clauses. First, the President and the members were to sit, covered — that is, wearing their hats — with the secretary standing at the President's side. Second, the General's arrival was to be announced to the secretary by a messenger, who would then introduce the General, attended by his aides, into the Hall of Congress. Third, the secretary would conduct the General to a chair and he would be seated, an aide standing on either side of him, while the secretary resumed his own place. Sitters, hat-wearers, standers, entrants: everyone had an assigned position.

The last three clauses dealt with the speech and the handover. After a proper time for the spectators to settle, silence was to be ordered if necessary, and the President was to address the General in set words: "Sir, The United States in Congress assembled are prepared to receive your communications." The General would rise, speak and sit again; the President would deliver the answer of Congress, which the General was to receive standing; and the secretary would hand him a copy of the answer and receive the commission, after which the General would take his leave. The draft as printed carries changes. In the sentence about the commission and the copy of the address, the text has them going to his aide to be presented to the secretary, with "[to the President]" in brackets. The editors note that the report is in the writing of Elbridge Gerry, "the words in brackets being in the writing of Elias Boudinot".

One further rule governed gesture alone: "When the General rises to make his address, and also when he retires, he is to bow to Congress, which they are to return by uncovering without bowing." Under this rule only the General bowed; Congress answered by raising their hats.

One reading of this is that Congress wrote the nature of the transaction into posture before a word was spoken. The speaker rises, bows, gives his account, and withdraws; the listeners remain seated throughout and do no more than lift a hat. Power is not returned only in the text of a speech; half of it is already returned in the asymmetry between the bow and the uncovered head. That is the author's reading. The journal records the rules and gives no reasons.

III. December 23: The Pause, the Address, the Commission

The next day, Tuesday, December 23, the audience proceeded in the agreed order. The journal is spare about it: according to order, the Commander in Chief was admitted to a public audience.

Once he was seated, silence was ordered, and the President let a pause go by before telling him that the United States in Congress assembled were prepared to receive his communications. The journal puts it this way: "the President, after a pause, informed him, that the United States in Congress assembled, were prepared to receive his communications". The pause made it into the record. The President did not rush to fill the silence; the silence itself was entered into the official account alongside the words. Then the General rose and spoke.

The address declared its business in the first line: "The great events on which my resignation depended, having at length taken place" — the events his resignation had been waiting on had finally occurred. He offered Congress his sincere congratulations and presented himself before them "to surrender into their hands the trust committed to me", returning the trust placed in him to their hands, and at the same time "to claim the indulgence of retiring from the service of my country", asking their indulgence to withdraw from the service of his country. Note the register of the request: he asks leave to retire rather than announcing that he has.

He went on: happy in the confirmation of independence and sovereignty, and pleased that the United States now had the opportunity of becoming a respectable nation, he resigned with satisfaction the appointment he had accepted with diffidence — "I resign with satisfaction the appointment I accepted with diffidence". Diffidence at the start, satisfaction at the end: those are his own words. What tone his voice carried is beyond the reach of the journal, and this piece will not guess.

In the middle of the address he gave thanks for Providence and for the help of his countrymen, and he singled out the officers who had served at his side through the war: "It was impossible the choice of confidential officers to compose my family should have been more fortunate." He asked Congress to give its favorable notice to those still in service. Then: "I consider it an indispensable duty to close this last act of my official life by commending the interests of our dearest country to the protection of Almighty God".

The close said the rest: "Having now finished the work assigned me, I retire from the great theatre of action" — the work assigned to him was finished, and he was withdrawing from the great theatre of action. He bid an affectionate farewell to the august body whose orders he had so long followed, "I here offer my commission", and took his leave of every employment of public life.

With that, he stepped forward and placed his commission, together with a copy of his address, in the President's hands. The journal closes the entry in one line: "He then advanced and delivered to the President his commission, with a copy of his address". The commission was the token of authority; the copy was the account of it, kept on file. Two objects, delivered at once. The draft had been revised at exactly this step; what the journal records is that he himself stepped forward and put the commission into the President's hands.

The author's inference is that the point of the procedure was that it depended on nobody's character. The order was written the day before, executed to the letter the next day, the token changed hands on the spot, and the words went into the archive along with it. A man's loyalty cannot be proven by a ceremony. Whether power has been put back where it belongs, however, can be entered in black and white on a particular day. That is an interpretation of how the record was made; the journal itself states no such intention.

IV. What the Form Is Arguing

Set this handover beside an ordinary spoken one and three things are happening at once. First, the sequence was fixed in advance: agreed on the twenty-second, carried out on the twenty-third, with no record of last-minute adjustment. Second, the etiquette was asymmetric: the General was to bow when he rose to speak and when he withdrew, and Congress to answer by uncovering: the standing man bowed, the seated body answered. The vertical distance between them was kept deliberately visible. Third, what changed hands was physical: the commission, plus a copy of the address. Ceremony, posture, and token together make up this documented transfer of power.

On one reading, the gap between bowing and uncovering is not an insult but a placement. Congress wanted those present to see the commander of the army incline himself to this institution, while the institution owed him no inclination in return. The covered, seated members are the striking detail. As the author reads it, men sitting with their hats on while another stands to address them draw a line in the room: we are the ones receiving the report. That explanation is the author's. The journal notes covering and seating, and no more.

There is another layer, and it is the author's reading. The Congress receiving this resignation was not sure it was competent to do so: only seven states were present, and it took a formal vote on whether seven were enough; on the very morning of the audience it was still writing to absent states to send their delegates. At such a moment a procedure written in advance, carried out in public and recorded on the spot may have lent weight to a body short of hands. The journal does not connect these things; the connection is the author's.

Other forms were available. Washington's own letter offered two: a resignation in writing, or an audience. Congress chose the public audience. The author can imagine other arrangements too, and they are not history: the General and the President facing each other and speaking in turn, or Congress simply taking the commission with no speech at all. Each would have returned the authority, perhaps faster, and each would have cost a visible difference in rank and a complete first-person account on the record.

V. Two Handovers Today

A modern scene, first. The lead maintainer of an open-source project decides to step back and hand the repository's administrative rights back to the community council. The handover is scheduled for a video call, and a queue of questions appears at once: who chairs, who speaks first, which window gets shared, and whether the rights are transferred by clicking "transfer" live on the call or by each side poking at settings afterwards. Put those answers into the agenda in advance and the handover depends on nobody's good manners. Leave them out and it becomes a contest of volume and availability.

A modern scene, second. A consulting engagement closes, and the consultant returns, item by item, the accounts, documents, and building passes the client issued over the life of the project. An administrator ticks them off a receipt sheet, each line naming the item and its date. Nobody looks at that sheet on an ordinary day. Two years on, if someone has to answer the question of whether those access rights were in fact recovered, the sheet is the only thing that can be produced. A token plus a list outlasts a warm and cordial goodbye.

VI. What This Record Does Not Tell Us

Everything in this piece rests on one source: the official Journals of the Continental Congress, volume 25 — the order of audience agreed on December 22, the audience of the twenty-third, and the text of the address. No private account by anyone present was consulted, and so the speaker's face and tone, and the reactions in the room, are simply absent. The journal records gestures, sequence, and speech; it does not record feeling. Nor does this piece follow the man beyond that day. Motives, private thoughts, and options he may have considered are never supplied by this writer where the source is silent.

Two technical reservations follow. First, the President's reply is not in this article's evidence pack and is not quoted. Second, the material comes from OCR of a facsimile and contains misreadings, so the English quotations here take only clean stretches, with doubtful spellings left outside the quotation marks. Third, the printed journal marks revisions to the draft protocol with brackets and deletions; the OCR text shows the bracketed words but not which words were struck out, so this piece reports only what is in the brackets.

Think about it

A company dissolves a temporary project team, and the team lead must hand back the approval authority and external signing power granted during the project to the departments that lent it. The handover has to be completed in one twenty-minute meeting, and both sides will keep working together afterwards.

At the handover meeting, do you have the person giving up authority stand and walk through each item while the receiving side sits and confirms, or does everyone chat around the table for two minutes and send a follow-up email afterwards? If both get the job done, which one do you choose for the record it leaves — when someone someday asks whether those permissions were actually recovered, do you want to be holding a signed receipt sheet, or a memory no one can reconstruct?

Pause for ten seconds and decide what you would do.

One possible approach

Assumptions: Both sides will be present and must continue cooperating afterwards The return of the permissions must leave a checkable record The meeting lasts twenty minutes; there is no room for a grand ceremony

What you could do: The day before, write the order of the handover in a few lines and send it to both sides: who chairs, who speaks first, and which items are being surrendered At the meeting, the receiving side sits and listens to the account while the departing side reads out each permission and item being returned Both sides sign the permissions list on the spot, a copy is filed immediately, and nothing is added by word of mouth afterwards

Another reasonable choice: Another approach the author would suggest: hold no dedicated meeting at all, just send a handover email with the list attached and have each side click confirm. Faster and less conspicuous, but the only checkable artifact is that one email, with no moment of joint confirmation in the same room.

Sources and uncertainty

All facts come from the official Journals of the Continental Congress, volume 25 (the order of audience agreed on December 22, 1783; the audience and Washington's address of December 23). No private account by anyone present was available for comparison, so mood and reaction in the room are unknowable; the President's reply is printed in that volume as a draft bearing signs of revision and is not quoted here; the material was captured by OCR from a facsimile and contains misreadings, so quotations take only clean stretches; in the draft protocol the OCR shows only the bracketed insertions, not what was struck out.
