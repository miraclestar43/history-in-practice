走六英里，进一英里

船被浮冰压毁后，沙克尔顿带队在冰上分批搬运、艰难行进，苦干四天之后决定在一块厚浮冰上扎营等待。他自己写下了理由：雪面太软走不远，而附近可能开出水道。

起步

南极的浮冰上，沙克尔顿的船被冰压得扶正无望，只能放弃。人还活着，食品和装备也还在。他写下新的任务，只有一句：「The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition」——任务是让全体队员抵达陆地。

可怎么到陆地？冰面是松软的湿雪，装备要分批来回搬运，一天下来直线只前进约一英里。苦干四天之后的第二天早上，他决定停下来扎营。

原计划与两磅的规矩

原计划要宏大得多：横穿队伍立刻出发，踏上1800英里的行程，希望在五个月内穿越极点，抵达罗斯海一侧的基地。船被压毁之后，任务换成了另一件事——让全队上岸。

船被冰困了281天，随浮冰向西北漂了570多英里。弃船时他算过他们的处境：离保利特岛还有346英里，那是最近一处可能找到食物和住处的地方。岛上有一座1902年瑞典探险队盖的小屋，里面存着阿根廷救援船留下的物资；这批物资他再熟悉不过，正是他当年在伦敦替阿根廷政府采购的。向西最近的冰障约180英里，可到了那里，离保利特岛仍有约360英里，而冰障上没有任何活命的办法。

从抛弃营到海洋营

在被他们称为抛弃营的地方，装备经历了一次大幅筛选。他决定给每人发一整套全新的 Burberry 外衣和内衣，再加新袜子；同时定下规矩：个人物品不得超过两磅，除了最必需的东西，一律不上路。用他的原话，负担不起多余的重量。

于是有了那个读完忘不掉的细节：金币被扔掉，照片被留下；他还从王后赠给船的那本圣经上撕下扉页，那一页上有她亲笔写的字。

真正压人的是船。两条小艇连同装备和底下的雪橇，每条都超过一吨。他写道，雪橇是最薄弱的一环：载着这样的重量走在粗糙的压力冰上，几乎不可能不被压碎，而前面这样的冰面大概还有300英里。

第一天的走法是接力。开路的小队拿着镐和铲，在冰脊间为拉船的雪橇凿出一条路；七组狗队把雪橇往前拉半英里，再折回去拉其余的雪橇；沃斯利管着两条船，十五个人拉，也得分段倒运。他们跨过两道正在张开的冰缝，缝里有虎鲸探出头来。到下午5点，他们向北偏西北走了一英里。

行军的账很难看。一天下来，直线只前进了约一英里；为了绕开障碍，实际走过的距离至少两英里；分批搬运、来回接力，又把当天走过的路程推到了六英里，有几组狗队至少跑了十英里。当晚只能在平坦但又年轻又咸的冰上扎营。夜里虎鲸一直在喷气，凌晨2点，离营地约20英尺处裂开一道缝。他写道，脚下的冰薄得足以让虎鲸撞破，可近处没有别的地方能扎营，只好冒这个险。

第二天早上雪下得极大，几十码外就看不清。他决定不拔营：在破碎的浮冰上很难找到路，把船弄进危险的位置可能酿成大祸。有人折回抛弃营取木头和鲸脂生火；天晴一些后，他和沃斯利往西探路，找到一块大浮冰，至少还能再往西北走一英里。午饭后，他们拔营继续。

下午开路顺利得多。两组狗拉着小艇出发，只需帮一次手；他写道，十四条狗干得和十八个人一样好，甚至更好。十几个人用镐和铲，十分钟就能在一道约14英尺高的冰脊上凿出一条还算平整的路。

可总账没有变。湿雪很深，又要不断停下来凿路，他们向目标走了将近一英里，倒运和绕路又让实际路程接近六英里。看到大家都已筋疲力尽，他下令在两条船的背风处搭帐篷。搭帐篷时，一名水手在一条船里的帆布上发现一小洼雪水，每人只够抿一口；他引了一位队员日记里的话：喝过更干净的水，但很少有来得这么是时候的。

苦干四天之后的第二天，天冷而平静，还是那种湿雪。他看清了两件事：以眼下松软的雪面，加上四天苦干换来的那点成果，走不了多远；同时，附近有可能开出水道，那样就能划船向西北方向去找陆地。两件事合起来指向同一个决定——找一块更结实的浮冰扎营，等条件更有利时再做第二次挣脱冰牢的尝试。

他们把帐篷和全部家当搬到一块又厚又重的老浮冰上，距沉船约一英里半，在那里扎下营，取名海洋营。

光是把两条船挪过去就费尽了力气。雪面糟透了，他们从没见过这样的冰面：人有时陷到齐臀深，到处的雪都有两英尺厚。

扎营后，他决定尽量省下宝贵的雪橇口粮，留给那趟必然要来的小艇航行，平时几乎全靠海豹和企鹅过活。他派人回抛弃营搜罗衣物、烟草之类，可几天大雪，加上融化下陷，留在那里的许多东西已经不见了。

这块浮冰起初约一英里见方，后来越裂越小，成了他们将近两个月的家。两个月里，他们常回到沉船附近，捞回许多衣物、食物，还有少数私人物品。他写道，那些东西当初是怀着轻松的乐观，以为可以一路冲过移动的冰面奔向安全，才打算丢在几英里之外的。

后来他们又换了营地，叫耐心营，在那里住了将近三个半月。

把停止写成有下一步的动作

一种解释是（作者推断），海洋营不是他第一次决定停下。行军第二天早上，雪大得看不清路，他就决定不拔营；午后天晴，探到一块好走的浮冰，又继续走。两次决定看的是同样的东西：眼前这段路找不找得到、值不值得把船带进去冒险。海洋营的不同在于，这一次他把等待写成了一个计划：营地选在更结实的浮冰上，理由里挂着一个具体的指望——附近可能开出水道，那时可以划船去找陆地——等条件更有利，再做第二次尝试。

还有一处值得注意（作者的解读）：沙克尔顿后来用“轻松的乐观”来形容最初那场冲过移动冰面奔向安全的打算。停下之后，他们反而能一趟趟回到沉船边，捞回衣物、食物和当初丢下的东西，又改吃海豹和企鹅，把雪橇口粮留给后面的小艇航行。在这份记述里，停下不只是不再消耗，也是把资源重新收拢的时间。

另一种解释是关于取舍的标准（作者推断）：金币在冰上派不上用场，照片和一页有亲笔字的扉页也换不来什么，但后两者被留下了。他在这几段里没有说明原因；也许那道上限筛的只是重量，也许只是个人偏好，两种说法都超出了他写下的内容。

作者提出的替代选择（他没有写自己考虑过这些）：比如把队伍分成两支，一支维持最低限度的探路，一支守营保存体力；或者先派少数人轻装去试水道。这类做法都要把队伍拆开。列在这里只是想说明，那天的决定不是唯一的可能，却和当时手里的条件对得上。

今天的两个场景

场景一：一个创业团队的融资没谈成，原计划已经不成立。负责人第一次全员会上最有用的，也许就是像那句“任务是让全体队员抵达陆地”一样，用一句话说清新的目标：现在要做到什么，怎么算做到。

场景二：个人创作。连续投稿，退稿的间隙越来越长，你开始怀疑该不该每天硬写凑数。此时可以先做那次两磅的筛选：留下真正必需的（读、记、改），扔掉看起来体面但拖慢你的部分；然后给等待定一个复查日，比如两个月后看一次信号再决定。停下来不等于停笔，只是不再用六份力气换一份进度。

想一想

你带一个小团队做有交付期的项目，投入连续几周增加，关键指标却只挪很小一段；与此同时，一个外部条件（合作方窗口、季节、审批）可能在一两个月内变好。继续加人硬推，还是收拢资源，等窗口成熟再做第二次冲刺？

什么信号出现时，你会把继续推进改成停下来等？你给自己定的复查日是哪一天？

先停十秒，想想你会怎么做。

一个参考思路

假设：每周追加投入，但关键指标每周只前进很小一段；外部条件在未来一到两个月内有可能变好；团队的体力和预算是有限的，且分散使用会让两边都做不成。

可以怎么做：先做一次两磅式的筛选：把资源分成必需与可弃，给每个人的负担设上限 把真实产出分开记：直线进展算一份，绕路和来回搬运的消耗单算 若直线进展持续远低于消耗，收拢队伍到一个稳固的据点，并定下明确的复查日 条件好转时做一次集中的第二次尝试，而不是每天小步硬推

另一种合理选择：作者提出的另一种做法：不整体停下，而是分两支，一支维持最低限度的试推，一支专门盯外部窗口。代价是人力分散，两支都可能不够用。

Six Miles of Walking, One Mile of Progress

After the pack ice crushed their ship, Shackleton's party struggled across the floes, ferrying loads in relays; after four days of hard effort he decided to camp on a thick floe and wait. He wrote down his reasons: the snow was too soft to get far, and leads might open close by.

The Starting Point

On the Antarctic pack ice, Shackleton's ship was crushed by the ice beyond all hope of ever being righted, and the men had to abandon her. They were alive and well, and their stores and equipment were still in hand. He set down the new task in a single sentence: "The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition."

But how to reach land? The surface was loose, wet snow, and loads had to be ferried back and forth in relays; a day's march gained about a mile in a straight line. On the morning after four days of hard effort, he decided to stop and camp.

The Original Plan, and the Two-Pound Rule

The plan he began with was far grander: "the Transcontinental party will set out on their 1800-mile journey at once," he wrote, "in the hope of accomplishing the march across the Pole and reaching the Ross Sea base in five months." Eighteen hundred miles, the Pole, a base on the far side of the continent. Once the ice had crushed Endurance, the task became something else entirely: get the whole party onto land.

The ship had been locked in the ice for 281 days and had drifted more than 570 miles to the north-west. When they left her he worked out where they stood. They were 346 miles from Paulet Island, "the nearest point where there is any possibility of finding food and shelter." A small hut built there by the Swedish expedition in 1902 held stores left by the Argentine relief ship, and he knew those stores well: he had bought them himself in London on behalf of the Argentine Government. The nearest barrier to the west was about 180 miles away, but a party going there would still be about 360 miles from Paulet Island, and "there would be no means of sustaining life on the barrier."

From Dump Camp to Ocean Camp

At a place they named Dump Camp, the equipment went through a severe weeding-out. He decided to issue each man a complete new set of Burberrys — Burberry coats — and underclothing, along with a supply of new socks. At the same time he fixed the rule: "personal gear must not exceed two pounds per man," which meant nothing but bare necessaries would go on the march. In his words, they could not afford to cumber themselves with unnecessary weight.

And so the detail that stays with you: "sovereigns were thrown away and photographs were kept." He tore the fly-leaf out of the Bible the Queen had given to the ship, the page with her own writing on it.

The real weight was the boats. Each of them, with its gear and the sledges beneath it, weighed more than a ton. "The sledges were the point of weakness." It seemed almost hopeless, he wrote, to keep them from smashing under their loads on rough pressure-ice "which stretched ahead of us for probably 300 miles."

The first day's method was the relay. A pioneer party with picks and shovels broke a road through the pressure-ridges for the sledges carrying the boats. The seven dog teams "took their sledges forward for half a mile, then went back for the other sledges." Worsley took charge of the two boats, "with fifteen men hauling, and these also had to be relayed." They crossed two opening cracks with killer whales pushing their snouts through, and by 5 p.m. they had covered a mile, north-north-west.

The arithmetic of the march made ugly reading. They had gained about one mile in a direct line; the detours made the ground actually covered at least two miles, and the relays pushed the day's marching up to six. Some of the dog teams had covered at least ten miles. That night they camped on flat ice that "proved to be young and salty." The killers were blowing all night, and at 2 a.m. a crack opened about 20 feet from the camp. The ice was thin enough for the killers to break through, he wrote, "but there was no other camping-ground within our reach and we had to take the risk."

The next morning "the snow was falling so heavily that we could not see more than a few score yards ahead, and I decided not to strike camp. A path over the shattered floes would be hard to find, and to get the boats into a position of peril might be disastrous." Men went back to Dump Camp for wood and blubber for the fire. When the weather cleared a little, he and Worsley went prospecting to the west and found a large floe that offered a fairly good road for at least another mile to the north-west. After lunch they struck camp.

The afternoon went better. Two dog teams were hitched to the cutter and "started off in splendid style. They needed to be helped only once; indeed fourteen dogs did as well or even better than eighteen men." A dozen men with picks and shovels could cut a road through a ridge about 14 feet high in ten minutes and leave a smooth, or comparatively smooth, path behind them.

The overall account did not change. Through wet, deep snow, with constant halts to cut a road, they "managed to march the best part of a mile towards our goal, though the relays and the deviations again made the actual distance travelled nearer six miles." Seeing that the men were all exhausted, he had the tents pitched in the lee of the two boats. While they were at it, a sailor found a little pool of thawed snow on a sail lying in one of the boats: just a sip each. He quoted what one man wrote in his diary: "One has seen and tasted cleaner, but seldom more opportunely found water."

The next day, after four days of hard effort, broke cold and still, with the same wet snow. In the clearing light he could see the situation plainly. With the surface as loose as it was, and given how little they had to show for four days of strenuous effort, they would never get any great distance. And there was a second possibility to weigh: leads might open close to them, which would let them row north-west to where they might find land. The two considerations pointed one way. He decided to find a more solid floe, camp there, and stay until conditions were more favourable for a second attempt to escape from their icy prison.

Tents and everything else were moved to a thick, heavy old floe about one and a half miles from the wreck, and there they made their camp. They called it Ocean Camp.

Shifting the two boats there took the utmost effort. The surface was terrible, like nothing any of them had seen before: "We were sinking at times up to our hips, and everywhere the snow was two feet deep."

Once in camp, "I decided to conserve our valuable sledging rations, which would be so necessary for the inevitable boat journey, as much as possible, and to subsist almost entirely on seals and penguins." A party went back to Dump Camp for clothing, tobacco and whatever else they could find, but heavy snow and the thaw had swallowed a good many of the things left there.

This floating lump of ice, about a mile square at first and later splitting into smaller and smaller pieces, was their home for nearly two months. In that time they went back again and again to the wreck and retrieved clothing, food "and some few articles of personal value which in our light-hearted optimism we had thought to leave miles behind us on our dash across the moving ice to safety."

Later they moved camp again, to a place they called Patience Camp, and lived there for nearly three and a half months.

Making a Stop That Carries a Next Step

One reading, and it is the author's, is that Ocean Camp was not the first time he chose to stop. On the march's second morning, with snow too thick to see a path, he decided not to strike camp; when it cleared in the afternoon and a good floe turned up, they moved on. Both decisions weighed the same things: could a road be found, and was it worth taking the boats into danger to use it? What set Ocean Camp apart is that this time he wrote the waiting down as a plan. The camp went onto a more solid floe, the reasoning carried a specific hope (leads opening nearby, boats in the water, land to the north-west), and a second attempt was to follow once conditions improved.

One more thing is worth noticing, and again it is the author's reading. Shackleton later called the first plan, the dash across the moving ice to safety, a piece of "light-hearted optimism". Once they stopped, they could go back to the wreck again and again for clothing, food and the things they had left behind, and they switched to seals and penguins so that the sledging rations could be kept for the boat journey ahead. In his account, stopping was not only a halt to the drain; it was time to gather their resources again.

A second reading, and it is the author's, concerns the standard behind the choices. Gold coins were of no use on the ice; the photographs and a page with the Queen's handwriting could not buy anything either, yet those were the things kept. He gives no reason in these passages. Perhaps the limit screened only for weight; perhaps it was personal preference. Both go beyond what he wrote.

Then there are alternatives the author raises for the sake of argument; nothing suggests Shackleton weighed them himself. The party could have been split in two, one half keeping up a minimal reconnaissance while the other held the camp and conserved its strength. Or a few men might have gone ahead lightly loaded, to feel out the leads. Both would have meant splitting the party. They are listed here only to show that the decision of that day was not the only possibility, though it did match the conditions in front of them.

Two Scenarios Today

Scenario one: a start-up's fundraising has fallen through and the original plan no longer stands. At the first all-hands meeting, the most useful thing the founder can do may be what that line did, "The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition": state the new goal in one sentence, what has to be achieved now and how you will know.

Scenario two: creative work of your own. You have been submitting steadily, the gaps between rejections keep stretching, and you have begun to wonder whether forcing out words every day is worth it. This is the moment for the two-pound sort. Keep what is genuinely necessary — reading, notes, revision. Drop what looks respectable but only slows you down. Then give the waiting a review date: in two months, you check the signals and decide again. Stopping does not mean putting down the pen. It means no longer trading six measures of effort for one measure of progress.

Where These Claims Stop

Everything in this piece comes from Shackleton's own narrative, read here through the OCR text of the 1920 printing: the account of a participant who led the party, parts of which read like a contemporary diary. No other member's diary has been brought in for comparison. What the rest of the camp thought, and whether anyone questioned the decision, is not in the evidence, and this piece does not put feelings in their mouths. The line about the sip of water comes from an unnamed member's diary, as quoted by Shackleton.

The text is machine-read, and it carries errors. Quotations are taken only from clean stretches; a few damaged words are reproduced without quotation marks. All the figures — 1,800 miles, 281 days, 346 miles, two pounds, a ton, one mile, six miles, one and a half miles, two months, three and a half months — come from his account and have not been checked against any other record. Nor does this piece tell the story of the rescue that followed; that falls outside the evidence gathered here.

Think about it

You lead a small team on a project with a delivery deadline. Weeks of rising input have moved the key metric by only a little. At the same time, an external condition — a partner's window, a season, an approval — may improve within a month or two. Do you keep adding people and pushing, or gather your resources and wait for the window, then make a second, concentrated push?

What signal would tell you to turn continued effort into a deliberate wait? And what review date do you set for yourself?

Pause for ten seconds and decide what you would do.

One possible approach

Assumptions: Extra effort goes in every week, but the key metric advances only a little each week. The external condition may improve within one to two months. The team's energy and budget are finite, and splitting them would leave both halves unable to finish.

What you could do: Run a two-pound sort first: divide resources into indispensable and disposable, and cap each person's load Record real output separately: direct progress counts as one figure, detours and ferrying are counted as their own cost If direct progress keeps running far below the cost, gather the team onto a firm base and set an explicit review date When conditions improve, make one concentrated second attempt instead of grinding out small steps daily

Another reasonable choice: Another course the author raises: instead of stopping wholesale, split in two — one part keeps up a minimal probe while the other watches the external window. The cost is a divided workforce, and neither half may prove large enough.
