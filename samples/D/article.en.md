# The Grievance Was Real, and So Was the Anonymous Letter: Washington at Newburgh

*March 1783: the war was nearly over and the officers were still unpaid. Anonymous letters called them together to press their case. Washington had not meant to attend; in the end he went.*

2026-11-05 · U.S. History · sample-D rev 1

## Money Owed, Letters Anonymous

The previous December the Continental Army had sent delegates to Congress with an address and petition. A congressional committee later summed it up in five articles: present pay; settlement of arrears with security for what was owed; commuting the promised half pay for life into a lump sum; and settling what was owed for rations and clothing. On 25 January 1783 Congress resolved only that the army would get one month's pay and that accounts would be settled as soon as possible; the half-pay commutation was sent back to committee. Major Samuel Shaw, an officer in the camp, wrote that this was "far from being satisfactory", but the certainty of peace kept the army quiet. [D-E1][D-E12]

Then an anonymous address began to circulate, calling the officers to a meeting. Washington, by an order of 11 March, set the meeting for the 15th instead, which drew a second anonymous address. [D-E2][D-E6]

## Time, Place, People

The place was the Continental Army's camp at Newburgh, New York, with the War of Independence nearly over. The people were the commander-in-chief, George Washington, and officers who had served for years; the meeting of 15 March was chaired by Major General Horatio Gates. The details of the scene come mainly from a letter Shaw wrote in April 1783, printed in his journals in 1847. [D-E3][D-E10][D-E13]

## From an Order to a Pair of Glasses

Shaw recorded how Washington began. He apologised for appearing at all: that had been no part of his intention when he issued the order. But the anonymous papers had been circulated so busily that he felt he had to give the army his view of them, and to do it clearly he had "committed his thoughts to writing", which he asked leave to read. [D-E3]

The address opened bluntly: "By an anonymous summons, an attempt has been made to convene you together." The author of the anonymous address, he said, deserved credit for the goodness of his pen, but should have had the charity not "to mark for suspicion the man who should recommend moderation and longer forbearance". [D-E6][D-E7]

He asked the officers not to take any step which, viewed calmly, "will lessen the dignity, and sully the glory you have hitherto maintained", and to rely on "the plighted faith of your country" and on Congress to settle their accounts fairly before the army was disbanded. [D-E8]

After the address he said he would read a letter from "a worthy member of that body", a congressman who had always been the army's friend, as evidence of Congress's goodwill. After the first paragraph he paused, took out his spectacles and asked their indulgence while he put them on, "observing at the same time, that he had grown gray in their service, and now found himself growing blind". Shaw thought it "superior to the most studied oratory". Note that this is Shaw's report of what Washington said, not his exact words. [D-E4][D-E5]

Washington then left. With Gates presiding, the meeting resolved unanimously that the officers "view with abhorrence, and reject with disdain, the infamous propositions contained in a late anonymous address", and condemned the secret attempt to assemble them as subversive of all discipline. A week later, on 22 March, Congress resolved that officers serving to the end of the war would receive five years' full pay, in money or in securities at six per cent interest, instead of the half pay for life they had been promised. [D-E5][D-E9][D-E10][D-E11]

## Why It Went This Way

One interpretation, the author's, is that Washington separated what the officers wanted from how they were going about it. He did not say their grievance was baseless; he asked them to trust Congress to settle accounts. What he opposed was the anonymous summons and the branding of moderates as suspect. He had not meant to attend; by Shaw's account, he decided to come and read a prepared text because the anonymous papers had spread so widely. [D-E3][D-E7][D-E8]

Why the spectacles moment worked, Shaw put down to its being natural and unaffected. But we have only his report and cannot check Washington's words or tone. The congressional resolution of 22 March followed hard on the meeting; whether one caused the other, the material we have cannot say. [D-E4][D-E5][D-E11]

## How to Use This Today

> A team learns that its year-end bonus will be paid late. The manager can wait for the rumours to arrive first, or speak first: say what is certain (it will be paid, roughly how late, and why), admit what is not (the exact date is not fixed), and make clear that she is in the same position. Bad news delivered first by the person responsible breeds far less suspicion than news pieced together from anonymous channels.

> Or take a community team whose reimbursements have piled up for half a year, with private talk that "the lead isn't doing anything." The lead can share one true fact about their own situation — say, that they have also fronted the costs for months and turned down other work because of it. Not to win sympathy, but so people can see the cost is shared. Then give a concrete deadline and an easier exit than confrontation, for example collecting all the receipts by next Wednesday so the lead can chase the payment in one go. The condition is that this must be true and that real action follows, otherwise it becomes theatre.

## Where Not to Copy This

The spectacles scene rests on Shaw's report alone, and the widely quoted line attributed to Washington is not in the material used here; do not stage it, and do not treat it as an emotional switch that always works. On who wrote the anonymous letters, the sources used here speak only of unknown persons, so this article does not go into it. How the five-year pay securities were later honoured is outside the material used here. Nor should anyone say that Washington alone saved the republic: the resolution was passed unanimously by the officers, Gates chaired the meeting, and Congress acted a week later. [D-E4][D-E9][D-E10][D-E11]

## Think about it

A six-person operations team at a software company was promised project bonuses two years ago that were never paid, and now a pay freeze has been announced. Someone posts on the internal anonymous forum calling on everyone to confront HR directly next Monday, naming and shaming anyone who doesn't take part as "standing with management." You lead the team, and you have a full-team meeting on Wednesday.

**Do you publicly criticize the organizing method of that anonymous post at the meeting? If you do, how do you avoid sounding like you are defending the side that owes the money? If you don't, how do you keep the whole meeting from being carried away by the post's rhythm?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The bonus promise exists in writing; I have no promise of when it will be paid either; two or three people in the team who don't want to stand out have been put on the spot by the post.
- **What you could do**: First lay out the checkable facts: what is owed, how much, and what the company has replied so far. Then say that I haven't been paid either and will keep pushing. Next, separately point out that marking non-participants as suspects is wrong, without going after the author. Finally, give a concrete deadline and an alternative channel — for example, collect the written demands by next Wednesday and I will pass them on — so everyone has an easier option than a collective confrontation.
- **Another reasonable choice**: You could also avoid criticizing the anonymous post by name, simply state the facts and the channel clearly, and talk to the poster one-on-one afterwards. The reason: public criticism tends to harden the opposition, while a private talk is more likely to bring the person back into the process. The cost is that nobody at the meeting knows you care about this, and the rhythm may still be set by the post.

</details>

## Sources and uncertainty

The details of the scene (that Washington had not meant to attend, that he read from a prepared text, the spectacles, the officers' reaction) come from a single letter Shaw wrote in April 1783, printed in his journals in 1847; it is one source, written after the event, and the passage used here does not name the recipient. The speech is taken from the printing in volume 24 of the Journals of the Continental Congress (OCR, with errors) and checked against the Wikisource text; the officers' resolution and the congressional resolution of 22 March come from the same volume. The five articles of the petition come from a later congressional committee report. "The previous December" is taken from context to mean December 1782. The authors of the anonymous letters are not identified in these sources, and the later fate of the pay securities was not researched.
