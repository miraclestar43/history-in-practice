# The Pay Was Really Owed, the Letters Really Anonymous: Where Washington Stood at Newburgh

*In March 1783, with the Revolutionary War nearly over, the officers' back pay was still unpaid. Anonymous letters urged them to turn their petition from persuasion into pressure, and even mapped an exit. Washington had not planned to be there. In the end he was.*

2026-11-05 · U.S. History · sample-D rev 2

## Money Owed, Letters Anonymous

On 21 December 1782 delegates of the Continental Army set out for Congress with a petition about arrears of pay and the half pay for life that had been promised to officers. The difficulty was plain: Congress held the money; the army held a petition, and a force that would sooner or later be disbanded once peace came. [D-E1][D-E11][D-E12]

Congress's answer did not settle much. A later committee report listed the petition's five articles: present pay; a settlement of the arrears with security for what was owed; a commutation of the half-pay for life into an equivalent in gross; settlement of the deficiencies in rations; settlement of the deficiencies in clothing. On 25 January 1783 Congress promised one month's pay and a settlement of accounts as soon as possible, and recommitted the half-pay commutation. Major Samuel Shaw judged the report "though far from being satisfactory"; what kept the army quiet was the certainty that a general peace was at hand. [D-E1][D-E12]

Just then an anonymous letter began circulating through camp, urging the officers to assemble themselves and turn up the pressure. Washington fixed the meeting for 15 March by an order of the 11th — which promptly produced a second anonymous address. [D-E2][D-E6]

## Time, Place, People

The setting is the Continental Army camp at Newburgh, New York, in the last weeks of the war. The principals are the commander-in-chief and a body of officers with years of service behind them; the meeting of 15 March was presided over by Major General Horatio Gates, as president of the assembly. [D-E6][D-E10]

Most of the scene comes from a letter Shaw wrote in April 1783, printed in The Journals of Major Samuel Shaw in 1847. The letter traveled with a bundle of documents: the army's address to Congress, the anonymous letters from Newburgh, the proceedings of the officers' meeting, Washington's address to the army, and the general orders issued on the occasion. The texts of the anonymous addresses and of Washington's speech survive separately as printed in volume 24 of the Journals of the Continental Congress. [D-E13]

## From an Anonymous Letter to a Pair of Spectacles

Shaw records how Washington opened. He apologized for appearing at all: when he published the order that summoned the meeting, that was by no means his intention. But the anonymous pieces had been circulated with such diligence that he felt bound to tell the army his sentiments on their nature and tendency, and he would seize the occasion. To speak with greater clarity he had committed his thoughts to writing, and with his brother officers' indulgence he would read them. [D-E3]

Begin with what the anonymous letter said. It told the officers to reach some final opinion of what they could bear and what they would suffer. If their determination matched their wrongs, then "carry your appeal from the justice to the fears of government." It scorned the past petition: "Change the milk-and-water style of your last memorial; assume a bolder tone — decent, but lively, spirited and determined" — and told them to "suspect the man who would advise to more moderation and longer forbearance." [D-E14]

The letter also mapped an exit for the army. In any political event, it said, the army has its alternative: if peace comes, nothing but death shall part them from their arms; if the war goes on, courting the auspices and "the direction of your illustrious leader," they would retire to some unsettled country and, as it put it, "smile in your turn". [D-E15]

Washington's written address named the occasion in its first sentence: "By an anonymous summons, an attempt has been made to convene you together." The author's pen, he said, deserved much credit; he wished he could say as much for the rectitude of his heart. Men see through different optics and take different means to the same end, and the author "should have had more charity than to mark for suspicion the man who should recommend moderation and longer forbearance, or, in other words, who should not think as he thinks, and act as he advises." [D-E6][D-E7]

Then the rhetorical hammer. What could this writer have in view by recommending such measures? "Can he be a friend to the army? Can he be a friend to this country? Rather is he not an insidious foe ?" He suspected some designing emissary from New York, plotting the ruin of both by sowing discord and separation between the civil and military powers of the continent. [D-E16]

He did not merely say no; he offered another course. He entreated the officers, in the calm light of reason, to take no measure that would "lessen the dignity, and sully the glory you have hitherto maintained." Rely on the plighted faith of your country, he asked, and on the purity of Congress's intentions: before the army dissolved, its accounts would be fairly settled, the resolutions published two days earlier would be followed, and Congress would "render ample justice to you for your faithful and meritorious services." [D-E8][D-E17]

Having finished, he said he would read a letter from a worthy member of Congress — one who on all occasions had proved the army's fast friend — as corroborating evidence of that body's good disposition. It was an exceedingly sensible letter, pointing out Congress's difficulties while insisting the army should at all events be generously dealt with. Then the small scene Shaw cannot omit: after the first paragraph, Washington paused, took out his spectacles, begged his audience's indulgence while he put them on, and observed that he had grown gray in their service and now found himself growing blind. Shaw writes that the appeal was so natural, so unaffected, that it "forced its way to the heart, and you might see sensibility moisten every eye." Note the provenance: this is Shaw's second-hand report, not a verbatim transcript. [D-E4][D-E5]

Washington then took his leave of the assembly. Under Gates, the meeting resolved unanimously that the officers "view with abhorrence, and reject with disdain, the infamous propositions contained in a late anonymous address," and resented "the secret attempts of some unknown persons to collect the officers together, in a manner totally subversive of all discipline and good order." A week later, on 22 March, Congress resolved that officers then in service who continued to the end of the war should receive five years' full pay in money, or securities bearing six per cent interest, in place of the half-pay promised for life. [D-E5][D-E9][D-E10][D-E11]

## Why It Played Out This Way

One explanation — the author's inference — is that Washington split the what from the how. He never said the officers' grievances were baseless; he asked them to trust Congress to settle the accounts. What he opposed was the anonymous summons and the marking of moderates as suspects. He had not intended to attend at all, and in the end went in person and read from a script: on Shaw's account, because the anonymous writings had spread so far. [D-E3][D-E7][D-E8]

The real sting of the anonymous letter may lie not in the demand for money but in the notion it handed the army — that it had its alternative, keeping its arms, withdrawing to unsettled country, and could hold these as chips (author's inference). That is precisely what "Rather is he not an insidious foe ?" targets: once the army begins weighing its own leverage, the line between civil and military authority collapses. Another reading: by aiming at the "unknown persons" behind the letter rather than at any officer in the room, he left everyone a way out. The thing to oppose was the letter, not the claims. [D-E7][D-E9][D-E15][D-E16]

Three uncertainties belong on the record. First, the spectacles scene rests on Shaw's report alone; we cannot check Washington's actual words or tone. Second, Congress's resolution of 22 March followed hard upon the meeting, and the surviving material cannot say whether one caused the other. Third, the texts of the anonymous addresses and of Washington's speech come from an OCR print of volume 24 of the Journals of the Continental Congress, which contains transcription errors; this piece quotes only clean passages. [D-E4][D-E5][D-E11][D-E14]

## How to Use This Today

> A design firm is merging two teams and the rumor mill is running: layoffs, the budget halved, one person already decided for or against. The head can open the weekly meeting by speaking first — state what is certain (the merger is real, the timetable is such-and-such), admit what is not (the list is not set, and their own role is within the pool), and say when the next update comes. Bad news from the leader's own mouth leaves far less room for guesswork than letting people assemble it from the grapevine.

> Or take an open-source maintainer who learns the main sponsor has pulled out, while someone in the community calls for a hold on commits to "force them to show their hand." The maintainer can post the numbers — what is left in the account, how many months it covers, how many hours a month they personally put in — and then offer a cheaper outlet than a freeze: say, a public fundraising plan and an expense sheet within two weeks, so anyone who wants to help has something to do. The condition is that the numbers are true and the accounts actually appear two weeks later; otherwise it is theater.

## What Not to Copy

Do not restage the spectacles. That scene rests on Shaw's second-hand account alone, and the widely quoted direct-speech version of his words about growing gray and blind is not in the material used here; Shaw reports them only in indirect speech: don't perform it, and don't treat it as a universal emotional switch. Who wrote the anonymous letters? The sources say only "unknown persons"; Washington's guess on the spot — an emissary from New York — was a suspicion with no evidence behind it, and it is not pursued here. The OCR text of Journals of the Continental Congress, volume 24, contains errors, so quote only clean passages. How the five years' full pay in securities was eventually redeemed is not covered by the sources drawn on here, so no conclusion. Nor should anyone say that Washington single-handedly saved the republic: the resolution was passed unanimously by the officers present, the meeting was chaired by Gates, and Congress's resolution came a week afterward. The author offers a counterfactual: if Washington had not shown up that day, would the outcome have differed? The material here cannot answer that. Don't fill the gap with imagination. [D-E4][D-E9][D-E10][D-E11][D-E16]

## Think about it

A six-person operations team at a software company has been waiting two years for a project bonus that was promised in writing, and salary increases have just been frozen. Someone posts anonymously on the internal forum calling for the whole team to march into HR on Monday and confront them together, adding that anyone who doesn't show up has "picked management's side." You lead the team, and you have an all-hands meeting on Wednesday.

**Do you publicly criticize the anonymous post's tactics at the meeting? If you do, how do you keep it from sounding like a defense of the side that owes the money? If you don't, how do you keep the meeting from being carried off at the post's pace?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The bonus promise exists in writing; I have no commitment on when it will be paid either; two or three quiet people on the team have been cornered by the post.
- **What you could do**: Lead with checkable facts: what is owed, how much, and what the company has said so far. Then say plainly that I haven't been paid either and will keep pushing. Next, flag separately that marking non-participants as suspects is wrong — without going after the author. Finally, give a concrete deadline and an alternative channel: written demands collected by next Wednesday and forwarded by me, giving everyone an easier outlet than a mass confrontation.
- **Another reasonable choice**: You could also skip criticizing the anonymous post by name, just lay out the facts and the channel, and talk to the poster privately afterward. The reasoning: public criticism hardens the对立 into two camps, while a private conversation is more likely to bring someone back into the process. The cost is that nobody at the meeting knows you care about the tactic, and the pace may still be set by the post.

## Sources and uncertainty

The scene-level details — Washington not intending to attend, reading from a script, the spectacles moment, the audience's reaction — come from a single letter Shaw wrote in April 1783, printed in The Journals of Major Samuel Shaw (1847): one source, reported after the fact, and the recipient is not stated in the passage used here. The texts of the anonymous addresses and of Washington's speech are taken from the printed version in volume 24 of the Journals of the Continental Congress (OCR, with transcription errors) and checked against the Wikisource text; the officers' meeting resolutions and the resolution of 22 March come from the same volume. The line about retiring to unsettled country appears in the OCR text with an error ('cotintry'); here it is rendered by meaning only, and English quotations take only clean fragments. The five articles of the petition come from a later committee report in Congress. 'The previous December' is inferred from context as December 1782. The authorship of the anonymous letters is unknown; Washington's suspicion of an emissary from New York was only his guess at the time, and no conclusion is drawn here. The later redemption of the securities is not covered by the sources used.
