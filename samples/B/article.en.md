# Did Removing the Pump Handle Really Stop the Cholera in Broad Street?

*In 1854 London, the handle of a public pump in Broad Street was taken off on a doctor's advice. The doctor himself warned that cases were already declining before then. The man who closed the gap in the chain of evidence was a clergyman who had set out to disprove the pump theory.*

2026-11-03 · World History · sample-B rev 2

## Ten Days, Five Hundred Dead, One Well

On 31 August 1854 cholera broke out with sudden violence in the Broad Street neighbourhood of London. By John Snow's tally, within two hundred and fifty yards of the point where Cambridge Street joins Broad Street there were upwards of five hundred fatal attacks in ten days, and in less than six days the worst-affected streets had been deserted by more than three-quarters of their inhabitants. The parish inquiry committee, looking back, described those days as a sudden, severe and concentrated outbreak that began on 31 August and lasted through the first few days of September. [B-E1][B-E7][B-E14]

The physician John Snow suspected the public pump on the street corner. He had no pathogen he could put in front of anyone, only lists of the dead and hearsay about what they drank: within the pump's catchment, sixty-one of the deceased were said to have drunk Broad Street pump-water constantly or occasionally; in six cases no information could be obtained, because everyone connected with the dead person had died or moved away; and in six cases the deceased were said not to have drunk the pump-water before falling ill. [B-E2]

## Time, Place, Players

The setting was the parish of St James's, Westminster. The body with authority over the pump was the parish Board of Guardians; Snow laid the circumstances before them on the evening of Thursday, 7 September, and the handle was removed the following day. The parish also appointed a Cholera Inquiry Committee — two churchwardens, clergymen and doctors; the list notes that Snow and the Revd. H. Whitehead were among those added after the committee was first appointed. [B-E3][B-E16]

There was no germ theory as we know it. Snow could only say the water contained "the cholera poison"; what it was, and how it caused illness, nobody could produce in physical form. He even wrote in his report to the committee that it was "not improbable that the water had, from some cause or other, ceased to contain the cholera poison" [B-E8]

The neighbourhood also carried an old debt of its own. During the Great Plague of 1665-66, the Earl of Craven first hired and then bought a field here, on which pesthouses (said to number thirty-six) were built for people struck by the disease, and where a common burial ground was made for thousands who died of it. The place became known as the plague field. In 1851 and again in the winter of 1853-54, deep cuttings for new sewers were driven through parts of this ground, the latter works finishing in February 1854. So one local explanation had to be ruled out: had the digging stirred up ancient plague pits underground? The committee examined the works records and wrote that "no evidence exists of either line having passed directly through an ancient plague-pit"; no serious nuisance occurred when the ground was opened, and no immediate ill consequences followed for the health of surrounding inhabitants. [B-E18][B-E19]

## Start with the People Who Were Fine

Snow's method was to look first at who escaped. The workhouse in Poland Street was more than three-quarters surrounded by houses in which cholera deaths occurred, yet of five hundred and thirty-five inmates only five died of cholera, the other deaths being people admitted after they were already attacked. The workhouse had its own pump-well on the premises, in addition to water from the Grand Junction Waterworks, and the inmates never sent to Broad Street for water. Had mortality matched the streets around it on three sides, upwards of a hundred people would have died. There was a brewery in Broad Street, close by the pump, employing above seventy workmen, and not one of them suffered severely; only two were indisposed, and not seriously, while the disease prevailed. The proprietor, Mr. Huggins, said the men were allowed a certain quantity of malt liquor and he believed they did not drink water at all; he was quite certain they never obtained water from the street pump. The brewery had a deep well of its own, in addition to the New River water. [B-E4][B-E5]

The same street also supplies the counterexample. At the percussion-cap manufactory at 37 Broad Street, where about two hundred workpeople were employed, two tubs stood on the premises always supplied with water from the street pump, for anyone who wished to drink; eighteen of those workers died of cholera at their own homes, sixteen men and two women. Brewery and manufactory were both on Broad Street. One drank malt liquor and deep-well water, the other drank the street pump-water, and the outcomes could hardly have differed more. [B-E17]

Yet the most telling case lay nowhere near Broad Street. A Mrs. E, living at West End, Hampstead, had not been in the neighbourhood of Broad Street for many months; she simply preferred the pump's water, and a cart went out from Broad Street every day carrying a large bottle of it for her. The water was taken out on Thursday, 31 August; she drank of it that evening and again on Friday. She was seized with cholera on Friday evening and died on Saturday. A niece who was visiting drank the water as well, returned to her home in a high and healthy part of Islington, and was attacked and died. Snow noted that at the time there was no cholera at West End, or in the neighbourhood where the niece died. [B-E9]

On the evening of 7 September Snow put these facts to the parish Board of Guardians, and the pump handle came off the next day. But his own figures show the outbreak weakening before then: daily fresh attacks fell from one hundred and forty-three to one hundred and sixteen, then to fifty-four. On 8 September — the day the handle was removed — there were twelve attacks; on the 9th, eleven; on the 10th and 11th, five apiece; on the 12th, only one; and after that never more than four in a single day. On these figures the decline began before the handle came off. [B-E3][B-E6][B-E8]

The Revd. H. Whitehead did not believe it at first. He later wrote that when he first heard of the outbreak being attributed to the pump, he told a medical friend "my belief that a careful investigation would disprove that theory", and he insisted on chasing every puzzling case to the bottom: "It is far too important a subject to be sacrificed to the symmetry of a theory." The turn came while he was reading the Registrar's Returns for an unrelated purpose and his eye fell on an entry: at 40 Broad Street, on 2nd September, a daughter aged five months, exhaustion after an attack of diarrhoea four days previous to death. He knew the case and had recorded the date of death, but had somehow neglected to ask when the illness began — he supposed he had passed over it lightly because it was an infant's case. Nor had it occurred to him that the child might have been ill all week. He went to the house at once and learned from the mother, who occupied the back parlour, that the child was attacked on Monday, 28 August, the dejections ceasing on Wednesday the 30th. Pressed further, she said the dejections were collected in napkins which were immediately steeped in pails, the water from which was poured partly into a sink in the backyard and partly into a cesspool in the front area. [B-E10][B-E11][B-E12]

What mattered was the position: the cesspool sat right beside the pump well. Whitehead reported the facts to the Committee without delay, and they ordered an investigation, carried out by their Secretary and Surveyor, which "clearly established the general fact of percolation of fluid from the cesspool into the well". A man who had set out to disprove the pump theory had found a case that began days before the outbreak, and had prompted the inquiry that traced the route by which filth reached the water. [B-E13]

The Committee also weighed the water hypothesis directly against the atmospheric one. If, it argued, the drinking of Broad Street water by those who died was a mere coincidence — what one would expect, since so many people in the neighbourhood, especially in Broad Street, constantly drank it — then that same coincidence bears far harder on any merely atmospheric theory: of those in Broad Street itself who breathed the air there during the great outbreak, only one in ten died. [B-E20]

In its report, published in 1855, the Committee concluded unanimously that the striking, disproportionate mortality in the "Cholera area" during the days from 31 August onwards "was in some manner attributable to the use of the impure water of the well". As for how the water acted, the Committee expressly declined to favour any hypothesis, leaving the question of mechanism exactly where it stood. [B-E14][B-E15]

## Why It Holds Up

What made this investigation persuasive was not the removal of the pump handle but the way the questions were asked: not merely what the dead had drunk, but what happened to those who did not drink it. The workhouse and the brewery stood close to the well and suffered almost nothing; the percussion-cap works and Mrs. E, one near and one far away, both drank from that well. One reading — the author's inference — is that these exceptions are harder to explain away than dozens of typical cases, such as bad air in the district. [B-E4][B-E5][B-E9][B-E17]

Whitehead's change of mind matters just as much. He expected his investigation to disprove the pump theory, and instead he found a case that began days before the outbreak and prompted the inquiry that showed how filth entered the well. The author's inference: when someone who begins in opposition tests a claim and ends up changing his mind, the result is often more convincing than a chorus of people who already agreed — provided he writes down both his doubts and his checking, so that others can pick them apart. [B-E10][B-E12][B-E13]

The evidence has boundaries. Most of Snow's information on who drank what came second-hand; some cases could not be asked about, and some may have been misremembered. The two deaths involving Mrs. E reached him through her son's account. When the handle was removed the outbreak was already fading, so one cannot say the removal ended it; one possibility is that it stopped people who came later from continuing to drink at that well. Between the infant's soiled napkins and this outbreak, what was found was a percolation route and a match in timing — not a laboratory report. [B-E2][B-E6][B-E8][B-E13]

## How to Use This Today

> Suppose you work at a small delivery-dispatch company, and for three evenings running a large share of orders arrive late. Everyone suspects a release that went out the night before the trouble began. Don't rush to roll it back. Do what Snow did and ask first who was fine: were the two districts that stayed on time running a different dispatch service? Drawing up a list of the unaffected cohorts often finds the boundary faster than staring at the people who were hit.

> At the postmortem, a colleague who wanted to prove the new workflow was not at fault digs through older logs and uncovers the real trigger. Record his investigation exactly as it happened, even if the conclusion is unwelcome; and split the findings into two columns — confirmed and inferred mechanism — so that a tidy narrative cannot blur how hard the evidence actually is.

## Where Not to Copy It

This was an investigation built on observation and enquiry, with no controlled experiment. Public health investigations today have laboratory tests and stricter epidemiological methods, and the method itself need not be imitated. Nor should the episode be told as a lone-hero story: Snow raised the question, the Board of Guardians removed the handle, and the inquiry committee and Whitehead supplied the crucial evidence — while the Committee never concluded how the water caused the disease. [B-E15]

The author offers a counterfactual worth holding on to. Had the sewer cuttings of 1851 or 1853-54 actually passed through an ancient plague pit, the explanation of the day would most likely have slid toward disturbed miasma underground, and the water account would have been harder to sustain. The works records happened to clear this side road for the inquiry — and ruling out a respectable rival explanation is itself part of the evidence. [B-E19]

## Think about it

Fictional setting: a bakery chain sees customer complaints of diarrhoea at three stores over the same weekend, and the company initially suspects a batch of cream. Store A has already stopped using that batch, yet sporadic complaints continue. The quality manager in charge of procurement originally set out to prove the cream was not at fault.

**Before the lab results arrive, what evidence would make you stop using this batch and notify customers — or stay silent? Stopping means destroying stock and conceding a risk; not stopping means people may keep being harmed. Which error do you think is harder to undo?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume the number of complaints is still small and causation is unconfirmed, but the cream batch did reach all three stores; and assume a public notice would trigger a regulatory reporting process.
- **What you could do**: First list who was fine: do stores or product lines that did not use the batch show similar complaints? Check whether onset times fall after the batch was delivered. Ask a colleague who does not want the settled conclusion to review the orders and production records independently. Set a trigger for temporary withdrawal — for example, a second case linked to the batch means stop using it.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another reasonable choice is to withdraw the batch only at store A on a small scale and keep samples for testing, without a public notice: the reasoning is that the evidence is still weak and one wants to avoid wrong attribution and panic; the cost is that if the judgement is wrong, action is delayed.

## Sources and uncertainty

The principal facts and figures come from Snow's 1855 book and from the report he submitted to the inquiry committee. Whitehead's change of mind, the infant at No. 40 and the cesspool investigation come from his own account and the Committee's enquiry in the same committee report. The two rest on different source bases, but are not fully independent: Snow himself sat on the Committee. The neighbourhood's plague-field history and the sewer-works check come from the committee report; the percussion-cap manufactory case comes from Snow's own account. Information about who drank what is mostly hearsay, with cases that could not be asked about; the two deaths involving Mrs. E and her niece reach us through her son's account. Whether removing the handle caused the decline in cases cannot be determined. In 1854 there was no germ theory; "the cholera poison" was Snow's term of the time. No comment from a central health authority on this episode was obtained, so none is attributed.
