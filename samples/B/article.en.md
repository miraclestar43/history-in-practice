# Did Removing the Pump Handle Stop the Broad Street Cholera?

*In 1854 the handle of a public pump on Broad Street, London, was taken off. The doctor behind it pointed out that cases were already falling. The evidence was completed by a clergyman who expected to disprove the pump theory.*

2026-11-03 · World History · sample-B rev 1

## Ten Days, Five Hundred Dead, One Well

From 31 August 1854, cholera broke out around Broad Street in London. By John Snow's count, within 250 yards of the corner of Cambridge Street and Broad Street more than five hundred people died of cholera in ten days. In less than six days, the worst-hit streets were deserted by more than three-quarters of their inhabitants. [B-E1][B-E7][B-E14]

Dr John Snow suspected the public pump on the corner. He had no germ to show anyone, only a list of the dead and what he could learn about what they drank. In the area the pump served, he was told that 61 of the dead had drunk Broad Street pump water, regularly or now and then; in 6 cases nobody connected with the dead could be found; and in 6 he was told they had not drunk it before falling ill. [B-E2]

## Time, Place, People

The place was the parish of St James, Westminster. The body that could act on the pump was the parish Board of Guardians; the parish's Cholera Inquiry Committee later added two members: Snow and the Reverend Henry Whitehead. Germ theory did not yet exist. Snow could only speak of a "cholera poison" in the water; nobody could show what it was or how it worked. [B-E3][B-E8][B-E16]

## First Ask Who Was Fine

Snow also looked at who was spared. The workhouse in Poland Street was more than three-quarters surrounded by houses where people had died of cholera, yet of its 535 inmates only 5 died of it. It had its own pump-well and never sent to Broad Street for water; at the death rate of the surrounding streets, more than a hundred would have died there. The brewery on Broad Street had more than seventy workmen; none fell seriously ill and only two were slightly unwell. The owner said the men had an allowance of malt liquor and he believed they did not drink water at all; the brewery also had a deep well of its own. [B-E4][B-E5]

The most telling case was not on Broad Street at all. Mrs E, who lived at West End, Hampstead, had not been near Broad Street for months, but she liked the water from that pump, and a cart going out from Broad Street each day brought her a large bottle. She drank the water brought on 31 August that evening and again on Friday, fell ill on Friday evening and died on Saturday. Her niece, who was visiting, drank it too; she went home to a high, healthy part of Islington, fell ill and also died. Snow noted that there was no cholera at the time in either place. [B-E9]

On the evening of 7 September Snow laid these facts before the parish Board of Guardians, and the next day the pump handle was removed. Yet his own figures show the outbreak was already weakening: new attacks fell from 143 a day to 116, then to 54, and on 8 September, the day the handle came off, there were 12. Snow wrote that "the daily number of fatal attacks was already much diminished by September the 8th" and that "it is not improbable that the water had, from some cause or other, ceased to contain the cholera poison". [B-E3][B-E6][B-E8]

The Reverend Henry Whitehead started out a sceptic. He later wrote that when he first heard the outbreak blamed on the pump, he told a medical friend that a careful investigation would disprove that theory; and he insisted on chasing every awkward case: "It is far too important a subject to be sacrificed to the symmetry of a theory." The turn came while he was reading the Registrar's returns for another purpose. An entry recorded the death on 2 September of a five-month-old girl at 40 Broad Street, after four days of diarrhoea. He knew the family but had never asked when she fell ill. He went straight to the house. The mother told him the baby had been taken ill on 28 August and that the discharges stopped on 30 August; the water used to soak her napkins had been poured partly into a sink in the back yard and partly into a cesspool in the front area, close to the pump well. He reported this to the committee, which ordered an inquiry. It "clearly established the general fact of percolation of fluid from the cesspool into the well". [B-E10][B-E11][B-E12][B-E13]

In 1855 the committee was "unanimously of opinion" that the sudden, severe and concentrated outbreak in the "Cholera area" from 31 August "was in some manner attributable to the use of the impure water of the well". On how the water acted, it refrained "from expressing an opinion in favour of any hypothesis". [B-E14][B-E15]

## Why It Worked

What made the inquiry persuasive was not the handle but the questions: not only what the dead had drunk, but what happened to those who had not. The workhouse and the brewery stood next to the well and were barely touched; Mrs E and her niece lived far away but drank its water. One interpretation, the author's, is that such exceptions are harder to explain any other way, for example by bad air in the district, than dozens of typical cases. [B-E4][B-E5][B-E9]

Whitehead's change of mind matters just as much. He had expected the inquiry to disprove the pump theory, and instead found the earliest case and a route by which waste reached the well. A conclusion reached by someone who went looking to refute it is often more convincing than agreement among people who were convinced from the start. [B-E10][B-E12][B-E13]

The evidence has limits. Most of what Snow knew about drinking habits came second-hand; some cases could not be traced and some answers may have been wrong. The outbreak was already fading when the handle came off, so it cannot be said that removing it ended the epidemic. One possibility is that it kept later visitors from drinking at the well. [B-E2][B-E6][B-E8]

## How You Can Use It Today

> Suppose you work for a small delivery-scheduling company, and for three evenings in a row a large share of orders has run late, and everyone suspects the release from the night before last. Don't rush to roll it back: do as Snow did and ask first who was fine — did the two districts with no delays run a different scheduling service? Drawing up a list of the groups that were untouched often finds the boundary faster than staring at the group that broke.

> In the review meeting, a colleague who originally wanted to prove it was not the new process dug out earlier logs and found the real trigger. Write up his verification process exactly as it happened, even if the conclusion is unwelcome; and at the same time split the findings into two columns, confirmed and mechanism conjecture, so that a tidy narrative doesn't hide how solid the evidence really is.

## What Not to Copy

This inquiry rested on observation and second-hand reports, with no controlled experiment; public-health investigations today have laboratory tests and stricter epidemiological methods, so the method itself is not a template. And do not tell it as a lone-hero story: Snow posed the question, the Board of Guardians removed the handle, the inquiry committee and Whitehead supplied key evidence, and the committee never settled how the water caused disease. [B-E15]

## Think about it

Fictional situation: three stores of a bakery chain receive customer complaints of diarrhoea over the same weekend, and the company first suspects a batch of cream; store A has already stopped using that batch, yet sporadic complaints continue. The quality manager in charge of purchasing first wants to prove that the cream is not at fault.

**Before the lab results come back, on what evidence would you stop using this batch of cream and notify customers, or stay silent? The cost of stopping is destroying stock and admitting a risk; the cost of not stopping is that more people may be harmed — which mistake do you think is harder to undo?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume the number of cases is still small and causation is unconfirmed, but that batch of cream really does cover all three stores; assume that a public notice would trigger a regulatory reporting process.
- **What you could do**: First list who was fine: are there similar complaints from stores or product lines that did not use this batch; check whether the onset times come after the batch arrived; ask a colleague who does not want the settled conclusion to review the orders and production records independently; set a trigger line for temporary withdrawal, for example stopping the batch as soon as a second case linked to it appears.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another reasonable choice is to withdraw the batch only at store A on a small scale, keep samples for testing, and not make a public notice for now: the reason is that the evidence is still weak and this avoids wrong attribution and panic; the cost is that a wrong call delays action.

</details>

## Sources and uncertainty

Most facts and figures come from Snow's 1855 book and his report to the inquiry committee. Whitehead's change of mind, the infant at No. 40 and the cesspool inquiry come from his own account and the committee's inquiry in the same 1855 report. These are different source bases but not fully independent: Snow himself sat on the committee. Drinking habits were mostly reported second-hand, and some cases could not be traced; the cases of Mrs E and her niece come from her son. Whether removing the handle caused the decline cannot be established. Germ theory did not exist in 1854; "cholera poison" is Snow's own term. This article has not drawn on the views of national health authorities, so it does not describe their position.
