# Just Before Graduation, a Hundred Students Were Called Home

*One hundred and twenty boys were sent to America on a fifteen-year plan. In the ninth year, about a hundred were recalled. What ended it was not one event but a chain of reports, refusals, a memorial and a round of consultations.*

2026-11-04 · Modern China · sample-C rev 2

## The Answer and the Recall

In the late 1870s Yung Wing, the mission's Chinese commissioner, applied to the U.S. State Department to have several of the students — by then advanced enough in their studies — admitted to West Point and the Naval Academy at Annapolis. The reply came in a single sentence: "There is no room provided for Chinese students." Yung Wing later wrote that when he reported the rebuff to Viceroy Li Hongzhang, the Viceroy's answer convinced him at once that the fate of the mission was sealed. [C-E7][C-E16]

In 1881, about a hundred students were summoned home. President Porter of Yale and other American well-wishers wrote jointly to the Zongli Yamen — the Qing office for foreign affairs — to protest that the young men had been taken away precisely when they were "about to reap the most important advantages from their previous studies." [C-E9][C-E10][C-E15]

## Time, Place, People

The scheme began in the first month of the eleventh year of Tongzhi (1872): Zeng Guofan, Governor-General of Liangjiang, and Li Hongzhang, Governor-General of Zhili, memorialized the throne to select boys for study in the United States, with Chen Lanbin and Yung Wing put in charge of the students and resident in America to manage everything. By Yung Wing's account the plan called for 120 boys in all, sent in four installments of thirty a year; candidates had to be between twelve and fifteen; and parents or guardians had to sign a paper agreeing to let their sons go abroad to study for fifteen years. In the late summer of 1872 the first installment of thirty was ready to cross the Pacific. [C-E12][C-E1][C-E2][C-E3]

None of the rules were improvised. Yung Wing records that he went to Nanjing to see Zeng Guofan and settled the essential points face to face: a preparatory school would be established; how many students would be selected and sent abroad; where the money to support them there would come from; and how many years they would be allowed to remain. With those four fixed, the scheme had its skeleton. [C-E18]

## From Recruiting to Recall

The first difficulty was finding anyone to recruit. Yung Wing writes that with no Chinese-language newspapers to carry the news to the public, few applications arrived when the preparatory school opened. All of them came from Cantonese families, and especially from the district of Heang Shan (Xiangshan). Nine-tenths of the 120 government students were southerners, and he puts the skew down to the news failing to travel. [C-E19]

A few months after the school opened, Zeng Guofan died in Nanjing — Yung Wing dates it to "the winter of 1871" — at the age of seventy-one, a blow he records as "an irreparable loss" to China. The date and age are Yung Wing's. The Qing history dates Zeng and Li's joint memorial to the first month of the eleventh year of Tongzhi (early 1872); the two sources do not line up, and this piece does not try to reconcile them. [C-E20]

In New England the disagreements surfaced first over small daily matters. Yung Wing lists what he and Chen Lanbin quarrelled over again and again: the students' school and personal expenses, vacation costs, changes of costume, attendance at family worship, attendance at Sunday school and church services, outdoor exercise and athletic games. He says he "had to stand as a kind of buffer between Chin and the students," defending their reasonable claims. [C-E4]

In the fall of 1876 a new commissioner took office, Woo Tsze Tung. Everything that follows is Yung Wing's retelling: Woo found fault with everything from the day he arrived, and rather than raise his complaints in person, he sent a clandestine stream of letters to Peking alleging that the students had been mismanaged and spoiled by Commissioner Yung; that they imitated American students in athletics, played more than they studied, and formed secret societies, religious and political; that they ignored their teachers and would not heed the new commissioner; that many went to church, attended Sunday school and had become Christians; and that left to their own devices they would soon lose all love of their country and be good for nothing on their return. The sooner the enterprise was broken up and everyone recalled, he argued, the better for China. Yung Wing also notes that Woo had "a decided taste for chemistry" but never made progress in it and "was regarded by all his friends as a crank" — one interested party's hostile characterisation of another. [C-E5][C-E6]

After the military academies' refusal, Woo pressed again — this time, Yung Wing says, with Chen Lanbin's covert cooperation. A censor from the reactionary ranks took advantage of strong anti-Chinese prejudice in America to memorialize the government to dissolve the mission and recall every student. The court first consulted Li Hongzhang, who refused to stand up for the students and consented to the recall; then Chen Lanbin, whose experience abroad was supposed to make him the authority, pronounced that the students had been in the United States long enough; and Woo urged recall without delay and strict supervision afterwards. Yung Wing was ruled out of the consultation altogether. In 1881, about a hundred students went home. [C-E7][C-E8][C-E9][C-E16]

The protest did not spring up by itself. Yung Wing names its organizers: his life-long friend the Rev. J. H. Twichell and the Rev. John W. Lane, through whose persistent efforts Presidents Porter and Seelye, Samuel Clemens — better known as Mark Twain — T. F. Frelinghuysen, John Russell Young and others were enlisted to try to halt the retrogression on the Chinese side. [C-E21]

Yung Wing describes the protest letter to the Zongli Yamen as couched in "the most dignified, frank and manly language of President Porter of Yale." The letter said the young men had been taken away just when they were "about to reap the most important advantages from their previous studies"; that "We have not finished the work which we were expected to perform," yet its results were judged unfavourably before it could possibly be finished; and that if the students had neglected their native language, "we never assumed the duty of instructing them in it." We read this letter today only through the text reprinted in Yung Wing's book. [C-E10][C-E11][C-E15][C-E17]

Keep one specific life in mind: Zhan Tianyou. According to the Record of Students Returned from America, compiled by Tsinghua School in 1917, he first studied at the mission's Shanghai office; in the eleventh year of Tongzhi (1872) he went to America at government expense; in the fourth year of Guangxu (1878) he entered Yale — printed in the original as "耶路大學" — to read civil engineering; in the seventh year of Guangxu (1881) he took a bachelor's degree, was elected to an honour society and won a prize for mathematics, and returned home the same year. He then studied at the naval academy of the Fuzhou shipyard. Only from the fourteenth year of Guangxu (1888) did he serve, in turn, as section engineer, deputy engineer and chief engineer on the Beijing–Zhangjiakou railway and other lines. [C-E13][C-E14]

The road after recall did not run in only one direction. According to the same 1917 record, Tang Shaoyi of Xiangshan, Guangdong, went to America at government expense, entered Columbia University and returned in the seventh year of Guangxu (1881); he went on to serve as assistant in the Korean customs, consul in Korea, intendant of the Tianjin customs circuit, special envoy to Tibet, acting junior vice-president of the Foreign Office, governor of Fengtian and imperial envoy to the United States; in the third year of Xuantong he was acting Minister of Posts and Communications and also plenipotentiary for peace talks with the revolutionary leaders, and in the first year of the Republic he became premier. Liang Dunyan of Shunde, Guangdong, had studied first at the Victoria School in Hong Kong; he went to America at government expense in the eleventh year of Tongzhi, attended primary and secondary school at Hartford, entered Yale in the fourth year of Guangxu (1878) and returned in the seventh (1881). He taught at the Tianjin telegraph school, later served as vice-minister and then minister of the Foreign Office, and in the third year of the Republic (1914) became Minister of Communications. The same book also records that Yale conferred a degree on him in the third year of Xuantong (1911). [C-E22][C-E23]

## Why It Ended This Way

This was not one man's decision. Yung Wing's account traces a chain: the commissioner's reports, the military academies' refusal, the censor's memorial, and the court's round of consultations with three officials. One explanation — the author's inference — is that the decision turned mainly on what kind of people the students had become rather than on what they had learned; Yung Wing, best placed to speak for the students' studies, was shut out of the consultation, and the sources record no consultation of the American teachers. But the text of the memorial that ended the mission could not be obtained for this piece, and what the decision-makers actually weighed is not something the surviving material can prove. [C-E5][C-E8][C-E9]

Then there is timing. The original plan gave the students fifteen years in America; the recall came after about nine years for the earliest batch. Zhan Tianyou just managed to graduate — and seven more years passed before the skills he had learned were put to use. Long-term investments are easiest to cancel in the window when results are nearly in hand but not yet visible. That is the author's observation, not a conclusion the sources draw. [C-E2][C-E13][C-E14]

## How This Can Be Used Today

> Modern fictional scenario: a retail company pilots a new inventory system. The vendor says it will take fourteen months to show results, and at the sixth-month review all anyone can see is "the books are messier than before" plus a handful of complaints about awkward procedures. One thing to do first: dig out the acceptance criteria written down before the pilot and measure against them; invite the store managers who live in the system every day to take part in the evaluation; and write the conclusion so that "transition-period chaos" and "the scheme does not work" are kept apart.

> Modern fictional scenario: a five-year community reading programme was championed by a deputy head teacher, who moved to another post in its second year; the new head's first act was to put it up for review. The move worth borrowing is to write down, at the start, the goals, the term, the mid-point checks and who carries them out, so that a long programme does not rest on one supporter.

## What Not to Copy

The account here of how and why the recall happened rests mainly on Yung Wing's 1909 memoir. He was one of the parties involved and writes of Chen Lanbin and Woo Tsze Tung with open hostility; the text of the Qing memorial ordering the recall could not be obtained. The usual identification of Woo Tsze Tung with Wu Zideng is not directly established by this evidence, so it is listed as background to be checked. The anti-Chinese mood in America is likewise Yung Wing's description, and the text of the Porter protest survives only in the reprint in his book. The details of the mission's start, its recruiting and Zeng Guofan's death also come from Yung Wing's one-sided account; the career records of Tang Shaoyi and Liang Dunyan come from the 1917 Record of Students Returned from America, a single source. So on the question of why they were recalled, what we have is one side's story plus one protest letter — not enough to settle the matter, and certainly not a model of organizational decision-making to imitate. [C-E5][C-E6][C-E8][C-E9]

The alternative the author offers is recorded here only for reference: reviewing the episode today, one might propose sending a small trial group first and setting review checkpoints, as a counterweight to the original plan's single, fifteen-year commitment. This alternative is the author's invention; no one in the historical record discussed anything of the kind.

## Think about it

A company's three-year data-engineering training secondment is two and a half years in. The business-line manager reports that the trainees "don't quite fit our culture" — just as they are about to move onto a core project.

**When you assess a long-term training program midway, and "do they fit the organization's culture" and "when will the professional output land" point to opposite conclusions, what evidence should decide whether to continue, scale back, or stop? And who is qualified to make that call?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The goal is to produce people who can independently take charge of core systems; three years is the agreed term; two and a half years of process data already exist; the cultural judgements are impressions, while the professional output is backed by checkable records.
- **What you could do**: First list the two kinds of measure separately: checkable output (deliverables, assessments, certificates) and subjective impressions ("do they fit in"), and score them apart. Bring the hiring side, the program side and the trainees into the room to state their cases. Then set explicit dates for the payoff and a stop-loss line, and only afterwards decide whether to continue.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another defensible choice is to scale back at once and keep only a few trainees for the final six months: the reasoning is that cultural friction has already damaged team collaboration, and even if professional output is about to land, the organizational cost may be too high. This is the author's alternative.

## Sources and uncertainty

The text of the memorial ordering the recall could not be obtained for this piece. The course and reasons of the recall come mainly from Yung Wing's 1909 memoir; he was a party to the affair and strongly hostile to Chen Lanbin and Woo Tsze Tung, and his retelling of Woo's reports, the censor's motives, and the anti-Chinese mood in America all lack independent corroboration. The text of the Porter protest is known through the reprint in Yung Wing's book; no original has been seen. Woo Tsze Tung is generally taken to be Wu Zideng, but this evidence pack does not directly establish that correspondence and it is listed as unverified. Zeng Guofan's date and age of death are given as Yung Wing gives them ("the winter of 1871," seventy-one); the standard chronology differs, and this piece does not correct it. The details of the mission's launch, its recruiting and the preparatory school likewise come from Yung Wing's one-sided account. Zhan Tianyou's record comes from the 1917 Record of Students Returned from America, a single source; his 1881 Yale bachelor's degree in civil engineering and his return home the same year are that book's entry (the seventh year of Guangxu). The career records of Tang Shaoyi and Liang Dunyan also come from that book, with official titles as printed there. The list of the points Chen Lanbin and Yung Wing quarrelled over comes from Yung Wing alone. Name correspondences: Chen Lanbin is "Chin Lan Pin" in Yung Wing's book; the Qing shi jishi benmo (Complete Accounts of Qing History) misprints his given-name character and renders Yung Wing's name incorrectly; such correspondences are standard identifications, not something this evidence directly supplies. That Samuel Clemens wrote as Mark Twain is background knowledge, not stated in the evidence used here.
