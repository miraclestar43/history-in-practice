# Called Home Before Graduation: China's Students in America, 1872–1881

*A hundred and twenty boys were sent to America for fifteen years of study. In the ninth year, about a hundred were called home. No single event stopped them; a chain of reports, a refusal, a memorial and a consultation did.*

2026-11-04 · Modern China · sample-C rev 1

## The Reply and the Recall

In the late 1870s Yung Wing applied to the State Department for some of the Chinese students, by now far enough along in their studies, to enter the Military Academy at West Point and the Naval Academy at Annapolis. The answer was one line: "There is no room provided for Chinese students." When he reported the rebuff to Li Hongzhang, Yung later wrote, the reply "at once convinced me that the fate of the Mission was sealed". [C-E7][C-E16]

In 1881 about a hundred students were called home. President Porter of Yale and others wrote to the Zongli Yamen, China's foreign office, that the young men had been taken away "just at the time when they were about to reap the most important advantages from their previous studies". [C-E9][C-E10][C-E15]

## Time, Place, People

The programme began in the first month of the eleventh year of Tongzhi (1872), when Zeng Guofan, Viceroy of Liangjiang, and Li Hongzhang, Viceroy of Zhili, asked the throne to send selected boys to study in America, with Chen Lanbin and Yung Wing to manage them and live in the United States. By Yung Wing's account the plan was for 120 boys in four groups of 30, one group a year, aged twelve to fifteen; parents had to sign a paper agreeing to fifteen years of study abroad. In late summer 1872 the first thirty were ready to cross the Pacific. [C-E12][C-E1][C-E2][C-E3]

## Four Stages of the Story

In New England the disagreements began with everyday things. Yung Wing lists the matters he and Chen Lanbin fought over: the students' school and personal expenses, vacation expenses, their change of dress, attending family worship, Sunday school and church, and outdoor exercise and athletic games. Yung says he had to stand as "a kind of buffer" between Chen and the students. [C-E4]

In the autumn of 1876 Woo Tsze Tung became commissioner. What follows is Yung Wing's account. Woo found fault with everything and, instead of raising it with Yung, sent a stream of letters to Peking: the students had been mismanaged and petted by Yung; they imitated American students in athletics and played more than they studied; they formed secret societies, religious and political; they ignored the new commissioner; most went to church and had become Christians; left alone they would lose their love of their country; and the "sooner this educational enterprise was broken up and all the students recalled, the better it would be for China". Yung adds that Woo "was regarded by all his friends as a crank", a hostile description from an interested party. [C-E5][C-E6]

After the academies' refusal, Woo renewed his efforts, this time, Yung says, with Chen Lanbin's secret co-operation. A censor, taking advantage of anti-Chinese feeling in America, asked the throne to break up the Mission and recall all the students. The court asked Li Hongzhang first, and he agreed. Chen Lanbin judged that the students had been in America long enough. Woo said they should be recalled without delay and closely watched after their return. Yung was "ruled out of the consultation altogether". In 1881 about a hundred students returned to China. [C-E8][C-E9]

The students' American teachers and guardians protested. Besides the line about the harvest, they wrote: "We have not finished the work which we were expected to perform." Why should the results "be judged unfavorably before it could possibly be finished"? As for any neglect of Chinese, "we never assumed the duty of instructing them in it". We can read the letter today only because Yung Wing reprinted it in his book. [C-E10][C-E11][C-E15][C-E17]

Keep one person in mind: Zhan Tianyou. According to the 1917 alumni record, he went to America on a government scholarship in 1872, entered Yale in 1878 to study civil engineering, took his bachelor's degree in 1881 with a prize for mathematics, and returned to China that same year. He was first placed at the naval school of the Fuzhou shipyard. Only from 1888 did he hold engineering posts on the railways, eventually as chief engineer on the Peking–Kalgan line and others. [C-E13][C-E14]

## Why It Ended This Way

This was not one person's decision. What Yung records is a chain: the commissioner's reports, the academies' refusal, the censor's memorial, the court consulting three officials. One interpretation, the author's, is that the decision turned mainly on what kind of people the students were becoming, not on what they had learned, and that those best placed to speak to their studies, Yung Wing and the American teachers, were not among those consulted. But this article has not obtained the Qing memorials on the recall, and the material we have cannot show how the decision-makers actually weighed the question. [C-E5][C-E8][C-E9]

The other point is timing. The plan called for fifteen years; when the recall came, the first group had been in America about nine. Zhan Tianyou happened to graduate just in time, and even then his training waited seven more years to be put to use. Long-term investments are most easily stopped when they are close to paying off but have not yet done so; that is the author's observation, not a finding of the sources. [C-E2][C-E13][C-E14]

## How to Use This Today

> A newly formed data team will not show results for nine months, but the company reviews it at three, as usual. The reviewers see "nothing delivered yet" plus a few awkward habits. One thing to do first: at the start, write down when the work should count as paying off and by which measures, and have the people who best understand the work sit in on the review, so that mid-course impressions do not replace the agreed measures.

> Modern fictional scenario: a team is sent out for training, the participants spend their spare time in industry communities, and some people worry their "minds have scattered." One method worth borrowing is to separate "behavior change" from "capability loss": the first is judged by whether it affects the outputs agreed in advance; the second is argued with checkable certificates, projects and exam results. When writing up review conclusions, state which parts are records and which are impressions.

## What Not to Copy

The account of the recall and its reasons comes mainly from Yung Wing's 1909 memoir. He was one of the parties involved and was strongly hostile to Chen Lanbin and Woo Tsze Tung. The Qing court's own memorials on the recall were not obtained for this article. That Woo Tsze Tung was the official known in Chinese as Wu Zideng is generally accepted but is not shown by this evidence, so it is listed as background to be verified. The anti-Chinese atmosphere in America is likewise Yung Wing's description, and the protest letter survives here only as he reprinted it. On why the students were recalled, then, the material offers one side's account and one protest letter. That is not enough for a verdict, and certainly not a model for organisational decisions. [C-E5][C-E6][C-E8][C-E9]

## Think about it

A company's three-year data-engineering overseas training program is two and a half years in. The business line head reports that the participants "don't feel like one of us culturally," while the participants are just about to enter core projects.

**When reviewing a long-term training program mid-way, if "does it fit the organizational culture" and "when will professional output pay off" point to opposite conclusions, on what evidence should we decide to continue, cut back, or stop? Who is qualified to make that judgment?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The program's goal is to develop people who can independently take charge of core systems; three years is the agreed term, and two and a half years of process data already exist. The cultural impressions are mostly impressions; the professional output has checkable records.
- **What you could do**: First list the two kinds of indicators separately: checkable output (deliverables, assessments, certificates) and subjective impressions ("are they like us"), scored separately. Have the hiring side, the implementing side and the participants all state their cases at the meeting. Finally, set a clear payoff point and a stop-loss line before deciding whether to continue.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another reasonable option is to cut back right away and keep only a few people for the last six months: the reason being that the cultural friction has already affected teamwork, and even if the professional output is about to pay off, the organizational cost may be too high. This alternative is proposed by the author.

## Sources and uncertainty

The original text of the recall memorial was not obtained for this piece. The course of and reasons for the recall come mainly from Yung Wing's 1909 memoir; he was one of the parties involved and was strongly hostile to Chen Lanbin and Woo Tsze Tung. The contents of Woo's reports as retold by him, the censor's motives for his memorial, and the anti-Chinese atmosphere in America are all uncorroborated by independent sources. The text of the protest by Porter and others survives only as reprinted in Yung Wing's book; no original has been seen. Woo Tsze Tung is generally taken to be Wu Zideng, but this evidence packet does not directly establish the identification, so it is listed as to be verified. Zhan Tianyou's record comes from the 1917 Record of Students Returned from America, a single source; his 1881 Yale bachelor's degree in civil engineering and his return home the same year are as recorded there (seventh year of Guangxu). The list of disputes between Chen Lanbin and Yung Wing comes from Yung Wing's one-sided account. Name identifications: Chen Lanbin is Yung Wing's "Chin Lan Pin"; the Qing history spells him 陳蘭斌 and Yung Wing 容閑, both slips. These identifications follow standard scholarship and are not stated in the evidence itself.
