# No Investigation, No Right to Speak: What the Line Originally Asked For

*Mao Zedong wrote the line in May 1930. The essay behind it is concrete: how many people an inquiry meeting needs, whom to invite, who keeps the notes. Eleven years later he named his teachers: a poor scholar, a bankrupt merchant and an unemployed tax clerk.*

2026-11-06 · Modern China · sample-E rev 1

## A Blunt Sentence

"If you have not investigated a problem, you are deprived of the right to speak on it." That is the first sentence of the first section of "Oppose Book Worship", which Mao Zedong wrote in May 1930. He expected readers to find it heavy-handed and asked the question for them: isn't that rather barbaric? Not at all, he answered. If you have looked into neither the present nor the history of a problem and do not know what lies beneath it, whatever you say about it is bound to be nonsense. [M-E15][M-E1]

The line is easy to quote on its own. This piece does not follow how it was used in the decades that followed; that would need other sources. It reads two original texts instead: what the 1930 essay actually asked people to do, and how its author later described whom he had learned from.

## Time, Place, People

According to a headnote added later by editors, the essay was written against what was then called "book worship" in the Red Army; the word "dogmatism" was not yet in use. Another editors' note explains the setting: in January 1929 Mao Zedong and Zhu De had led the main force of the Red Fourth Army out of the Jinggang Mountains toward southern Jiangxi and western Fujian, and that is where the army was operating when the essay was written. [M-E8][M-E17]

The text used here is the one published in the Chinese section of the Marxists Internet Archive. Its headnote and notes come from later editors and are not part of the 1930 essay. This piece has not seen an original 1930 printing, so it cannot check whether the text was changed in later editing. Wherever the editors' notes are used below, that is said. [M-E8][M-E9]

## A Short Essay About Method

The essay names its targets: "many inspectors, many guerrilla leaders, many cadres newly taking up their posts" who announce their views the moment they arrive, see a surface or a side issue, and start pointing fingers at what is wrong. That purely subjective talking nonsense, he wrote, was bound to spoil things, lose the support of the people and leave the problem unsolved. [M-E3]

His first remedy was plain: "Use your legs", go round every part of the area you are responsible for, and, like Confucius, ask about everything. Before you set out, your head is empty; when you come back it is loaded with the material you need, and that, he said, is how problems get solved. [M-E4]

From this he drew two conclusions. All conclusions come at the end of an investigation, not at its beginning. And investigation is like the long months of pregnancy, solving the problem like the day of birth: investigation is solving the problem. [M-E2][M-E5]

The most practical part is the last section, on the technique of investigation. First: hold an inquiry meeting and investigate by discussion; relying on one person's account of his experience, or asking a few casual questions without putting the central issue up for debate, leads to mistakes. Second: whom to invite? People who know the social and economic situation well. The old are best, because they understand not only how things are but why; young people with experience of struggle are wanted too; so are workers, peasants, merchants and intellectuals, sometimes soldiers, even vagrants. Third: how many depends on the chairman's ability to run the meeting; a dozen or two dozen will do, and a larger group helps with statistics and with judging, say, whether land should be shared out equally or unequally, though it is harder to keep in order. Either way, "at least three people are needed, otherwise the information will be limited and will not match the real situation." [M-E6]

Fourth: prepare an outline of questions in advance, with headings and sub-headings. His example was trade: cloth, grain, groceries and medicines as sub-headings, with cloth divided again into foreign cloth, homespun and silk. At the meeting the investigator asks along the outline, the others answer aloud, and anything unclear or doubtful is argued out. [M-E18]

The remaining points read like rules for leaders. Everyone in charge, from the chairman of a township government to the chairman of the central government, from a battalion commander to the commander-in-chief, from a branch secretary to the general secretary, must "personally undertake investigation of actual social and economic conditions, and cannot rely on written reports alone, because the two are different things." Someone new to the work should get to the bottom of one place or one problem first; after that, other places come more easily. And the last point: chair the meeting yourself and keep the record yourself. Leaving it to someone else will not do. [M-E7]

Eleven years later, in March 1941, he wrote a preface for a collection called Rural Surveys and said whom he had learned from. For his inquiry in Xunwu he had brought in some middle-ranking and junior cadres, a poor scholar, a bankrupt former head of the chamber of commerce, and a former clerk who had handled the county magistrate's grain and tax money and was out of work. What first taught him how thoroughly corrupt China's prisons were was a minor jailer he met while investigating in Hengshan County, Hunan. The inquiry meeting, he wrote, was the simplest and most reliable method there was, a better school than any university. [M-E16][M-E12][M-E13][M-E11]

The preface is blunter about attitude. First, "look down, and do not just gaze up at the sky"; without the interest and the determination to look down, he wrote, you will never really understand China's affairs in your whole life. He called the scholar, the jailer, the merchant and the tax clerk his respected teachers, and said that being their pupil required diligence and respect; otherwise they would ignore him, keep back what they knew, or tell only part of it. [M-E19][M-E20]

On the meetings themselves, the 1941 advice is not quite the same as in 1930. Now he wrote that a meeting need not be large: three to five, or seven or eight people, are enough. He still insisted on allowing time, preparing an outline, asking the questions and writing the answers yourself, and discussing with those present. [M-E21]

The same preface contains a sentence that is easy to skip: the material he had gathered through inquiry meetings in Hunan and in the Jinggang Mountains had all been lost. What the book printed was mainly three surveys, of Xingguo, Changgang township and Caixi township. [M-E10]

He also admitted that the line had drawn criticism: it had been mocked as "narrow empiricism", he wrote, but he did not regret it. His target was another kind of person, the one who starts holding forth and handing out opinions the moment he steps off the cart, criticising this and blaming that; the Party, he said, had suffered no end from such "imperial envoys". And he stressed that the book was printed to show a method for finding out how things stood at the bottom, not so that comrades would memorise its particular findings and conclusions. [M-E22][M-E23][M-E24]

One of the editors' notes records an addition. On 2 April 1931, in a General Political Department notice on investigating population and land, Mao expanded the line into two: first, no investigation, no right to speak; second, no correct investigation, no right to speak either. [M-E14]

## Why It Matters

One reading, and it is the author's inference: the weight of the essay is not in the slogan but in turning "finding out" into a set of steps that can be checked. Hold a meeting rather than hearing one person. Have at least three people. Mix ages and occupations. Prepare the questions. Turn up and take the notes yourself. The 1931 addition amounts to admitting that the first sentence was not enough, because an investigation can itself be done badly, and the shift from "a dozen or two dozen" in 1930 to "three to eight" in 1941 shows the practical advice changing too. The texts do not say why. [M-E6][M-E7][M-E14][M-E18][M-E21]

The other point is whom he asked. The teachers he named, a poor scholar, a bankrupt merchant, an unemployed tax clerk and a minor jailer, were not people with power; they were people who knew how one corner of things actually worked. The author's inference is that the list is part of the method: the people who know most are not always the ones at the head of the table. [M-E12][M-E13]

Two kinds of material need to be kept apart. One is Mao's own writing. The other is the later editors' commentary, such as the note saying that under Mao's leadership the Red Fourth Army's investigative work gradually got under way. That is the editors' summary of the period, and this piece has no independent source to check how far such work actually went. [M-E9]

## Using It Today

> A newly appointed store manager wants to rework the shift rota in her first week. She could start with "use your legs": spend a day on the early shift, the late shift and a weekend shift, then sit down once with three to five staff of different seniority, and write down "what I assumed" and "what they said" in two columns, taking the notes herself.

> A product team judges customer satisfaction from monthly dashboards. The dashboards look fine, but complaints keep rising. Once a quarter they could hold a small inquiry meeting: a few front-line support staff, a long-standing customer and one who has just left, around one concrete question. A report and what you see with your own eyes are sometimes two different things.

## Where Not to Copy

Inquiry meetings have limits. Who chooses the participants, who dares to speak frankly and how the chairman asks questions all shape the result. The essay's rule of at least three people of mixed ages and occupations is meant to reduce that bias, but reducing is not removing. Today there are also sampling, anonymous surveys and data checks; meetings need not carry the whole load. [M-E6]

This piece is about these two texts and nothing more. Mao's record as a whole, and how the line was used in later decades, need other sources, and no conclusion is drawn here. Nor should a short essay from 1930 be taken as proof of everything done later in its name.

## Think about it

You have just taken over a regional business. On your desk is a detailed report from your predecessor concluding that customers mainly find the price too high. You have two weeks to propose a plan.

**Do you act on the report, or spend a week asking for yourself first? If you ask, whom do you ask, and how many people? What would you need to find to overturn the report?**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: The report is six months old; your predecessor has moved on but can be reached; a plan is due in two weeks.
- **What you could do**: Spend a day on the report and list its three key conclusions and the evidence for each. Then spend three or four days talking to long-standing customers, customers who have just left, front-line sales staff and someone in finance, and at least once bring several of them together to discuss it. Take the notes yourself, sorting what agrees with the report from what does not. Then decide whether to keep, revise or overturn it.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another reasonable choice is to run a small pilot based on the report while the inquiry goes on. When time is short and the report is not baseless, doing both at once is defensible; the cost is that if the report is wrong, part of the pilot is wasted.
