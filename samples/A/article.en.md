# Chen Ping Defects, Fan Zeng Walks Away: Two Men Xiang Yu Could Not Keep

*In the third year of Han, Xingyang was under siege, and the King of Han spent forty thousand catties of gold on one plan to split the enemy apart. In the same war, two men left Xiang Yu's side, one after the other, for very different reasons.*

2026-11-02 · Ancient China · sample-A rev 1

## A Fine Spread, and a Crack

In the third year of Han, Xiang Yu cut the Han army's supply road several times. Trapped in Xingyang and out of food, the King of Han sent an offer to make peace, on condition that everything west of Xingyang be given to Han. Xiang Yu was ready to accept, but Fan Zeng of Liyang stopped him: "The Han army is easy to deal with; if you let them go now and do not take them, you will regret it later." The Chu forces then pressed the siege of Xingyang hard. [A-E7]

As the Records tell it, this time the King of Han did not try to fight his way out. He looked for a way to make the Chu camp turn on itself, and the plan came from Chen Ping, who had earlier crossed over from Xiang Yu's side. Xiang Yu lost two men in turn: first Chen Ping, who went over to Han, and later Fan Zeng, his "Second Father", who asked to go home. Both men used the same old phrase for resigning, asking for their "bones", but they left for different reasons. [A-E7][A-E1][A-E6]

## Time, Place, People

The time is the third year of Han (usually dated to 204 BC); the place is Xingyang, where the Han army was besieged and its supply road cut. Around Xiang Yu stood Fan Zeng, Lord of Liyang, whom he honoured as "Second Father", and generals such as Zhongli Mo, Long Ju and Zhou Yin. By Chen Ping's count, men of that calibre "were no more than a few". On the Han side, Chen Ping had defected from Chu some time earlier and now served as Commandant Protector of the Army, overseeing the generals. [A-E7][A-E3][A-E9]

## Three Turns

Chen Ping nearly died on his way to Han. Crossing a river alone, he looked to the boatman like a runaway officer with gold and jade hidden in his belt, and the man eyed him with murder in mind. Chen Ping took off his clothes and helped pole the boat. Once the boatman saw he had nothing on him, he left him alone. [A-E11]

In the Han camp the danger took another form. The Marquis of Jiang, Guan Ying and others denounced him to the King of Han: handsome, like the jade on a cap, but perhaps hollow inside; at home he had had an affair with his sister-in-law; he had failed to settle with Wei or with Chu; and now, in charge of supervising the army, he took money from the generals, so that those who paid more got good posts and those who paid less got bad ones. The King of Han summoned him to explain. Chen Ping's answer was blunt. The King of Wei would not use his advice, so he went to Xiang Yu. Xiang Yu "cannot trust people; those he relies on and loves are all Xiangs or his wife's brothers", so even a man of rare talent was wasted, and he left. As for the money: "I came here naked. Without taking gold I had nothing to work with." If his plans were useless, he said, the gold was still there to be sealed and handed in, and he could ask for his bones, that is, be allowed to go. The King of Han apologised, rewarded him richly and made him Commandant Protector over all the generals. The generals said no more. [A-E8][A-E1][A-E9]

Looking at the Chu camp, Chen Ping saw a crack: Xiang Yu was "jealous and suspicious, and believed slander", and the men who truly held his side together were few. He asked for tens of thousands of catties of gold to sow discord. The King of Han handed over forty thousand catties, "to use as he pleased, without asking where it went". The money went into the Chu army, and so did a rumour: Zhongli Mo and others had won great merit for Xiang Yu "yet would never be given land to rule as kings", so they meant to join Han, destroy the Xiang clan and divide its lands. Xiang Yu did indeed begin to doubt Zhongli Mo and the others. [A-E3][A-E4][A-E5]

Then came the meal. When Xiang Yu sent an envoy to the Han camp, the King of Han had a full feast of beef, mutton and pork brought in. Then, on seeing the man, he pretended to be startled: "I thought you were the Second Father's envoy, but you are Xiang Yu's!" The fine dishes were carried out and coarse food served instead. The envoy reported all of it, and Xiang Yu came to suspect the Second Father deeply. Fan Zeng urged a quick assault on Xingyang; Xiang Yu would not listen. Hearing that he was suspected, Fan Zeng said in anger: "The affairs of the realm are largely settled. Let Your Majesty manage them yourself! I ask for my bones, to go home!" The Basic Annals of Xiang Yu record that Xiang Yu "granted it". Before Fan Zeng reached Pengcheng, an abscess broke out on his back and he died. [A-E6][A-E7]

## Why It Happened

Both departures turn on one word: trust. Chen Ping said Xiang Yu's trust went only to his own clan and his wife's family; Fan Zeng was a senior adviser, yet once suspected, even his advice to storm Xingyang was ignored. Chen Ping had also given a finer reading of Xiang Yu: he was "respectful and kind to people", so "men of integrity who valued courtesy flocked to him"; but "when it came to granting ranks and fiefs for merit, he held on to them", so others "for this reason did not stay attached". One explanation, and it is the author's inference, is that the plot worked because the rumour named a real grievance: great merit, no land. Why Xiang Yu believed it so readily, the histories do not say. [A-E1][A-E2][A-E5][A-E6]

One more thing worth noticing, and this is the author's observation: both men used the phrase "ask for my bones". Chen Ping said it to put the decision in the king's hands, and he was kept and promoted. Fan Zeng said it in anger after he had been suspected, and Xiang Yu let him go. The same words, once to earn trust and once after trust was gone. As for the scene of the feast and the coarse food, it reads like theatre; it may have happened as told or been polished in the telling, and the surviving material cannot settle which. [A-E1][A-E6]

## How to Use This Today

> Word goes round a small team that a key member has an offer elsewhere and is about to leave. The manager's first impulse is to find out who started the talk. A more useful first question may be: if it were true, why would she want to go? A rumour spreads when it names something everyone already knows but nobody has raised, such as a promotion that keeps getting postponed. Deal with that, and the rumour loses most of its power.

> A colleague who moved from contractor to permanent staff takes over purchasing, and older hands mutter privately that he will favour suppliers he knows. He does not swear oaths; he just posts the three-way price comparison into the group chat and gives a reason for every price difference, open for anyone to check. For an outsider to dissolve suspicion, the cheapest move is not to make statements but to lay the process open for people to see.

## Where Not to Copy

The King of Han handing over forty thousand catties of gold without asking where it went would be an internal-control failure today, not magnanimity. What Chen Ping bought with it was the enemy's suspicion, not cooperation on his own side; this is not a management technique. In practice all of this rests on one historical source, and the feast scene is especially dramatic. Finally, Fan Zeng asking to leave is how the histories record the ending. Staying to talk it through, or starting over elsewhere, are possibilities the author raises, not choices Fan Zeng is recorded as weighing. [A-E4][A-E6]

## Think about it

Fictional: a six-person back-of-house team in a coffee shop splits the year-end profit share, and the shop manager decides it alone, on a scheme ranked by length of service. A roaster who joined from another shop improved the blend and gets the best feedback, yet receives the least; there is already talk in the shop that the manager only looks after the old hands.

**Should the roaster stay and get the rules of the split talked through, or look for another shop? Staying means being the one who picks at things, which may change nothing and may get him labelled; leaving is clean and quick, but it amounts to accepting the system, and gives up the feel for the work and the regular customers he has built up.**

<details><summary>One possible approach</summary>

- **Assumptions**: Assume he has hard data (the past two months of everyone's shifts, output and complaint records), and that the manager is not acting in bad faith, only out of inertia and a dislike of trouble; assume there are two or three other shops nearby he could go to.
- **What you could do**: First turn the shift and output records into one table, listing facts only; ask the manager for a one-to-one talk, about the rules and the measure, not about people; offer a transparent formula for the next split and a transition plan; only if the manager will not even look at the data, talk about staying or going.
- **Another reasonable choice**: Another reasonable choice is simply to leave, for a shop where the split follows written rules. The reason is that once trust collapses, the cost of rebuilding it falls entirely on the individual, and leaving is itself a signal to the shop and to the trade. Both are reasonable; it depends on how he judges whether this shop can still change.

## Sources and uncertainty

All the material comes from Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, in the Hereditary House of Chancellor Chen and the Basic Annals of Xiang Yu, plus Ban Gu's Book of Han, which rewrites the Records (A-E10 shares a source with A-E4); there is no genuinely independent second source. The "taolao feast — coarse fare" scene is highly dramatic and cannot be checked. "Fan Zeng left because of the deception" is the causal account given by the Records (suspicion -> his advice not followed -> asks to leave -> dies of illness on the road), not independent evidence. Fan Zeng's words on leaving differ between the two chapters ("I beg leave to resign and return home" in the Hereditary House of Chancellor Chen; "I beg leave to resign and return to the ranks" in the Basic Annals of Xiang Yu). Chen Ping's stated reasons for leaving Wei and Chu come from his own account to the King of Han and may contain self-justification (the author's inference). "An affair with his sister-in-law" and "taking the generals' money" are only accusations by generals in the Han camp, with no independent verification. The inner thoughts of Xiang Yu and Fan Zeng are not recorded in the source texts and are not written into this article. The conversion of 'the third year of Han' to 204 BC follows the standard chronology and is not taken from the evidence above.
