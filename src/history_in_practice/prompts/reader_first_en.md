[Make it clear: reader-first rules (owner's reader feedback, 2026-10-31). The Chinese article was written to the same rules; the English must meet them too.]
First ask: can a reader with no background tell, at every point, who is doing what, and why a given line or action changes the situation?

1. People and titles. When a person first appears, give only the role and relationship the reader needs at that moment (e.g. "Gongshu, Wei's chief minister, whose wife was a Wei princess"). Introduce people as the story needs them; the dek and the opening must not list many names or give away the whole outcome.
2. Same title, different people. When two people share a title (two princesses, successive rulers, successive chief ministers, two men both called "the prince"), give each a stable, distinguishable English label and keep it throughout ("Gongshu's wife" vs "the princess Marquis Wu offered to Wu Qi"; "Marquis Wen" vs "his son, Marquis Wu"). Never leave "the princess" or "the ruler" to the reader's guess.
3. Pronouns and referents. In adjacent sentences, he / she / they / the ruler / the minister must point to one obvious person. If two people could be meant, use the name or role. Do not copy an ambiguous Chinese referent into English; resolve it from what the Chinese and the evidence already say, without adding facts.
4. Turning points. For each key turn the reader must see who did what, who saw or heard it, and why another person read the action differently. Use only what the source records (words, actions, and the source's own explanations). Do not add thoughts or feelings the Chinese does not state.
5. Classical lines. Render a quoted classical line as a faithful, readable translation and keep, in the same paragraph, the plain explanation of what it means and what it does in the exchange (answer, challenge, test, concession).
6. Record, judgment, inference. Keep the Chinese article's distinction between what the source records, whose judgment a characterization is ("Li Ke described him as greedy and fond of women"), and the author's inference ("One reading is..."), in natural sentences, not bracketed labels. No notes about the review process in the body; limits stay at the end.
7. Dek and opening. The dek raises the situation or question in one or two sentences, with no ending and no cast list. The opening unfolds a concrete situation and must not repeat the dek's plot.
