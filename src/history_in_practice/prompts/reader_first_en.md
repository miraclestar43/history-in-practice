[Column direction and reader-first rules (editor-in-chief, revised 2026-11-01). The Chinese article was written to the same rules; the English must meet them too. Where older instructions conflict, these win.]
The column exists so readers enjoy a good story, understand the people's situations and choices, and sharpen their own judgment. Completeness of historical scholarship is not the measure of an article.

1. Material and honesty. The material may be a history, a scholarly study, popular history, a biography, a memoir, a business story, a novel or a fable. Be faithful to it; do not claim more than it says. Say naturally, once, what kind of material it is ("as Kuhn tells it in Soulstealers", "in this fable", "Knight recalls"). Never present a novelised scene or an author's guess as established fact, and never invent dialogue, thoughts or details and pass them off as the book's.
2. People and titles. Make clear who is who, how they are related, what each wants, why they act, and what the choice led to. When a person first appears, give only the role and relationship the reader needs at that moment. The dek and the opening must not list many names or give away the outcome.
3. Same title, different people. When two people share a title (two princesses, successive rulers, successive chief ministers), give each a stable, distinguishable English label and keep it throughout. Never leave "the princess" or "the ruler" to the reader's guess.
4. Pronouns and referents. In adjacent sentences, he / she / they / the ruler must point to one obvious person. Resolve an ambiguous Chinese referent from what the article and the evidence already say, without adding facts.
5. Turning points. For each key turn the reader must see who did what, who saw or heard it, and why another person read it differently, using only what the material records.
6. Classical lines. Render a quoted classical line as a faithful, readable translation and explain, in the same paragraph, what it means and what it does in the exchange.
7. Attribution in the sentence, no process notes. Attribute judgments and inferences in natural sentences ("Kuhn argues", "one possibility is"), not bracketed labels, and do not repeat "the author infers" throughout. No notes about the review process in the body; material notes go briefly at the end.
8. Dek and opening. The dek raises the situation or question in one or two sentences, with no ending and no cast list. The opening unfolds a concrete situation and does not repeat the dek's plot.
9. The takeaway. One short modern scenario, in the closing question only. Make it concrete and bounded (when it applies, where it does not); no success-manual morals, and not the same workplace template every time.
10. What must be fixed: wrong relationships or identities, wrong key causes, distorted or fake quotations, seriously misleading claims, invented facts. Minor wording, side dates, and disputes that exist in the material itself are notes, not reasons to rewrite.
